Module 01

Introduction to Psychology & Research Methods

Foundations

1. What is Psychology? Definition, Etymology, and Modern Scope

Etymology: The word Psychology originates from two Greek roots: Psyche (meaning soul, mind, or spirit) and Logos (meaning study, discourse, or scientific investigation). Historically, it was defined as the "study of the mind or soul."

Formal Definition: Modern Psychology is defined as the scientific study of behavior and mental processes in both humans and animals.

  • Behavior: Any observable, measurable outward action or response exhibited by an organism (e.g., speech, facial expressions, movement, physiological reaction).
  • Mental Processes: Internal, covert cognitive activities and subjective experiences that cannot be directly observed but can be inferred through scientific measurement (e.g., thinking, memory, emotion, perception, problem-solving).

Modern Scope: Psychology spans a continuum from biological neuroscience (molecular brain chemistry) to macroscopic socio-cultural interactions (group dynamics, cultural norms).

2. Why is Psychology called a Biopsychosocial / Biosocial Science?

[4 Marks Target]

Psychology is classified as a Biopsychosocial (or Biosocial) science because human behavior cannot be understood in isolation through a single perspective. It sits at the intersection of biological natural sciences and social sciences, combining three interconnected domains:

  1. Biological Factors: Genetics, neurotransmitter systems, brain anatomy, hormonal activity, and evolutionary mechanisms.
  2. Psychological Factors: Cognition, emotional regulation, personality traits, perceptual filters, and learned behavioral responses.
  3. Social/Socio-cultural Factors: Environmental influences, family structure, cultural values, peer dynamics, and socio-economic context.

Examiner Summary: It bridges the physical mechanics of the central nervous system with the social realities of human society.

3. Why Does Psychology Study Animal Behavior?

Comparative psychology studies non-human animals for several critical scientific reasons:

1. Evolutionary Continuity

Based on Darwinian evolutionary theory, biological and behavioral mechanisms in animals share homology with human systems (e.g., basic nervous system structures, fear conditioning).

2. Experimental Control

Researchers can control genetic background, developmental history, diet, and environment far more rigorously than in human cohorts.

3. Physiological Simplicity

Simpler nervous systems (e.g., Aplysia sea slugs used by Eric Kandel) allow scientists to isolate basic neural pathways for memory and learning.

4. Ethical Feasibility

Allows invasive neurological experiments (e.g., deep brain stimulation, gene knockout, brain lesioning) that are ethically prohibited in human subjects.

4. Branches of Psychology

Clinical Psychology Diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of severe psychological disorders and mental illnesses.
Cognitive Psychology Investigation of internal mental processes: memory, language, perception, decision-making, and reasoning.
Developmental Psychology Study of physical, cognitive, and social behavioral changes across the lifespan from conception to death.
Social Psychology How individual thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are influenced by actual or imagined presence of others.
Biological / Neuro-Psychology Neural, anatomical, chemical, and genetic substrates underlying mind and behavior.
Industrial-Organizational Application of psychological principles to workplace dynamics, productivity, leadership, and human resources.

5. Behavioral Research Methods & The Experimental Method

[12.5 Marks Core Question]

Behavioral research methods in psychology include: Observational Method (naturalistic/controlled), Survey Method, Case Study Method, Correlational Studies, and the Experimental Method.

The Experimental Method: In-Depth Breakdown

The Experimental Method is the gold standard of scientific psychology because it is the only research method capable of establishing definitive cause-and-effect relationships between variables.

Independent Variable (IV) The variable manipulated or controlled directly by the experimenter (the hypothesized cause).
Dependent Variable (DV) The variable measured to observe the effect of the IV (the hypothesized outcome).
Experimental vs. Control Group Experimental group receives the treatment/IV; Control group receives baseline condition or placebo.
Random Assignment Assigning participants by chance to ensure equal probability across conditions, eliminating confounding variables.
Critical Evaluation: Advantages vs. Disadvantages
Advantages Disadvantages & Limitations
Causality: Can uniquely demonstrate cause-and-effect relationships. Artificiality / Low Ecological Validity: Controlled laboratory environments may not reflect real-world human behavior.
High Control: Extraneous variables can be isolated or eliminated. Experimenter Bias & Demand Characteristics: Participants may alter responses due to perceived expectations.
Replicability: Standardized operational procedures allow other scientists to replicate tests. Ethical Constraints: Certain variables (e.g., trauma, severe deprivation) cannot be ethically manipulated.
Module 02

Biological Bases: Neuron & Central Nervous System

Neuroscience

1. The Nerve Cell (Neuron): Structure, Action Potential & Synapse

[4-Mark & 12.5-Mark Relevant]

A Neuron (nerve cell) is the fundamental structural and functional unit of the nervous system (~86 billion in the human brain), specialized for receiving, processing, and transmitting electrical and chemical impulses.

Interactive Diagram: Structural Anatomy of a Neuron
Soma (Cell Body): Contains nucleus and organelles; maintains metabolic functions and integrates incoming synaptic signals.
Dendrites: Branch-like extensions that receive electrochemical signals from neighboring presynaptic neurons.
Axon: Long single fiber that conducts electrical action potentials away from the soma toward target cells.
Myelin Sheath & Nodes: Fatty glial layer (Schwann cells / Oligodendrocytes) insulating axon; Nodes of Ranvier allow rapid saltatory conduction.
Action Potential & Neurotransmission:

Resting Potential (-70mV): Maintained by Na+/K+ pump. Threshold (-55mV): All-or-None response triggered. Depolarization: Na+ ion influx. Repolarization: K+ efflux. Synaptic transmission occurs across the Synaptic Cleft via neurotransmitter vesicles.

2. Structure of Brain & Brain Function

Hindbrain
  • Medulla Oblongata: Autonomic vital functions (breathing, heart rate, blood pressure).
  • Pons: Sleep, REM cycles, facial expressions, bridge to cerebellum.
  • Cerebellum: Motor coordination, balance, procedural motor memory.
Midbrain
  • Reticular Formation (RAS): Controls arousal, alertness, sleep-wake transitions.
  • Substantia Nigra: Dopamine production, voluntary motor control initiation.
Forebrain
  • Thalamus: Sensory relay station (relays all senses except smell).
  • Hypothalamus: Homeostasis, 4 Fs (Fighting, Fleeing, Feeding, Mating), endocrine control.
  • Limbic System: Hippocampus (memory consolidation) & Amygdala (emotion/fear).

The 4 Lobes of the Cerebral Cortex:

Frontal Lobe Executive functions, decision-making, planning, primary motor cortex, and Broca's Area (speech production).
Parietal Lobe Primary somatosensory cortex (touch, pressure, temperature, pain), spatial awareness and body orientation.
Temporal Lobe Auditory processing, memory processing, facial recognition, and Wernicke's Area (language comprehension).
Occipital Lobe Primary visual cortex, visual processing (color, motion, shape, spatial awareness).

3. Brain Function, Neurotransmitters & Mental Health

Mental health disorders reflect underlying chemical, structural, and neuro-circuitry imbalances:

  • Dopamine: Excess in mesolimbic pathway linked to positive symptoms of Schizophrenia (hallucinations/delusions); deficit in basal ganglia leads to Parkinson's Disease.
  • Serotonin (5-HT): Deficiency strongly associated with Major Depressive Disorder and anxiety disorders (SSRIs block reuptake to elevate synaptic levels).
  • GABA (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid): Major inhibitory neurotransmitter. Deficits cause hyper-excitability, panic, and clinical Anxiety Disorders.
  • Neuroplasticity: The brain's dynamic capacity to reorganize synaptic connections in response to learning, environment, or injury. Impaired neuroplasticity in the hippocampus is linked to chronic stress and depression.
Module 03

Sensation & Sensory Systems

Perception

1. What is Sensation? Core Concepts & Thresholds

Sensation is the process by which sensory receptors detect physical energy (stimuli) from the environment and encode it into neural signals. The process of converting physical stimulus energy into electro-chemical nerve impulses is termed Transduction.

Absolute Threshold The minimum intensity of a stimulus required for a person to detect it 50% of the time (e.g., a candle flame seen 30 miles away on a clear night).
Difference Threshold (JND) & Weber's Law Just Noticeable Difference (JND) is the minimal change required to detect a difference between two stimuli. Weber's Law states that JND is a constant proportion ($\Delta I / I = k$) of the original stimulus.
Sensory Adaptation Diminished sensitivity to a prolonged, unchanging stimulus (e.g., no longer noticing the feeling of clothes against skin or a constant background odor).

Overview of Human Sensory Modalities:

Vision (Visual): Receptors = Rods (scotopic/low-light vision) & Cones (photopic/color vision in Fovea). Pathway = Retina → Optic Nerve → Thalamus (LGN) → Occipital Cortex.
Audition (Hearing): Receptors = Hair cells on the basilar membrane inside the Cochlea. Pathway = Auditory nerve → Thalamus (MGN) → Temporal Cortex.
Gustation (Taste): Receptors = Taste buds inside papillae (Sweet, Salty, Sour, Bitter, Umami).
Olfaction (Smell): Receptors = Olfactory epithelium in nasal cavity. Unique: Signals bypass the Thalamus directly to Olfactory Bulb and Limbic System.
Somatosensation (Touch): Mechanoreceptors (pressure), Thermoreceptors (temperature), Nociceptors (pain).
Kinesthetic & Vestibular: Kinesthesis (proprioceptors in joints/muscles for body position); Vestibular (semicircular canals in inner ear for equilibrium/balance).
Module 04

Mind, Consciousness & Freud's Psychoanalysis

[12.5-Mark High Weight]

1. What is the Mind? Mind vs. Brain Distinction

The Brain is a tangible, physical biological organ composed of neurons, neuroglia, blood vessels, and neurotransmitter chemicals. The Mind refers to the non-tangible functional aggregate of conscious, subjective experiences, thoughts, feelings, memories, perceptions, and self-awareness. In philosophy of mind: "The mind is what the brain does."

2. Freud's Psychoanalytic Theory: Topographical & Structural Models

Visual Model: Freud's Iceberg Metaphor of Mind & Personality
1. Conscious Mind Everything an individual is actively aware of at the present moment (perceptions, current thoughts, immediate feelings).
2. Preconscious Mind Information not currently in active awareness, but easily recalled into consciousness with effort (e.g., home address, phone numbers).
3. Unconscious Mind The vast reservoir of primitive drives, repressed traumatic memories, forbidden sexual/aggressive urges (Eros/Thanatos).

Structural Tripartite Model of Personality:

ID (Pleasure Principle) Innate, present at birth. Completely unconscious. Operates on Pleasure Principle seeking immediate gratification of instinctual impulses (Eros/Libido & Thanatos). Uses Primary Process Thinking.
EGO (Reality Principle) Develops in early childhood. Operates on Reality Principle. Pragmatic mediator between Id demands, Superego constraints, and external reality. Uses Secondary Process Thinking.
SUPEREGO (Morality Principle) Develops around age 5 (resolution of Oedipus complex). Internalized moral compass and societal standards. Consists of Conscience (guilt) and Ego Ideal (pride).
Intrapsychic Conflict & Ego Defense Mechanisms:

Conflict between Id desires and Superego moral prohibitions generates Neurotic Anxiety. To protect the conscious mind, the Ego deploys unconscious Defense Mechanisms:

Repression: Pushing unacceptable thoughts into unconscious.
Projection: Attributing one's unwanted motives onto others.
Sublimation: Channeling unacceptable drives into socially valued achievements.
Reaction Formation: Expressing the exact opposite of actual feelings.
Module 05

Learning Theories & Behavioral Conditioning

Behaviorism

1. What is Learning? Pavlov's Classical Conditioning Theory

Definition: Learning is a relatively permanent change in behavior, knowledge, or behavioral potential that occurs as a result of experience or practice.

Ivan Pavlov's Classical Conditioning Paradigm (S-R Association):
1. Before Conditioning:
Unconditioned Stimulus (UCS: Food) → Unconditioned Response (UCR: Salivation)
Neutral Stimulus (NS: Bell) → No Salivation
2. During Conditioning (Acquisition):
NS (Bell) repeatedly paired with UCS (Food) → UCR (Salivation)
3. After Conditioning:
Conditioned Stimulus (CS: Bell) → Conditioned Response (CR: Salivation)
Core Principles:
• Extinction: CS presented without UCS repeatedly leads to fading of CR.
• Spontaneous Recovery: Re-emergence of CR after rest period.
• Generalization & Discrimination: Responding to similar stimuli vs. distinguishing between them.

2. Bandura's Observational Learning & The Role of Reinforcement

[12.5 Marks Target]

Albert Bandura's Social Learning Theory posits that individuals learn complex behaviors through observing, retaining, and imitating the actions of role models (as demonstrated in the classic Bobo Doll Experiment).

1. Attention Observer must actively attend to model's behavior and features.
2. Retention Encoding observed behavior in memory (cognitive symbolic representation).
3. Motor Reproduction Physical/cognitive execution capability to replicate the observed behavior.
4. Motivation Incentive to perform (Direct, Vicarious, or Self-reinforcement).
Role of Reinforcement in Learning (Operant Conditioning):
Positive Reinforcement: Presenting a rewarding stimulus to increase behavior frequency (e.g., praise, rewards).
Negative Reinforcement: Removing an unpleasant stimulus to increase behavior frequency (e.g., taking aspirin removes headache).
Punishment: Introducing adverse consequence to decrease behavior frequency.
Schedules: Fixed/Variable Ratio (VR produces highest resistance to extinction) and Fixed/Variable Interval.

Module 07

Memory Architecture & Causes of Forgetting

Cognitive Science

1. Definition, Stages & Atkinson-Shiffrin Model (STM vs. LTM)

Memory is the cognitive mechanism through which an organism encodes, stores, consolidates, and retrieves information over time. The 3 core stages are: Encoding (converting sensory input into neural code), Storage (maintaining information over time), and Retrieval (accessing stored information).

Feature Short-Term Memory (STM / Working Memory) Long-Term Memory (LTM)
Capacity Limited: $7 \pm 2$ chunks of information (Miller's Law). Virtually unlimited capacity.
Duration 15 to 30 seconds without maintenance rehearsal. Minutes to a lifetime.
Primary Code Acoustic / Auditory & Phonological buffer. Semantic (meaning-based) and network associations.
Neuroanatomy Prefrontal Cortex & Working Memory circuits. Hippocampus (consolidation) & Neocortex.

2. What is Forgetting? Comprehensive Causes of Forgetting

[4-Mark & 12.5-Mark Core]

Forgetting is the inability to retrieve or recall previously stored information from memory.

1. Trace Decay Theory (Disuse) Memory traces (engrams) in the brain physical structure naturally fade over time if not periodically reactivated or rehearsed.
2. Interference Theory • Proactive Interference: Old information interferes with recalling new information.
• Retroactive Interference: Newly learned material interferes with recalling older information.
3. Encoding Failure Information never successfully entered long-term memory due to lack of attention or shallow processing.
4. Retrieval Cue Failure Information exists in LTM but cannot be accessed due to missing environmental or emotional retrieval cues (e.g., Tip-of-the-Tongue state).
5. Motivated Forgetting (Repression) Psychoanalytic defense mechanism: active pushing of painful or anxiety-provoking traumatic memories into the unconscious.
6. Organic / Neurological Causes Brain damage, traumatic brain injury (TBI), Alzheimer's neurodegeneration, or amnesia (Anterograde vs. Retrograde).
Module 08

Personality Assessment & Trait Theories

Assessment

1. Personality Definition, Characteristics & Projective Tests (Rorschach & TAT)

Personality: According to Gordon Allport, personality is "the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his unique adjustments to his environment."

Rorschach Inkblot Test • Developed by Hermann Rorschach (1921). Consists of 10 standardized inkblots (5 black/white, 2 black/red, 3 multicolored).
• Subject states what they see. Administered in 2 phases: Free Association & Inquiry.
• Scored across dimensions: Location (whole blot vs detail), Determinants (form, color, shading, movement), and Content.
Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) • Developed by Henry Murray & Christina Morgan (1935). Consists of 31 ambiguous picture cards depicting human scenes.
• Subject tells a story detailing what led up to the scene, what is happening now, what characters feel, and outcome.
• Reveals latent motives: Need for Achievement (nAch), Power, and Affiliation.

2. Raymond Cattell's 16 Trait Theory of Personality

Raymond Cattell used Factor Analysis (statistical technique) to reduce Allport's list of trait words into 16 primary source traits (measured by the 16PF Questionnaire).

Surface Traits: Observable behavioral tendencies (e.g., altruism, quietness) resulting from combinations of source traits.
Source Traits: Underlying basic structural building blocks of personality (e.g., Warmth, Emotional Stability, Dominance).
Tri-partite Data Collection:
• L-Data: Life record data.
• Q-Data: Questionnaire self-reports.
• T-Data: Objective experimental testing data.
Module 09

Intelligence & Cognitive Assessment

[12.5-Mark Core]

1. What is Intelligence? Heredity vs. Environment & Binet-Simon Scale

Intelligence: According to Wechsler, intelligence is the global capacity of an individual to act purposefully, think rationally, and deal effectively with their environment.

Heredity vs. Environment Dynamics • Genetics (Heritability ~ 50-80%): Identical twins reared apart show higher IQ correlation ($r \approx 0.72$) than fraternal twins reared together ($r \approx 0.60$).
• Reaction Range Model: Genetics sets the lower and upper limits of potential intelligence; environmental quality determines where actual IQ falls within that range.
Binet-Simon Scale & IQ Formula • First practical intelligence test developed in 1905 by Alfred Binet & Theodore Simon to identify children needing special education.
• Introduced concept of Mental Age (MA).
• Lewis Terman (Stanford-Binet) popularized Stern's IQ formula:
IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) × 100

2. Critical Limitations of Intelligence Tests

[12.5 Marks Target]
  • 1. Cultural & Linguistic Bias: Tests historically favor Western, middle-class urban populations and penalize minority or non-standard linguistic backgrounds.
  • 2. Narrow Conceptual Scope: Focuses heavily on academic, analytical, and linguistic abilities, neglecting Emotional Intelligence (EQ), Practical Intelligence (Sternberg), and Creative/Musical/Spatial talents (Gardner).
  • 3. Labeling Effects & Self-Fulfilling Prophecy: Rigid IQ scoring can result in damaging social tracking, low expectations, and reduced self-esteem.
  • 4. Non-Intellective Influences: Scores can be distorted by test anxiety, physical fatigue, socio-economic motivation, or dynamic test environments.
Module 10

Emotion & Physiological Responses

[4-Mark Focus]

1. What is Emotion? 3 Elements & Difference Between Emotion and Feeling

Emotion: A complex psychological reaction involving 3 distinct elements: (1) Cognitive appraisal of a stimulus, (2) Physiological arousal, and (3) Expressive/behavioral response.

Distinction: Emotion vs. Feeling (4 Marks)
Emotion • Objective, neuro-biological, bodily reaction to external or internal stimulus.
• Instantaneous, un-conscious, short-lived, measurable via autonomic physiological markers (GSR, heart rate).
Feeling • Subjective, mental interpretation and conscious cognitive awareness of the underlying emotion.
• Long-lasting, shaped by personal history, beliefs, and cultural context.

2. Physiological & Bodily Changes Associated with Emotion

Autonomic Nervous System: Sympathetic nervous system activation ("Fight-or-Flight"): increased heart rate, dilated pupils, inhibited digestion.
Galvanic Skin Response (GSR): Increased sweat gland activity leading to heightened skin electrical conductance.
Hormonal Secretions: Adrenal glands release Adrenaline (Epinephrine) and Cortisol into bloodstream.

Short Answer Collection [4 Marks Each]

Q: Why is psychology called a biosocial science? (4 marks)

4 MARKS

Model Answer: Psychology is termed a biosocial (or biopsychosocial) science because it systematically integrates the biological natural sciences and the social sciences to explain human behavior and mental processes.

Specifically, it studies:

  1. Biological Foundations: Neural mechanisms, neurotransmitter pathways, genetic inheritance, and brain structures.
  2. Social Dynamics: Environmental influences, cultural norms, interpersonal relationships, and socio-economic contexts.

Examiner Tip: Mentioning that behavior sits at the nexus of biological hardware and social environment secures full 4 marks.

Q: What is a nerve-cell / neuron? (4 marks)

4 MARKS

Model Answer: A nerve cell or neuron is the fundamental specialized biological unit of the nervous system responsible for receiving, integrating, and transmitting electrochemical impulses throughout the body.

Core Anatomical Structures:

  • Soma (Cell Body): Contains the nucleus and maintains cell metabolism.
  • Dendrites: Receives chemical input from adjacent neurons.
  • Axon: Single long fiber transmitting action potential electrical impulses away from soma.
  • Synaptic Terminals: Releases chemical neurotransmitters across the synaptic cleft.

Q: What are the differences between emotion and feeling? (4 marks)

4 MARKS

Model Answer: While used interchangeably in casual language, psychology distinguishes them clearly:

Emotion: Unconscious, bodily physiological reaction (ANS arousal, hormonal surge, heart rate spike). Instantaneous and short-lived.
Feeling: Conscious subjective experience and cognitive interpretation of that emotional state. Long-lasting and mentally processed.

Q: What are the causes of forgetting? (4 marks)

4 MARKS

Model Answer: Forgetting is the inability to retrieve stored LTM information. The 4 major causes are:

  1. Trace Decay: Disuse fading of neural memory traces over time.
  2. Interference: Proactive (old blocks new) or Retroactive (new blocks old).
  3. Retrieval Failure: Lack of proper environmental/cognitive retrieval cues.
  4. Motivated Forgetting: Freud's repression of anxiety-inducing memories.
Long Essay Collection [12.5 Marks Each]

Q: What is meant by psychology? Briefly discuss different branches of Psychology. (12.5 marks)

12.5 MARKS

1. Introduction & Formal Definition (2.5 marks): Psychology is defined as the scientific study of behavior and mental processes. Etymologically derived from Psyche (mind) and Logos (study). It systematically investigates overt actions and covert cognitive processes using empirical research methodologies.

2. Detailed Exploration of Branches (8 marks):

  • Clinical Psychology: Focuses on assessment, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of mental illness and psychological pathology.
  • Cognitive Psychology: Examines internal mental mechanics: memory storage, language acquisition, problem-solving, and perception.
  • Developmental Psychology: Investigates cognitive, physical, and emotional growth across the entire human lifespan.
  • Biological / Neuro-Psychology: Explores brain anatomy, neurotransmitters, and neural pathways driving behavior.
  • Social Psychology: Analyzes how individual behavior is affected by group dynamics, social norms, and authority.
  • Industrial-Organizational Psychology: Applies psychological theories to workplace efficiency, leadership, and organization.

3. Synthesis & Conclusion (2 marks): These branches interlock to provide a holistic biosocial framework for human behavior.

Q: What are the different methods of psychology? Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of experimental method. (12.5 marks)

12.5 MARKS

1. Overview of Psychological Research Methods (3 marks): Includes Observational Studies, Case Studies, Surveys, Correlational Research, and the Experimental Method.

2. Anatomy of the Experimental Method (4.5 marks): The experimental method manipulates an Independent Variable (IV) under strict controlled conditions to observe changes in a Dependent Variable (DV). Relies on operational definitions, experimental vs. control groups, and random assignment to eliminate confounding variables.

3. Critical Evaluation: Advantages & Disadvantages (5 marks):

Advantages • Establishes true cause-and-effect relationships.
• High internal validity through variable control.
• High replicability due to standardized protocols.
Disadvantages • Low ecological validity (artificial lab setting).
• Experimenter bias & demand characteristics.
• Ethical constraints prevent manipulating severe trauma/stress.

Q: Explain the theory of structure of mind and structure of personality according to Freud. (12.5 marks)

12.5 MARKS

1. Topographical Model of Mind (5 marks): Sigmund Freud conceptualized the mind using an iceberg metaphor divided into three levels of awareness: Conscious (active awareness), Preconscious (easily accessible memory), and Unconscious (repressed trauma, primal drives).

2. Structural Tripartite Theory of Personality (5 marks):

  • Id: Fully unconscious, present at birth, operates on Pleasure Principle seeking immediate gratification via primary process thinking.
  • Ego: Realistic mediator, operates on Reality Principle using secondary process logical thinking to satisfy Id safely.
  • Superego: Moral compass, develops at ~5 years, contains Conscience (guilt) and Ego Ideal. Operates on Morality Principle.

3. Intrapsychic Conflict & Anxiety (2.5 marks): Ego deploys Defense Mechanisms (repression, projection, sublimation) to manage anxiety caused by Id-Superego conflict.
