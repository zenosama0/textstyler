1. Islam as a Complete Code of Life

5 marks + essays

In a course framed around Islam as a comprehensive way of life, “complete code” means that Islam is not confined to private belief or ritual. The Qur'an and Sunnah provide principles and commands that shape the believer's relationship with God, personal character, family, social relations, economic conduct, justice and public life.

1. Aqidah / belief

Faith in Allah's oneness, prophets, revelation, angels, the Hereafter and divine accountability gives life a moral and metaphysical framework.

2. 'Ibadah / worship

Prayer, fasting, zakat, pilgrimage and other forms of worship discipline the individual and orient life toward God.

3. Akhlaq / ethics

Truthfulness, justice, mercy, modesty, patience, trustworthiness and respect are treated as practical duties.

4. Family

Marriage, parental duties, inheritance, rights and responsibilities are given a normative structure.

5. Social justice

Human dignity, care for the vulnerable, charity, fair dealing and opposition to oppression are central social themes.

6. Economic life

Honesty in trade, fulfillment of contracts, zakat and restrictions on exploitative practices frame economic behavior.

7. Law and public order

Islamic thought develops principles concerning rights, duties, justice, dispute resolution and governance.

8. Knowledge

Seeking beneficial knowledge, reflection on creation and intellectual activity are valued within a religious framework.

9. Politics

Political thought discusses justice, legitimacy, consultation, obedience, accountability and the public good.

10. Afterlife

Final accountability gives moral action an ultimate horizon beyond immediate worldly reward or punishment.

5-mark exam answer: Islam is described as a complete code of life because it guides both the inner and outer dimensions of human existence. It gives principles of faith, worship, morality, family life, economics, social justice, law, politics and personal conduct. The Qur'an and Sunnah provide the primary foundations, while Muslim scholars developed detailed rules and institutions from them. Thus Islam connects belief with action: faith should shape character, worship should discipline the self, and social conduct should reflect justice and responsibility. The ultimate goal is a balanced life under the consciousness of God and accountability in the Hereafter.

2. Muslim Philosophy: Definition, Nature, Sources & Scope

High priority

What is Muslim philosophy?

Muslim philosophy may be understood as the philosophical activity carried out by Muslim thinkers, especially the rational investigation of reality, God, human nature, knowledge, ethics, society and the cosmos, undertaken in an Islamic intellectual environment. In medieval sources, falsafa often refers to the philosophical tradition shaped heavily by Greek philosophy, especially Aristotle and late Platonism.

The label is not perfectly uniform. Some authors use “Islamic philosophy” and “Muslim philosophy” almost interchangeably; others distinguish them by scope.

Nature / characteristics

  1. Rational: it uses argument, logic, analysis and demonstration.
  2. Metaphysical: it asks about God, being, causation, soul, eternity and existence.
  3. Ethical: it asks how human beings attain perfection and happiness.
  4. Religious: it develops in dialogue, agreement and sometimes conflict with revelation and theology.
  5. Historical: it arose in the context of a multilingual, multicultural intellectual world that absorbed Greek, Persian and Indian materials.
  6. Systematic: thinkers built interconnected accounts of logic, physics/nature, psychology, metaphysics, ethics and politics.

Sources

Primary Islamic sources

Qur'an and Sunnah/Hadith are the foundational religious sources. They supply concepts, values, theological questions and a worldview with which Muslim thinkers engage.

Greek philosophical heritage

Aristotle, Plato and late antique/Neoplatonic materials became important through translation and commentary.

Kalām and jurisprudence

Theological debate and legal reasoning supplied categories for questions of divine attributes, freedom, causation, authority and moral responsibility.

Persian, Indian and other traditions

Muslim intellectual life also interacted with Persian, Indian and other scientific/philosophical traditions.

Scope

The scope is broad: logic, epistemology, metaphysics, natural philosophy, psychology of the soul, ethics, politics, religion, mystical knowledge and the relation between revelation and reason.

Model 12.5-mark answer: “Define Muslim philosophy and analyze various definitions.”

Introduction: Muslim philosophy refers to philosophical inquiry developed by Muslim thinkers in the intellectual world of Islam. The medieval philosophical movement is often called falsafa, and it engaged questions of being, God, knowledge, soul, ethics and politics through rational argument.

Al-Kindi: He defines philosophy in terms of knowledge of the realities of things within human capacity. The emphasis is epistemological: philosophy seeks truth about reality, but recognizes the limits of human reason.

Al-Farabi: His conception is more systematic. Philosophy studies existing things through their causes and principles and ultimately serves the attainment of human perfection and true happiness. Thus it unites theoretical knowledge with practical purpose.

Ibn Sina: Avicenna develops a highly systematic conception in which philosophy studies things as they are insofar as human beings can know them. His system gives central importance to being, necessary and contingent existence, causation, intellect and soul.

Miskawayh: His ethical orientation links philosophy with perfection of the soul and cultivation of virtue, showing that philosophy is not simply speculation but also self-formation.

Analysis: The definitions differ in emphasis—Al-Kindi stresses truth and human capacity, Al-Farabi knowledge of causes and happiness, Ibn Sina systematic knowledge of being, and Miskawayh moral perfection. Yet they share a common idea: philosophy is rational, systematic and directed toward truth and human perfection.

Conclusion: Muslim philosophy is therefore best understood as a broad rational enterprise seeking knowledge of reality and the good life, developed within the Muslim intellectual tradition and in dialogue with revelation, theology and inherited philosophies.

3. Historical Development of Muslim Philosophical Thinking

Timeline
  1. Prophetic and early caliphal period: religious teaching centered on revelation, faith, law and community. Philosophical inquiry was not yet organized into a separate discipline.
  2. Early political and theological conflicts: questions about succession, sin, faith, divine justice, human freedom and God's attributes generated debate. This helped produce the environment of kalām.
  3. Translation movement: under the Abbasids, especially in Baghdad, Greek philosophical and scientific works became available in Arabic. This included Aristotle, late Platonist materials and scientific texts.
  4. Rise of systematic kalām: Mu'tazilites and later Ash'arites developed sophisticated arguments concerning God, attributes, creation, causation, knowledge and free will.
  5. Emergence of falsafa: al-Kindi, al-Farabi and later Ibn Sina built philosophical systems drawing mainly on Aristotelian and Neoplatonic sources while attempting to harmonize them with Islamic thought.
  6. Critique and synthesis: al-Ghazali criticized key claims of the philosophers while also employing logic and philosophical methods. This did not simply end philosophy; later philosophy developed in new forms.
  7. Andalusian flowering: Ibn Bajjah, Ibn Tufayl and Ibn Rushd developed distinctive western Islamic philosophy, with Ibn Rushd especially defending Aristotelian philosophy.
  8. Mystical synthesis: Sufi thought developed from ascetic practices toward elaborate theories of spiritual knowledge, purification, love and union/gnosis.
  9. Later developments: illuminationism, post-Avicennian philosophy, later kalām, and continued philosophical-mystical synthesis extended the tradition.
  10. Modern thought: thinkers such as Muhammad Iqbal reworked Islamic philosophical questions in conversation with modern science and Western philosophy.
Fakhry memory line: Greek & Oriental legacy → translation → early religious/political tensions → kalām → systematic philosophy → interaction with theology → al-Ghazali → Sufism → Andalusian philosophy → later developments → Ibn Khaldun / modern trends / Iqbal.

12.5-mark answer: Compare Qadariya and Jabariya on freedom of will

The debate concerns how to reconcile God's complete power and knowledge with human responsibility. Qadariya emphasized human capacity and freedom: if people are morally judged for their acts, their choices must be genuinely significant. This position therefore protects moral responsibility and divine justice.

Jabariya stands at the opposite extreme. It emphasizes divine determination so strongly that human acts are treated as compelled or lacking independent causal efficacy. The goal is to preserve God's absolute sovereignty.

The strengths and problems of each position are clear. Pure determinism threatens to weaken ordinary ideas of moral responsibility, while unlimited human freedom can appear to compromise divine omnipotence. Later Sunni theology, especially Ash'arism, sought a middle formulation through kasb (acquisition), while Maturidism gave human choice a more substantial role. Thus the Qadariya-Jabariya dispute helped shape one of the central problems of Islamic theology: how divine omnipotence and human responsibility can coexist.

Mu'tazilites

The Mu'tazilites developed a strongly rationalist form of kalām. Their famous “Five Principles” are often summarized as: divine unity (tawhid), divine justice ('adl), promise and threat (al-wa'd wa'l-wa'id), the intermediate position, and commanding right / forbidding wrong.

  • God's unity means divine attributes should not compromise radical divine simplicity.
  • God is just, so human beings must have meaningful responsibility.
  • The Qur'an is created rather than eternal as an independently co-eternal entity.
  • Reason can discover important moral truths.
  • A grave sinner is neither simply a believer nor an unbeliever in this world, but occupies an intermediate position in Mu'tazili doctrine.

Ash'ariyya

Founded by Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari, Ash'arism sought a Sunni kalām position that defended revelation while allowing reasoned argument. It strongly protects divine omnipotence. The doctrine of kasb (acquisition) became a key way of explaining human responsibility without granting humans independent creative power over acts.

Terminology alertYour previous-year question says “Beautification of Allah.” In standard English this is almost certainly a mistranslation/typo for Beatific Vision / Vision of Allah (Ru'yat Allah). Write the standard term in the exam and, if needed, note “vision of Allah in the Hereafter.”

Sifatiyya

Sifatiyya refers broadly to those who emphasize affirmation of the divine attributes (sifat). The central theological problem is how to affirm God's attributes without implying that God resembles created beings. This label can cover a range of positions in early Sunni theological history, so do not treat it as a perfectly uniform sect.

Murji'a

The Murji'a (“those who defer/postpone”) emerged in early disputes over faith, sin and political conflict. They argued that ultimate judgment belongs to God and resisted declaring sinful Muslims unbelievers merely on the basis of grave sin.

Exam hook: “Irja' = defer judgment to Allah.”

Tahawiyya

Al-Tahawi's al-'Aqidah al-Tahawiyyah is a concise Sunni creed rather than a parallel philosophical school in exactly the same sense as Mu'tazilism or Ash'arism. It became influential across Sunni traditions and affirms core doctrines about God's oneness and attributes, prophecy, revelation, the Hereafter, divine decree and the companions. If your lecturer calls it a “school,” present it as a creedal tradition associated with Imam al-Tahawi.

Maturidiyya

Associated with Abu Mansur al-Maturidi of Samarkand, Maturidism is a major Sunni theological school. It gives reason a substantial role in knowing God and moral truths while maintaining revelation and divine sovereignty. In discussions of free will, Maturidism generally allows a stronger role for human choice than strict determinism while maintaining that God's knowledge and power encompass all things.

One-paragraph comparison of the major theological schools

Mu'tazilites emphasize divine unity, justice, rational moral knowledge, human freedom and the created Qur'an; Ash'arites strongly emphasize divine omnipotence, affirm real attributes and explain human responsibility through kasb; Maturidites also defend Sunni orthodoxy but give reason a substantial role and a stronger account of human choice; Murji'a defer final judgment of sinners to God; Sifatiyya emphasize affirmation of divine attributes; Qadariya and Jabariya represent opposite poles in the free-will debate; and the Tahawiyya tradition preserves a concise Sunni creed rather than constituting a separate philosophical school of the same kind.

Model 12.5-mark answer: Shia vs Sunni vs Kharijis

Introduction: The three traditions developed around the problem of legitimate political-religious leadership after the Prophet Muhammad.

Sunni: Classical Sunni theory treats the caliphate as a political institution for preserving the unity and order of the Muslim community. Leadership is not an article of faith requiring a divinely designated hereditary line. Consultation, communal recognition and practical legitimacy are emphasized in different formulations.

Shi'a: Shi'i thought places the Imamate at the center. The Imam is not merely a political administrator but a uniquely authoritative religious guide. Twelver Shi'ism teaches a succession of twelve Imams descending from 'Ali and Fatima through Husayn, while other Shi'i branches have different lines of succession.

Kharijites: The Kharijite tradition opposed dynastic privilege and insisted that leadership should be open to a morally qualified Muslim. A ruler who becomes unjust or gravely sinful can lose legitimacy. Thus leadership rests more on righteousness than lineage.

Comparison: Sunni theory prioritizes community order and caliphal governance; Shi'a emphasizes divinely guided Imamate and the Prophet's family; Kharijites emphasize justice and moral fitness and reject hereditary monopoly. The key contrast is therefore caliphate vs Imamate vs merit-based leadership.

Conclusion: These differences arose from both political events and deeper beliefs about authority, justice and the proper relation between religion and government.

Ithna Ashariyya (The Twelvers)

The Ithna Ashariyya or Twelver Shi'a are the largest branch of Shi'ism. They recognize a succession of twelve Imams beginning with 'Ali ibn Abi Talib and continuing through his descendants from Husayn. The twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is believed to have entered occultation and will return in the end times. The Imams are regarded as specially guided authorities in religion, and the doctrine of Imamate is central to Twelver identity.

6. Sufism: Main Doctrines & The Sufi Path

5 + 12.5 marks

Sufism (tasawwuf) is the tradition of Islamic spirituality concerned with purification of the heart, discipline of the ego, remembrance of God and experiential knowledge of the Divine.

Fundamental principles

  1. Tawhid: radical awareness of God's oneness.
  2. Tazkiyat al-nafs: purification of the lower self and its passions.
  3. Dhikr: remembrance of Allah through repeated names, prayers or devotional practices.
  4. Muhasaba: self-examination/accounting of one's inner state and conduct.
  5. Tawbah: sincere repentance and return to God.
  6. Mahabba: divine love; the heart moves from fear and duty toward intimate love of God.
  7. Ma'rifa: spiritual/gnostic knowledge of God.
  8. Fana' and baqa': often explained as the passing away of ego-centered selfhood and abiding in God, with terminology differing among Sufi authors.
  9. Adab: disciplined spiritual etiquette and right conduct.
  10. Shaykh / murshid: in many orders, guidance by an experienced spiritual teacher.

Model 12.5-mark answer: Fundamental principles of Sufism and the Sufi Path

Sufism is the inward or spiritual dimension of Islamic life. Its central goal is purification of the human being so that the heart becomes deeply conscious of God and the person approaches spiritual perfection.

Its main principles include tawhid, purification of the self, dhikr, repentance, self-examination, spiritual discipline, divine love, humility, trust in God and ma'rifa or spiritual knowledge. The Sufi does not merely seek information about God but attempts to transform the self through practice.

The Sufi path is often described through Shari'a → Tariqa → spiritual stations and states → ma'rifa → fana' and baqa'. Shari'a provides the disciplined religious foundation. Tariqa is the method of spiritual training. Through struggle, remembrance and guidance, the seeker advances through maqamat such as repentance, patience and trust, while experiencing ahwal or spiritual states.

The culmination is a transformed awareness in which the ego's independent claims are weakened and the seeker becomes oriented toward God. Sufism therefore links knowledge, worship, ethics and inner transformation rather than treating philosophy or religion as merely abstract subjects.

7. Muslim Philosophers of East & West

Memorize life + 3 works + 3 ideas

Al-Kindi (c. 800–870)

Known as: “Philosopher of the Arabs”; first major self-identified philosopher in the Arabic tradition.

Key works: On First Philosophy, On the Intellect, works on psychology, mathematics, cosmology and optics.

Ideas: philosophy as rational knowledge of reality; God's absolute unity; creation rather than an eternal world; intellect and soul; harmony between truth and revelation.

Al-Farabi (c. 870–950)

Known as: “Second Teacher” after Aristotle.

Key works: Al-Madina al-Fadila (The Virtuous City), The Political Regime, Enumeration of the Sciences, The Attainment of Happiness.

Ideas: hierarchy of intellects, active intellect, happiness, virtuous city, philosopher-prophet/ruler, relationship between philosophy and religion.

Ibn Sina / Avicenna (c. 970–1037)

Key works: Kitab al-Shifa, al-Najat, al-Qanun fi'l-Tibb.

Ideas: essence/existence distinction; Necessary Existent; emanation; intellects; soul as immaterial substance; famous “Flying Man” thought experiment; medicine and science.

Ibn Bajjah / Avempace (d. 1138)

Key work: Tadbir al-Mutawahhid (The Governance of the Solitary).

Ideas: intellectual perfection, solitary philosopher in imperfect society, active intellect, relation between individual and political community.

Ibn Tufayl (c. 1105–1185)

Key work: Hayy ibn Yaqzan.

Ideas: a human can discover truths about nature and God through observation, reason and intellectual development; philosophical truth and revealed religion can converge at the highest level.

Ibn Rushd / Averroes (1126–1198)

Key works: Tahafut al-Tahafut, Fasl al-Maqal, commentaries on Aristotle.

Ideas: defense of demonstrative philosophy, Aristotle, harmony of reason and revelation, law/philosophy relationship.

Ibn Miskawayh (c. 932–1030)

Key work: Tahdhib al-Akhlaq (Refinement of Character).

Ideas: ethics, cultivation of virtue, balance of faculties, perfection of the soul, moral education and happiness.

Al-Ghazali (c. 1056–1111)

Key works: Tahafut al-Falasifa, Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din, al-Munqidh min al-Dalal.

Ideas: critique of certain philosophical doctrines, certainty and epistemology, Sunni theology, ethics and Sufism. He did not simply reject every kind of philosophy.

Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406)

Key work: al-Muqaddimah.

Ideas: 'asabiyyah (group solidarity), rise and decline of dynasties, economics, social organization, historical causation and critical historical method.

Muhammad Iqbal (1877–1938)

Key works: The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam, Persian poetry including Asrar-e-Khudi.

Ideas: dynamic self (khudi), creativity, action, reconstruction of religious thought, engagement with modern science and Western philosophy, spiritual democracy/community.

Al-Farabi's Political Philosophy — full exam answer

12.5-mark model answer

Introduction: Al-Farabi's political philosophy is closely connected with his theory of human perfection and happiness. Humans cannot fully realize their highest capacities in isolation; they need society, education, law and a properly ordered political community.

The purpose of society: The true purpose of political association is not merely food, security and economic exchange. The highest end is sa'ada (true happiness/felicity). The best political community helps its members develop the intellectual and moral capacities needed for this end.

The Virtuous City: His ideal city, al-Madina al-Fadila, is a cooperative political order in which people work together to attain genuine happiness. It resembles a healthy organism: different social functions contribute to a common end.

The ruler: The ruler should possess exceptional intellectual and moral qualities. Ideally he is a philosopher-ruler who understands reality, the human soul, the proper ends of society and the means of guiding citizens. In some formulations this ideal ruler combines philosophical wisdom, moral virtue, leadership and the ability to communicate truths symbolically to the wider population.

Religion and philosophy: Religion translates or represents metaphysical truths in forms accessible to the wider community through symbols, images, laws and practices. Philosophy reaches those truths through demonstration. This is why Farabi connects political rule to both wisdom and religion.

Non-virtuous cities: Farabi also classifies defective political orders—cities that pursue wealth, honor, pleasure, power or other lower ends rather than true happiness. Democracy receives a complicated treatment: he regards it as a non-virtuous regime but also as a setting from which a virtuous city might emerge more easily than from some other corrupt regimes.

Evaluation: The strength of Farabi's theory is that politics is treated as moral education and human flourishing rather than mere power management. A weakness, from a modern democratic perspective, is the elitist character of the philosopher-ruler ideal and the suggestion that the wise should guide the less knowledgeable toward happiness.

Conclusion: Al-Farabi's political philosophy is therefore a theory of the city as a means to human perfection. Its key concepts are happiness, virtue, the virtuous city, wise leadership and the relationship between philosophy and religion.

Ibn Sina's Philosophy & the Soul

12.5-mark model answer

Ibn Sina developed one of the most systematic philosophical systems in the Islamic world. Major areas include metaphysics, logic, natural philosophy, psychology and medicine.

Essence and existence: For contingent beings, what a thing is (essence) is conceptually distinct from the fact that it exists. The Necessary Existent, God, is unique in that His existence is necessary rather than received from another cause.

Necessary and contingent: Contingent beings require a cause. The chain of dependent beings cannot ultimately explain itself; therefore, Avicenna argues for a Necessary Existent that explains the existence of contingent reality.

Soul: Avicenna treats the human soul as an immaterial substance and distinguishes its faculties. The soul is not reducible to the body. Vegetative faculties handle nutrition and growth; animal faculties include sensation, appetite and movement; human rational faculties include practical and theoretical intellect.

Flying Man: Imagine a person created fully formed and suspended in empty space, unable to touch anything or receive sensory input. Avicenna argues that such a person would still affirm his own existence. This is used to demonstrate the self-awareness and non-bodily character of the soul.

Intellect: Human cognition develops from potentiality to actuality, aided by the Active Intellect in Avicennian cosmology. Knowledge therefore involves abstraction from material conditions into intelligible forms.

Immortality: Because the rational soul is immaterial and not simply a bodily organ, Avicenna gives it a basis for survival beyond bodily death.

Evaluation: Avicenna's theory is powerful because it unites psychology, metaphysics and epistemology. Its main philosophical challenge is explaining precisely how an immaterial soul relates to a material body and how the afterlife should be understood.

8. Previous-Year Questions — 5 Marks

Write 1 compact page / 1–3 paragraphs
1. Discuss different aspects of Islam as a complete code of life.5
Answer: Islam is a complete code of life because it regulates both belief and conduct. It covers aqidah (belief), worship, moral character, family life, economic behavior, social justice, law, politics and personal responsibility. Its primary guidance comes from the Qur'an and Sunnah, while Muslim scholars develop detailed legal and ethical applications. Islam therefore links spiritual life with social life and emphasizes accountability before Allah in the Hereafter. A good Muslim life is expected to unite faith, worship, ethics and justice rather than keeping religion limited to ritual.
2. Who are Murjias? Describe in short their standpoint on the status of Muslims who commit sin.5
Answer: Murji'a were an early theological movement associated with the principle of irja', meaning postponement or deferral. In disputes over faith, sin and political conflict, they argued that ultimate judgment of a person's religious status should be left to Allah. Therefore, a Muslim who committed a grave sin should not automatically be declared an unbeliever on account of that sin. The movement thus emphasized faith and discouraged premature takfir, while leaving final judgment to God.
3. Who are the Ithna Ashariya (the Twelves)?5
Answer: Ithna Ashariyya, or Twelver Shi'a, are the largest branch of Shi'ism. They believe in twelve divinely guided Imams beginning with 'Ali ibn Abi Talib and continuing through his descendants from Husayn. The twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is believed to be in occultation and to return in the future. Imamate is therefore a central doctrine of Twelver Shi'ism.
4. Describe in short the classifications of different schools of thought in Muslim philosophy.5
Answer: Muslim intellectual schools may be classified broadly into: (1) theological schools, such as Mu'tazila, Ash'ariyya and Maturidiyya, which discuss God, attributes, free will and revelation; (2) religious-political schools, such as Sunni, Shi'a and Kharijite traditions, which debate authority and leadership; (3) philosophical schools, represented by thinkers such as al-Kindi, al-Farabi and Ibn Sina; and (4) mystical traditions, especially Sufism, which emphasizes spiritual purification and knowledge of God. Early debates also include Qadariya, Jabariya, Murji'a and Sifatiyya.

4. Discuss with comparison the views of Zabariya and Qadariya on freedom of will.12.5

Answer: The two positions represent opposite poles. Qadariya stress meaningful human choice and capacity, because moral reward and punishment presuppose responsibility. Jabariya (often “Jabriya”; your question's “Zabariya” is likely a spelling/transliteration issue) stress divine determination so strongly that human action is treated as compelled. Qadariya protects moral agency and justice; Jabariya protects absolute divine sovereignty. Later Sunni theology tried to formulate middle positions, especially Ash'ari kasb and Maturidi theories of choice. The debate is significant because it frames the enduring problem of reconciling divine omnipotence with human responsibility.

5. Discuss the fundamental principles of Sufism. In this regard, describe the Sufi Path.12.5
Answer: Sufism seeks purification of the heart and closeness to God. Core ideas include tawhid, repentance, dhikr, self-accounting, mujahada, divine love, ma'rifa, spiritual discipline, adab and guidance. The path can be remembered as Shari'a → Tariqa → Mujahada/Dhikr → Maqamat & Ahwal → Ma'rifa → Fana' & Baqa'. The outer law gives the discipline; the tariqa provides the method; stations and states describe spiritual development; ma'rifa signifies deeper experiential knowledge; fana' and baqa' symbolize profound transformation of the self in relation to God.

5-mark

1. Distinguish between kalām and falsafa.5
Reveal answer
Kalām is theological reasoning used to defend and formulate religious doctrine, whereas falsafa is the systematic philosophical tradition concerned with being, causes, knowledge, soul, ethics and politics. They overlap in topics and both use reason, but their historical purposes, methods and inherited sources differ. Kalām starts more directly from doctrinal questions; falsafa developed strongly through Aristotelian and Neoplatonic philosophy.
2. Write a short note on the Five Principles of the Mu'tazila.5
Reveal answer
Tawhid (divine unity), 'adl (divine justice), al-wa'd wa'l-wa'id (promise and threat), al-manzila bayna al-manzilatayn (intermediate position of the grave sinner), and commanding right/forbidding wrong.
3. What is the role of the Active Intellect in Avicennian thought?5
Reveal answer
The Active Intellect is the intellect that actualizes intelligible forms for the human mind in Avicennian psychology. It helps explain how human intellect moves from potentiality toward actual intellectual knowledge and is part of the broader hierarchy of intellects in Avicenna's cosmology.
4. What is the importance of Hayy ibn Yaqzan?5
Reveal answer
Ibn Tufayl's philosophical tale illustrates how observation, reason and intellectual development can lead a person toward knowledge of nature and God. It explores the relation between philosophical truth and revealed religion and the limits or capacities of society.

8-mark

1. Explain the historical causes behind the rise of kalām.8
Reveal answer
Discuss political conflicts, disputes over leadership and grave sin, questions about divine justice and human freedom, disputes over divine attributes and revelation, and later engagement with intellectual traditions. Then explain that kalām developed as rational theology to formulate and defend doctrine.
2. Discuss the place of reason in Mu'tazili and Maturidi theology.8
Reveal answer
Both give reason important work. Mu'tazilism is more strongly rationalist, especially concerning divine justice and moral knowledge. Maturidism also argues that reason can know God and basic moral truths, while maintaining a Sunni framework in which revelation remains indispensable.
