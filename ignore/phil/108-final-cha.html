1. Reading

Definition

Reading is the active process of receiving, interpreting, analyzing, and understanding meaning from written language.

It is not merely looking at words. A good reader connects the writer's words with context, prior knowledge, purpose, and evidence.

Components of Reading

  1. Word recognition: identifying written words.
  2. Vocabulary: understanding the meanings and uses of words.
  3. Grammar/syntax: understanding how words form sentences.
  4. Comprehension: understanding the explicit and implied meaning.
  5. Background knowledge: connecting the text with what the reader already knows.
  6. Critical evaluation: judging ideas, evidence, purpose, and reliability.

Reading Strategies

StrategyWhat it meansUse

Reading Materials

Reading materials include textbooks, academic articles, newspapers, magazines, novels, short stories, essays, reports, manuals, websites, advertisements, notices, and examination passages.

Types of Reading

  • Skimming: quick reading for the overall idea.
  • Scanning: quick reading to locate particular information.
  • Intensive reading: detailed study of a relatively short text.
  • Extensive reading: reading longer texts for broad understanding or pleasure.
  • Critical reading: examining arguments, evidence, assumptions and conclusions.
  • Silent reading: reading without producing the words aloud.
  • Reading aloud: vocalizing a written text, useful for pronunciation and fluency practice.

Techniques Used by Good Readers

  • Set a clear purpose before reading.
  • Preview the text and predict its content.
  • Identify the main idea and supporting details.
  • Use context to understand unfamiliar vocabulary.
  • Distinguish facts from opinions.
  • Take short notes and mark important points.
  • Ask questions about the text.
  • Make inferences instead of depending only on directly stated information.
  • Review and summarize after reading.
  • Adjust reading speed according to the purpose.

SQ3R Reading Strategy

SQ3R = Survey → Question → Read → Recite → Review.

  1. Survey: Quickly examine the title, headings, introduction, conclusion, illustrations and other structural clues.
  2. Question: Turn headings into questions. This gives you a purpose for reading.
  3. Read: Read actively to answer your questions and identify important information.
  4. Recite: Close the book and explain the important ideas in your own words.
  5. Review: Go back over the major ideas, notes and questions to strengthen memory.
Example: Heading: “Causes of Climate Change” → Question: “What are the major causes?” → Read to find them → Recite the causes without looking → Review them later.

Likely Exam Question + Model Answer

Question: What is SQ3R? Explain its steps.

Answer: SQ3R is a systematic reading strategy designed to improve comprehension and retention. The five steps are Survey, Question, Read, Recite and Review. First, the reader surveys the text to understand its structure and topic. Second, the reader develops questions from the headings or subject matter. Third, the text is read carefully to find answers and understand the important ideas. Fourth, the reader recites the information in their own words without looking at the text. Finally, the reader reviews the material to reinforce the main ideas and correct gaps in understanding. Thus, SQ3R makes reading active, purposeful and organized.

2. Writing

Definition

Writing is the process of communicating ideas, information, feelings, opinions, or arguments through organized written language.

Purposes of Writing

  • To inform: provide facts or explanations.
  • To explain: make an idea or process clear.
  • To describe: create a clear picture of a person, place, object or situation.
  • To narrate: tell events or experiences in sequence.
  • To persuade: convince readers to accept an idea or take action.
  • To entertain: engage readers through creative writing.
  • To evaluate/argue: present a position supported by reasons and evidence.

Components of Effective Writing

  • Content: relevant and sufficient ideas.
  • Organization: logical arrangement.
  • Unity: all parts support the central idea.
  • Coherence: ideas connect smoothly.
  • Cohesion: grammatical and lexical links connect sentences.
  • Vocabulary: appropriate and precise word choice.
  • Grammar: correct sentence construction.
  • Mechanics: spelling, punctuation, capitalization and formatting.

Types of Writing

  • Narrative writing
  • Descriptive writing
  • Expository/informative writing
  • Persuasive/argumentative writing
  • Academic writing
  • Creative writing
  • Functional writing: applications, letters, CVs, notices, reports, etc.

Writing Process

Prewriting → Drafting → Revising → Editing → Publishing/Finalizing

  1. Prewriting: understand the topic, brainstorm, research if necessary and make an outline.
  2. Drafting: write the first version without trying to make every sentence perfect.
  3. Revising: improve ideas, organization, unity, coherence and supporting details.
  4. Editing: correct grammar, spelling, punctuation, vocabulary and sentence errors.
  5. Finalizing: produce a clean final copy with appropriate format.

High-scoring academic writing checklist

Before:
Understand command words, identify topic, brainstorm, outline.
During:
Clear topic sentence/thesis, logical paragraphs, examples, transitions.
After:
Check grammar, spelling, punctuation, repetition and whether every part answers the question.

3. Summary Writing

What is a Summary?

A summary is a short, clear restatement of the main ideas of a longer text using your own words. It keeps the essential information and removes unnecessary details.

Rules to Follow

  1. Read the passage carefully at least twice.
  2. Identify the central idea and major supporting points.
  3. Remove examples, repetition, minor details, quotations and unnecessary descriptions.
  4. Write in your own words as much as possible.
  5. Keep the original meaning and logical order.
  6. Do not add your personal opinion.
  7. Use concise sentences and appropriate linking words.
  8. Follow any word limit given in the question.

Useful Format

Opening: The passage discusses/explains/describes ...
Body: State the main points in logical order.
Ending: Conclude naturally if needed; do not add a new idea.

Example

Original idea: “Regular exercise improves physical health. It strengthens the heart, helps control body weight, reduces the risk of several diseases and can also improve mood and sleep.”

Sample summary: Regular exercise benefits both physical and mental well-being by strengthening the heart, supporting healthy weight, reducing disease risks, and improving mood and sleep.

Notice: the central ideas remain, but the wording is shorter and more compact.

Likely Questions

  • Read the passage and write a summary in about one-third of its length.
  • Write a suitable title and summarize the passage.
  • Identify the main idea and summarize the passage in your own words.

4. Essay

Definition

An essay is a structured piece of writing that develops a central idea, argument, explanation, or discussion through an introduction, organized body paragraphs, and a conclusion.

Types of Essay

TypePurpose
NarrativeTell a story or sequence of events.
DescriptiveCreate a detailed picture of a person, place, object or experience.
ExpositoryExplain or inform objectively.
ArgumentativePresent a position and support it with reasons/evidence while addressing opposing views where appropriate.
PersuasiveAttempt to influence the reader toward a particular position or action.
Compare and contrastExamine similarities and/or differences between subjects.
Cause and effectExplain causes, effects, or both.

Parts of an Essay

  1. Introduction — introduces the topic, provides context and ends with the thesis statement in most academic essays.
  2. Body paragraphs — develop the thesis using main points, explanations, examples/evidence and analysis.
  3. Conclusion — restates the central argument in fresh words, summarizes major points and provides a final closing thought when appropriate.

Thesis Statement

A thesis statement is the central claim or controlling idea of an essay. It tells the reader what the essay will argue, explain, or establish.

Strong: “Regular physical exercise should be part of university students’ routines because it supports physical health, reduces stress, and can improve concentration.”
Weak: “Exercise is important.” — Too broad; it does not show the specific direction of the essay.

Easy thesis formula

Topic + position/main idea + 2–3 controlling points

Academic Essay Skeleton

Title: The Importance of Reading Habits for Students

Introduction: Background → context → thesis.

Body 1: First main point → explanation → example.

Body 2: Second main point → explanation → example.

Body 3: Third main point → explanation → example.

Conclusion: Restate thesis → synthesize main points → final observation.

Sample Essay Question + Model

Question: “The importance of effective communication in academic life.” Write an essay.

Introduction: Communication is an essential part of academic life because students must exchange ideas with teachers, classmates and institutions. Effective communication allows information to be expressed clearly and understood accurately. Therefore, effective communication is important in academic life because it improves learning, collaboration and students’ ability to express their ideas.

Body paragraph 1 — Learning: Effective communication supports learning by helping students ask questions, clarify difficult concepts and receive useful feedback. When students can explain what they understand and identify what they do not understand, classroom interaction becomes more productive.

Body paragraph 2 — Collaboration: Academic work frequently requires group discussion and cooperation. Clear communication helps group members divide responsibilities, exchange information and solve disagreements. As a result, collaborative tasks can be completed more efficiently.

Body paragraph 3 — Expression: Students also need communication skills when writing assignments, giving presentations and participating in discussions. Organizing ideas clearly enables students to demonstrate their knowledge rather than allowing poor expression to hide it.

Conclusion: Effective communication is therefore closely connected with successful academic participation. By improving their ability to listen, speak and write clearly, students can participate more confidently, cooperate with others and communicate their knowledge more effectively.

5. Paragraph

Definition

A paragraph is a group of related sentences that develops one main idea in a clear and organized way.

Parts of a Paragraph

  1. Topic sentence: states the main idea.
  2. Supporting sentences: explain, prove, illustrate or develop the main idea.
  3. Concluding sentence: brings the paragraph to a logical close or reinforces the main idea.

Key qualities: unity, coherence, adequate development and logical organization.

Five-step Process of Paragraph Development

  1. Choose a topic: identify exactly what you will discuss.
  2. Write the topic sentence: state one clear controlling idea.
  3. Develop supporting points: add reasons, facts, examples, explanations or details.
  4. Arrange and connect ideas: use logical order and transitions.
  5. Write a concluding sentence: close the paragraph without introducing an unrelated idea.

Useful Transition Words

PurposeWords
AdditionMoreover, furthermore, in addition, also
SequenceFirst, next, then, finally
CauseBecause, since, due to, as a result of
ContrastHowever, nevertheless, in contrast, whereas
ExampleFor example, for instance, such as
ConclusionTherefore, thus, consequently, in conclusion

Sample Paragraph

Question: Write a paragraph on “Benefits of Reading.”

Reading is a valuable habit that provides students with several academic and personal benefits. First, regular reading increases vocabulary and exposes learners to different styles of language. It also improves comprehension because readers learn to identify main ideas, supporting details and relationships between concepts. Furthermore, reading introduces students to new information and perspectives, which can strengthen their critical-thinking skills. For example, a student who regularly reads academic and literary texts may become more comfortable analyzing unfamiliar ideas. Thus, developing a regular reading habit can improve both language ability and intellectual development.

  • How technology affects education

6. Listening

Definition

Listening is the active process of receiving, interpreting, evaluating and responding to spoken communication.

Listening vs Hearing

HearingListening
Physical ability to perceive sound.Active mental process of understanding spoken messages.
May happen automatically.Requires attention and concentration.
Does not necessarily involve understanding.Involves interpretation, evaluation and response.

Easy distinction: Hearing is receiving sound; listening is actively making meaning from it.

Tips for Effective Listening

  • Pay full attention to the speaker.
  • Maintain appropriate eye contact when culturally and socially suitable.
  • Avoid interrupting unnecessarily.
  • Listen for the main idea and supporting details.
  • Notice tone, emphasis and changes in the speaker's voice.
  • Take brief notes when appropriate.
  • Ask relevant questions for clarification.
  • Paraphrase or summarize important information to confirm understanding.
  • Keep an open mind and avoid judging before the message is complete.

Barriers to Effective Listening

  • Noise and physical distractions
  • Poor concentration
  • Prejudice or preconceived ideas
  • Emotional stress or anger
  • Language and vocabulary difficulties
  • Daydreaming
  • Interrupting the speaker
  • Information overload
  • Focusing on how to reply instead of understanding the speaker

Benefits of Effective Listening

  • Better understanding and learning
  • Fewer misunderstandings
  • Stronger interpersonal relationships
  • Better problem solving
  • Improved academic and professional performance
  • More effective teamwork
  • Greater empathy and awareness of other perspectives

Likely Question + Model Answer

Question: Distinguish between hearing and listening.

Hearing is the physical ability to perceive sound, whereas listening is an active process of paying attention to, interpreting, evaluating and responding to spoken communication. A person may hear sounds without understanding them, but effective listening requires concentration and purposeful processing of the message. Therefore, hearing is mainly a sensory process, while listening is a conscious communicative process.

7. Speaking

Definition

Speaking is the process of communicating ideas, information, feelings and opinions orally through language, supported by pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar, fluency and appropriate non-verbal communication.

Ways to Improve English Speaking Skills

  1. Speak English regularly, even for a few minutes every day.
  2. Learn useful phrases rather than isolated words.
  3. Read aloud to improve pronunciation and rhythm.
  4. Listen to good English speakers and imitate natural expressions.
  5. Record your speech and identify recurring errors.
  6. Practice conversations with classmates or language partners.
  7. Think in English instead of translating every sentence mentally.
  8. Learn vocabulary by topic and use new words in sentences.
  9. Give short talks on familiar topics to build fluency.
  10. Focus on communicating meaning; do not stop after every small mistake.

Public Speaking: Definition

Public speaking is the planned delivery of a spoken message to an audience for a particular purpose, such as informing, persuading, explaining or entertaining.

Public Speaking Techniques

  • Know the audience: adapt vocabulary and examples to them.
  • Organize the speech: introduction → main points → conclusion.
  • Use an attention-getter: question, short story, fact or relevant statement.
  • Maintain eye contact: look at different parts of the audience naturally.
  • Use appropriate gestures: support the message without distracting movement.
  • Control voice: vary pace, volume, stress and intonation.
  • Use pauses: short pauses can emphasize important points.
  • Use visual aids carefully: slides should support rather than replace the speaker.
  • Practice: rehearse timing, pronunciation and transitions.

Public Speaking Guidelines

  1. Choose a clear purpose.
  2. Research and understand the subject.
  3. Prepare a logical outline.
  4. Use simple and precise language.
  5. Begin confidently and end clearly.
  6. Avoid reading the entire speech from paper or slides.
  7. Speak at a moderate pace.
  8. Stand/sit with appropriate posture.
  9. Anticipate possible questions.
  10. Practice before delivery.

Possible Exam Question

Question: Discuss five techniques of effective public speaking.

Effective public speaking requires preparation and purposeful delivery. First, a speaker should organize the speech into a clear introduction, body and conclusion. Second, the speaker should maintain appropriate eye contact to establish connection with the audience. Third, voice modulation through changes in pace, volume and emphasis can make the speech easier to follow. Fourth, suitable gestures and posture can reinforce confidence and meaning. Finally, sufficient rehearsal helps the speaker improve timing, pronunciation and fluency. These techniques together make oral communication clearer and more engaging.

8. Grammar

Collocations

Collocations are words that commonly occur together in a natural and conventional combination.

Correct collocationIncorrect/unusual alternative
make a decisiondo a decision
take a breakdo a break
heavy rainstrong rain
strong argumentpowerful argument* (possible, but different usage)
pay attentiongive attention (possible in some contexts, but less standard here)
deep sleepstrong sleep

Exam tip: collocations are learned as natural word combinations, not simply translated word-by-word.

Comma Splices

A comma splice occurs when two independent clauses are incorrectly joined only with a comma.

Incorrect: The exam was difficult, I studied all night.

Fix it by:

  • Using a full stop: The exam was difficult. I studied all night.
  • Using a semicolon: The exam was difficult; I studied all night.
  • Using a coordinating conjunction: The exam was difficult, so I studied all night.

Run-on Sentences

A run-on sentence occurs when two or more independent clauses are joined incorrectly without proper punctuation or conjunction.

Incorrect: I finished my assignment I submitted it before midnight.
Correct: I finished my assignment, and I submitted it before midnight.

Other fixes: use a full stop or semicolon.

Sentence Fragments

A fragment is an incomplete group of words presented as a sentence. It often lacks a subject, a finite verb, or a complete thought.

Fragment: Because I was tired.
Complete: I went to bed early because I was tired.
Fragment: After the examination.
Complete: We went home after the examination.

Conditionals

TypeStructureUseExample
ZeroIf + present, presentGeneral truths/factsIf you heat ice, it melts.
FirstIf + present, will + base verbReal/possible futureIf I study, I will pass.
SecondIf + past, would + base verbUnreal/hypothetical present/futureIf I had more time, I would travel.
ThirdIf + past perfect, would have + past participleUnreal past situationsIf I had studied, I would have passed.

Mixed conditional (useful extra)

If I had studied harder, I would be more confident now. — past condition with a present result.

Grammar Practice

  1. Correct: “She made a decision, she informed her teacher.”
  2. Correct: “I was late I missed the bus.”
  3. Correct: “Because the weather was bad.”
  4. Complete: “If I study regularly, ______.”
  5. Complete: “If I were the class representative, ______.”
  6. Complete: “If he had left earlier, ______.”
  7. Choose: “make/do a mistake.”
  8. Choose: “heavy/strong rain.”
Answers

1. She made a decision, and she informed her teacher. / She made a decision; she informed her teacher. 2. I was late, so I missed the bus. 3. I stayed home because the weather was bad. 4. will + base verb. 5. would + base verb. 6. would have + past participle. 7. make a mistake. 8. heavy rain.

9. Application & CV Writing

Formal Application / Letter: Academic Format

  1. Date
  2. Recipient's designation and institution/organization
  3. Subject — short and specific
  4. Salutation — e.g. “Dear Sir/Madam,” or the appropriate formal form required by your course.
  5. Opening: state why you are writing.
  6. Body: provide necessary details and justification.
  7. Request/action: clearly state what you want the recipient to do.
  8. Closing: polite closing sentence.
  9. Formal sign-off and name/ID if required.

Writing Under Exam Pressure

  1. Read the question twice. Identify exactly what it asks.
  2. Spend a minute planning. Write 3–4 keywords for your main points.
  3. Use a visible structure. Introduction/body/conclusion for essays; topic/support/conclusion for paragraphs.
  4. Prefer clear English over unnecessarily complicated English.
  5. Use transitions. They make your logic easier to follow.
  6. Leave time to edit. Check subject–verb agreement, tense, spelling, articles, punctuation and sentence boundaries.
Golden rule: A simple, correct, logically organized answer is usually stronger than an impressive-looking answer full of grammar errors.
