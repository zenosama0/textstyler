1. Nature of Ethics

Quiz 1 + likely 5 marks

Ethics is the philosophical study of morality: it examines what is right or wrong, good or bad, virtuous or vicious, and why people ought to act in particular ways.

Main characteristics

  1. Normative: ethics asks what ought to be done, not merely what people do.
  2. Rational and critical: ethical claims are examined through reasons and arguments.
  3. Concerned with values: it studies good, right, duty, virtue, justice and responsibility.
  4. Universalizing tendency: ethical theories seek principles that can apply beyond one isolated case.
  5. Practical: ethics guides decisions and conduct in personal and social life.
  6. Reflective: it questions moral assumptions rather than simply accepting custom.

Branches

Meta-ethics

Meaning, truth and objectivity of moral judgments.

Normative ethics

Theories of right action and the good life.

Applied ethics

Application to real issues such as punishment, medicine, technology and business.

Ethics vs psychology

EthicsPsychology
Normative: asks what people ought to do.Empirical/descriptive: asks how people actually think, feel and behave.
Uses philosophical reasoning and evaluation.Uses observation, experiments, surveys and psychological theory.
Judges conduct as right/wrong or good/bad.Explains causes of behavior, cognition, emotion and development.
Concerned with justification of moral standards.Concerned with psychological facts about moral behavior and decision-making.
Connection: psychology supplies facts about motivation and behavior; ethics evaluates what those facts mean for moral responsibility and good conduct.
Model 5-mark answer: Explain the nature of ethics.
Ethics is the branch of philosophy that studies morality and asks what is right or wrong, good or bad, and what human beings ought to do. It is normative because it evaluates conduct rather than merely describing it. Ethics is rational and critical, since moral claims should be supported by reasons. It deals with values such as duty, justice, virtue and responsibility and applies them to real-life decisions. Ethics may be divided into meta-ethics, normative ethics and applied ethics. Its purpose is to provide rational guidance for responsible human conduct.
Model 5-mark answer: Explain the difference between ethics and psychology.
Psychology is mainly an empirical study of human behavior, mental processes, emotion and motivation, while ethics is the philosophical and normative study of what people ought to do. Psychology asks questions such as why a person lies; ethics asks whether lying is morally wrong and under what conditions. Psychology relies on evidence and observation, whereas ethics relies primarily on conceptual analysis and moral reasoning. They are connected because psychological facts about motivation, development and decision-making help ethics understand human moral agency.

2. Ethics & Other Disciplines

Short questions

Ethics and sociology

Sociology studies society, institutions, groups, norms and patterns of social behavior. Ethics asks whether such norms are morally justified. Sociology can explain why a community accepts a norm; ethics asks whether that norm ought to be accepted.

Exam line: Sociology is descriptive about social morality; ethics is evaluative and normative about moral standards. Social facts inform ethical reasoning but do not automatically prove that a practice is morally right.

Ethics and psychology

Psychology studies motivation, emotion, cognition, development and personality. Ethics studies the moral quality and justification of action. Psychology therefore helps explain moral behavior; ethics evaluates it.

Exam line: “Psychology explains the moral actor; ethics evaluates the moral action and its justification.”

Ethics and philosophy

Ethics is a branch of philosophy. Philosophy supplies critical reasoning, conceptual clarification, argument, consistency and evaluation. Studying philosophy prevents moral thinking from becoming mere custom or unsupported authority.

Why study ethics in current society?

  • Modern technology creates decisions affecting huge numbers of people.
  • Pluralistic societies contain competing values that require reasoned dialogue.
  • AI, privacy, misinformation, biotechnology, climate responsibility and digital behavior need normative analysis.
  • Professional and political decisions involve conflicts among rights, welfare, fairness and freedom.
  • Ethics helps distinguish social acceptance from moral justification.
Model 5-mark answer: Connection between ethics and sociology.
Ethics and sociology both study human social life, but they do so differently. Sociology is mainly descriptive: it studies institutions, norms, customs, groups and patterns of behavior. Ethics is normative: it asks whether those norms and behaviors are right or wrong and whether they can be justified. Sociology can show why a society accepts a practice, but it cannot by itself establish that the practice is morally justified. Thus sociology supplies social facts, while ethics critically evaluates their moral significance.
Model 5-mark answer: Connection between ethics and psychology.
Psychology and ethics are connected through the study of human action. Psychology explains motivation, emotion, cognition, personality and moral development, while ethics evaluates whether actions and motives are morally good, right or wrong. Psychological facts can help explain why people act selfishly, altruistically or under pressure, but factual explanation alone does not determine moral duty. Ethics uses psychological knowledge as an input while retaining its normative role.

3. Plato's Four Cardinal Virtues

5 marks

1. Wisdom

Knowing what is genuinely good and how life should be ordered.

2. Courage

Firmness in preserving correct beliefs about what should be feared.

3. Temperance

Self-control and harmony among desires, emotion and reason.

4. Justice

Each part of the soul and each class of the city performs its proper function in harmony.

Easy memory: W–C–T–J

Wisdom = know the good. Courage = stand by the good. Temperance = control yourself. Justice = put everything in proper order.

Model 5-mark answer
Plato's four cardinal virtues are wisdom, courage, temperance and justice. Wisdom is knowledge of what is truly good. Courage is steadfastness in preserving correct moral convictions in the face of fear. Temperance is self-control and harmony among the different desires of the person. Justice is the condition in which each part of the soul and each class of the city performs its proper function without unjust interference. Together these virtues produce an orderly and morally excellent individual and society.

4. Goodwill & Perfectionism

Short questions + Kant

Goodwill

For Kant, a good will is good not because of what it achieves but because of the moral principle from which it acts. Talents, intelligence, courage and success can be used badly; a good will is morally valuable because it chooses duty for duty's sake.

Key distinction: acting in accordance with duty is not always the same as acting from duty. Moral worth is clearest when the motive is duty rather than self-interest, fear, reward or inclination.

Perfectionism

Perfectionism in ethics treats the development or realization of important human capacities and excellences as central to the good life.

PerfectionismHedonismUtilitarianism
Good = human flourishing, excellence or realization of capacities.Good = pleasure; bad = pain.Right action maximizes overall welfare/happiness.
Values character and development.Focuses on desirable experience.Focuses on consequences for everyone affected.
Model 5-mark answer: What is goodwill?
Goodwill is Kant's idea of a morally good will: a will that acts from respect for moral duty rather than merely from inclination, self-interest or expected results. A good will is intrinsically good because its moral worth depends on the principle and motive of action, not on whether the action succeeds. Thus a person who helps another because it is morally right displays clearer moral worth than someone who acts only for praise or profit.
Model 5-mark answer: Explain perfectionism.
Perfectionism is the ethical view that a good life consists partly in developing and realizing important human capacities and excellences. It values virtues, knowledge, rationality, character and human flourishing rather than reducing morality to pleasure or simple rule-following. Education and character development matter because they help a person become a better human being.

5. Aristotle's Virtue Ethics

Very high priority

Aristotle's ethics is primarily a theory of character. The goal of human life is eudaimonia, usually translated as flourishing, living well or human fulfillment. Flourishing is achieved through rational activity in accordance with virtue over a complete life.

Core properties

  1. Teleological: actions are understood in relation to ends, with eudaimonia as the highest human end.
  2. Virtue-centered: focuses on becoming a good person, not only following rules.
  3. Habitual: moral virtues are developed through repeated practice.
  4. Social: humans are social/political beings; flourishing needs community.
  5. Rational: good action requires practical wisdom (phronesis).
  6. Context-sensitive: the right response depends on circumstances.

Two kinds of virtue

Moral / ethical virtues

Courage, temperance, generosity, truthfulness and other character excellences, developed by habituation.

Intellectual virtues

Excellences of reason, including practical and theoretical wisdom, developed through teaching and intellectual activity.

The Golden Mean

Virtue is generally a mean between extremes of excess and deficiency, relative to us and determined by reason and practical wisdom. The mean is not an arithmetic midpoint, and not every action has a mean.

DeficiencyVirtue / MeanExcess
CowardiceCourageRashness
InsensibilityTemperanceSelf-indulgence
StinginessGenerosityWastefulness
UnderstatementTruthfulnessBoastfulness
Excessive anger / apathyGood temperIrascibility

“Certain virtue ethics”

When the question says “certain virtue ethics,” treat it as particular virtues. Explain 4–6 examples using the deficiency–mean–excess structure and then show that practical wisdom identifies the appropriate mean.

Model 12.5-mark answer

Introduction: Aristotle's virtue ethics asks not simply “What rule should I follow?” but “What kind of person should I become?” Its highest goal is eudaimonia, a life of flourishing achieved through rational activity in accordance with virtue.

Character and habit: Ethical character is formed by habit. We become just by repeatedly doing just actions and courageous by repeatedly practicing courage. The moral person therefore learns both what is good and how to desire and choose it.

Virtue: Aristotle distinguishes intellectual and moral virtues. Intellectual virtues grow through teaching and reasoning; moral virtues develop through habituation. Practical wisdom (phronesis) enables a person to judge what the situation requires.

Golden Mean: Moral virtue is ordinarily a mean between excess and deficiency relative to the person and circumstance. Courage is between cowardice and rashness; temperance between insensibility and self-indulgence; generosity between stinginess and wastefulness. The mean is not mediocrity: it is the right response at the right time for the right reason.

Evaluation: Aristotle explains moral development, habit and context well. A challenge is that finding the correct mean can be difficult and may depend on judgment and social assumptions.

Conclusion: Aristotle connects morality with character, habit, practical wisdom and flourishing. Virtue + habit + practical wisdom + right mean → eudaimonia.

6. Free Will & Ethics

Long question

Free will is the capacity of an agent to make choices that are genuinely attributable to the agent. Ethics connects free will to responsibility: praise, blame, reward and punishment seem justified only when a person had meaningful control.

Main positions

PositionClaimEthical implication
LibertarianismSome choices are genuinely free and not fully determined by prior causes.Strong basis for moral responsibility.
Hard determinismEvery event, including choice, is determined by prior causes.Traditional responsibility is threatened; punishment may need non-retributive justification.
CompatibilismFreedom can exist with causation if action flows from the agent's reasons/desires without coercion.Responsibility can survive determinism.

Arguments supporting free will

  • Deliberation: people experience themselves as choosing between options.
  • Moral responsibility: praise and blame assume meaningful agency.
  • Rational deliberation: giving reasons presupposes some control over action.
  • Choice vs coercion: we distinguish what we choose from what we are forced to do.

Challenges

  • Choices are influenced by biology, upbringing, social conditions and prior mental states.
  • Deterministic explanations appear to leave no ultimate alternative possibilities.
  • If a choice is completely uncaused, it can seem arbitrary rather than genuinely controlled.
Model 12.5-mark answer

Free will is the capacity of a person to make choices that are genuinely attributable to the person. It is central to ethics because moral responsibility normally requires control: we praise, blame or punish people because their actions are thought to be meaningfully their own.

Libertarianism argues that some choices are not determined by prior causes, protecting strong responsibility. Hard determinism argues that choices are fully determined by preceding causes and therefore challenges traditional responsibility. Compatibilism argues that freedom does not require absence of causation; a person can be free when acting according to their own reasons and desires without external coercion.

Support for free will comes from deliberation, the experience of choice, the practice of holding people responsible and the distinction between voluntary and coerced actions. Critics point to biological, psychological and social determinants. A major difficulty is that a completely determined choice may not seem free, while a completely uncaused choice may seem random.

Conclusion: free will remains ethically important because responsibility requires some meaningful form of agency. Compatibilism offers a powerful middle position by defining freedom in terms of rational self-direction rather than absolute independence from causality.

7. Kant: Categorical & Hypothetical Imperatives

Very high priority

Hypothetical imperative

A hypothetical imperative commands conditionally: “If you want X, do Y.” It depends on a particular goal or desire.

Categorical imperative

A categorical imperative commands unconditionally. It tells a rational agent what to do regardless of personal desire or desired outcome. Moral obligation is categorical because morality cannot depend on what someone happens to want.

The three main formulas

1. Universal Law

Act only on a maxim that you could will as a universal law.

2. Humanity as an End

Treat humanity, in yourself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means. Respect rational persons.

3. Kingdom of Ends / Autonomy

Act as a rational lawgiver in a community of ends, choosing principles fit for a universal moral order.

Kant and free will

Autonomy means rational self-legislation. Freedom is not doing whatever one desires; it is acting according to rational moral law rather than being ruled by impulse or external coercion. Freedom and morality are therefore intimately connected.

HypotheticalCategorical
FormIf you want X, do Y.Do Y.
ConditionDepends on a goal/desire.No condition.
TypeGoal-dependent practical reason.Unconditional moral obligation.
Model 12.5-mark answer

A hypothetical imperative is conditional: it directs a person to take a means to an end they already want. A categorical imperative is unconditional: it binds a rational agent regardless of personal desires or goals. Therefore moral duties, for Kant, must be categorical.

The first formula is the Universal Law: act only on a maxim that you can will as universal law. The second is the Humanity formula: treat humanity in oneself and others always as an end and never merely as a means. The third is the Kingdom of Ends: act as if you were a rational legislator in a universal community of ends.

Freedom: Kant connects morality and freedom through autonomy. A free moral agent is not simply one who follows desires; freedom is rational self-legislation. Because morality requires autonomous obedience to universal law, freedom is closely connected to moral responsibility.

Conclusion: Kant's ethics is deontological because moral worth depends on duty and the principle of action rather than consequences.

8. Ethical Dilemmas

Long question

An ethical dilemma occurs when a decision-maker faces two or more morally significant obligations or values that conflict, so satisfying one appears to require violating another.

Nature

  • There is a genuine conflict between significant moral considerations.
  • The decision-maker faces competing duties, values or harms.
  • All options may carry moral cost.
  • The case requires judgment, not merely factual information.

Contemporary examples

Technology

Privacy vs security; AI efficiency vs fairness; AI assistance vs academic integrity.

Medicine

Patient autonomy vs beneficence; scarce resources; end-of-life decisions.

Business

Profit vs worker safety; confidentiality vs public interest; whistleblowing vs loyalty.

Society

Freedom of expression vs harm; liberty vs public safety; development vs future generations.

Reasons dilemmas arise

Competing principles, limited resources, uncertainty, pluralism, conflicting roles, institutional pressure, incomplete information and unequal distribution of benefits and harms.

Prevention / management

  1. Identify stakeholders and competing values.
  2. Separate facts from assumptions.
  3. List realistic alternatives and consequences.
  4. Apply relevant moral principles consistently.
  5. Check rights, fairness, proportionality and reversibility.
  6. Document reasons and consult appropriate ethical/legal standards.
Model 12.5-mark answer

An ethical dilemma occurs when a decision-maker faces competing moral obligations or values and cannot satisfy all of them without moral loss. It is different from an ordinary difficult decision because the conflict is specifically normative.

Contemporary society generates many dilemmas. Digital technology creates tensions between privacy and security and between AI efficiency and fairness. Medicine creates conflicts involving autonomy, beneficence and limited resources. Business raises conflicts between profit and employee welfare, while public life can involve freedom of expression versus prevention of serious harm.

Dilemmas arise because values conflict, resources are limited, information is incomplete, institutions create pressure and modern pluralistic societies contain people with different moral priorities. Prevention requires ethical education, clear policies, transparency, stakeholder analysis and consistent decision procedures.

When a dilemma cannot be eliminated, it should be managed by identifying the strongest duties, considering consequences, respecting rights and treating persons fairly. Ethical maturity does not mean finding a cost-free solution; it means making a reasoned, transparent and defensible choice while minimizing avoidable harm.

9. Crime & Punishment

Quiz 4 + long question

Crime and punishment can clearly be discussed through ethics because punishment involves moral questions about responsibility, justice, proportionality, rights, deterrence, rehabilitation and state authority.

Major doctrines of punishment

Retributive

Punishment is justified because an offender deserves proportionate suffering for wrongdoing. Focus: desert and justice for the past.

Deterrence

Punishment is justified because it discourages future crime: specifically for the offender or generally for society.

Rehabilitation

Punishment should reform the offender through education, treatment, skills and reintegration.

Incapacitation

Restraining offenders protects society by limiting their ability to cause further harm.

Restorative justice

Focuses on repairing harm to victims and communities through accountability, restitution and reconciliation.

Death penalty — arguments

Argument forArgument against
Severe retribution for exceptionally grave crimes.Irreversible punishment makes wrongful convictions uniquely catastrophic.
Possible deterrent effect.Deterrence claims do not automatically justify taking life.
Permanent incapacitation.Life imprisonment can incapacitate without execution.

Personal-position structure

State your position → recognize the strongest opposing argument → answer it → apply retribution/deterrence/rehabilitation/incapacitation → conclude.

Model 12.5-mark answer: Crime, punishment and death penalty

Yes. Crime and punishment are ethical issues because punishment deliberately imposes a burden on a person and therefore requires justification. Ethics asks whether punishment is deserved, proportionate, effective, fair and respectful of human dignity.

Retribution says offenders deserve proportionate punishment because they committed wrongdoing. Deterrence justifies punishment by its ability to prevent future crime. Rehabilitation aims to reform the offender, incapacitation protects society by restricting dangerous people, and restorative justice focuses on repairing harm.

The death penalty can be defended on retributive and incapacitation grounds and sometimes on deterrence grounds. However, it is irreversible, so wrongful conviction creates an extraordinary moral problem. It also raises questions about equality, dignity, proportionality and whether the state should intentionally take life when permanent imprisonment can protect society.

Position: A strong ethical argument is to oppose the death penalty because the possibility of irreversible error, unequal application and loss of rehabilitation opportunities outweighs its claimed benefits where secure alternatives exist. This does not minimize victims or serious crime; it supports accountability without an irreversible sanction.

Conclusion: punishment is ethically legitimate only when its purpose and limits can be justified. A morally serious system should combine accountability, protection, fairness and rehabilitation.

10. Hedonism & Utilitarianism

Quiz 3 + long question

What is hedonism?

Hedonism holds that pleasure is fundamentally good and pain fundamentally bad, or that the value of life is substantially determined by pleasure and freedom from suffering.

Characteristics

  • Pleasure/pain is the central standard of value.
  • Choices are explained through desirable experience or avoidance of suffering.
  • It can be individual or universal in scope.
  • It raises questions about quantity, quality and distribution of pleasure.
Egoistic HedonismUniversalistic / Altruistic Hedonism
My own pleasure is the standard.The happiness/pleasure of everyone affected is the standard.
Focuses on self-interest.Requires impartial consideration of others.
Can justify self-serving actions.Can require personal sacrifice for greater total happiness.

What kind of hedonism is utilitarianism?

Classical utilitarianism is generally a form of universalistic hedonism: actions are judged by how much happiness/pleasure and how little pain they produce for everyone affected, rather than the agent alone.

Bentham vs Mill

Jeremy BenthamJ. S. Mill
ApproachUtility = maximize pleasure and minimize pain.Also maximizes happiness, but distinguishes higher and lower pleasures.
QualityPrimarily quantitative.Qualitative distinction: intellectual/moral pleasures can be higher.
MethodHedonic calculus: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent.Competent judges help compare higher and lower pleasures.

Exam verdict: Mill is easier to defend as more modern

He keeps welfare maximization but recognizes qualitative human goods and gives more room to individuality, liberty and development. Bentham's quantitative clarity remains a major strength.

Model 12.5-mark answer

Hedonism treats pleasure as intrinsically good and pain as intrinsically bad. Egoistic hedonism makes the individual's own pleasure the central standard, while universalistic or altruistic hedonism extends concern to the happiness of everyone affected.

Utilitarianism is a form of universalistic hedonism in its classical formulation because an action is morally right when it tends to maximize overall happiness and minimize suffering, considering all affected.

Bentham develops a quantitative approach. Pleasures and pains can be compared using factors such as intensity, duration, certainty and extent. Mill preserves the greatest-happiness principle but adds a qualitative distinction: some pleasures are higher because they engage intellectual and moral capacities. Competent judges who have experienced both kinds are evidence for these distinctions.

Bentham's strength is clarity and impartial aggregation; Mill's strength is a richer account of human well-being. Bentham may seem too reductionist if all pleasure is treated primarily by quantity. Mill is harder to measure because quality is less precise.

Conclusion: classical utilitarianism is best understood as universalistic hedonism, and Mill can be defended as more modern because he combines welfare maximization with qualitative development and liberty.

11. Standards of Morality

Long question

A standard of morality is a principle or criterion used to determine whether conduct is morally right, wrong, good or bad.

Major standards

Divine command / law

Moral obligations are grounded in God's will, revelation, law or authority.

Consequences

Actions are judged by outcomes, especially welfare or happiness in utilitarianism.

Duty / reason

Kant evaluates maxims through rational universalization and respect for persons.

Virtue

Judges moral life by character traits and human flourishing.

Intuition

Some moral truths are immediately or non-inferentially apprehended.

Divine command / divine law

Strengths: gives morality a strong authority, provides guidance, and connects moral duty with ultimate reality.

Challenges: disagreement over revelation and interpretation; question of whether morality depends on command or whether God commands what is independently good.

Divine law vs hedonism/utilitarianism

Divine law grounds morality in divine authority, hedonism in pleasure/pain, and utilitarianism in overall consequences for welfare. A divine-law theory can prohibit an act despite greater pleasure; utilitarianism can permit it if it produces better total consequences.

Model 12.5-mark answer

A standard of morality is a criterion by which moral judgments are made. Major standards include virtue, duty, consequences, divine law and intuitionism.

Divine law/command: morality is grounded in God's will, revelation or law. It offers authority and an objective framework but faces interpretation problems and the Euthyphro challenge.

Hedonism and utilitarianism: hedonism treats pleasure as good and pain as bad. Classical utilitarianism develops a universalistic form by judging actions according to overall happiness or welfare. Its advantage is impartial concern for all affected; its difficulty is measurement and the possible sacrifice of individuals for aggregate benefit.

Kantian duty: morality is grounded in rational obligation and universal law. Virtue ethics: morality concerns good character and flourishing. Intuitionism: some moral truths can be apprehended directly.

Evaluation: no single standard solves every moral problem without difficulty. Divine law emphasizes authority, Kant rational duty, utilitarianism consequences, virtue ethics character and intuitionism direct moral knowledge. Ethical reasoning improves when assumptions and limits of each standard are made explicit.

12. Euthyphro Dilemma

Long question

Plato presents a famous dilemma in the Euthyphro: Is something morally good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is morally good?

Horn 1

If something is good because God commands it, morality may appear arbitrary: God could command something else.

Horn 2

If God commands something because it is already good, goodness appears independent of God's command.

Possible solutions

  1. Divine nature theory: goodness is grounded not in arbitrary commands but in God's necessarily good nature.
  2. Modified divine command: moral law is linked to God's stable character and wisdom rather than bare arbitrary commands.
  3. Independent moral realism: accept that some moral truths exist independently of divine command.
  4. Reject the assumptions: argue that “goodness” and God's nature are not two independent standards.
Model 12.5-mark answer

The Euthyphro dilemma challenges a simple divine-command theory of morality. The question is whether something is good because God commands it or whether God commands it because it is already good.

If the first answer is accepted, morality seems arbitrary: God's command alone would make an act good. If the second answer is accepted, goodness seems logically prior to God's command, suggesting morality is independent of God.

Several responses are possible. One influential solution is the divine nature theory: moral goodness is grounded in God's necessarily good nature, so God's commands are neither arbitrary nor dependent on an external moral standard. Another approach accepts independent moral truths and treats God as their knower or guarantor.

Conclusion: the dilemma forces a theory of divine morality to explain how divine authority, rational moral truth and non-arbitrariness fit together. A strong theistic response grounds morality in God's necessary and perfectly good nature rather than arbitrary command.

13. Intuitionism as a Standard of Morality

Long question

Ethical intuitionism holds that some moral truths or properties are known directly or non-inferentially rather than being derived entirely from other moral premises. Important intuitionists include G. E. Moore, H. A. Prichard and W. D. Ross, though their versions differ.

Main ideas

  • Some moral propositions are basic rather than reducible to non-moral facts.
  • Moral knowledge can involve direct rational apprehension.
  • Ross emphasizes prima facie duties such as fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence and self-improvement.
  • A prima facie duty is a genuine moral reason unless outweighed by another duty in the case.

Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths

Respects ordinary moral experience, allows multiple moral reasons, and avoids reducing everything to pleasure or a single rule.

Weaknesses

People can have conflicting intuitions, and intuitions may be influenced by culture, upbringing or prejudice.

Model 12.5-mark answer

Ethical intuitionism holds that some moral truths can be known directly or non-inferentially. Instead of deriving every obligation from pleasure, divine command or one universal rule, intuitionism argues that basic moral principles can be apprehended by moral reason.

G. E. Moore treats “good” as a fundamental property, while H. A. Prichard emphasizes direct apprehension of obligation. W. D. Ross develops a pluralistic theory of prima facie duties, including fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence and self-improvement.

The strength of intuitionism is that moral life contains several genuine reasons at once. Its weakness is epistemological: people can disagree about their intuitions, and intuitions may reflect social conditioning rather than objective moral insight.

Conclusion: intuitionism is useful as a pluralistic account of moral knowledge, especially where several duties conflict, but it must explain how reliable moral intuitions are distinguished from mere feelings or prejudice.
