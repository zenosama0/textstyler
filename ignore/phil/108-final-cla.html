01 Reading

Definition

Memorise

Reading is an active cognitive process of constructing meaning from written text. It involves decoding written symbols, connecting to prior knowledge, and understanding the author's intended message.

Components of Reading

  • Decoding - recognising and sounding out written words
  • Fluency - reading smoothly, accurately, and at an appropriate pace
  • Vocabulary - knowing the meaning of encountered words
  • Comprehension - understanding the full meaning of the text
  • Background Knowledge - connecting the text to what the reader already knows

Strategies

  • Previewing - looking at headings, titles, and images before reading
  • Predicting - forming expectations about content before reading
  • Skimming - reading quickly to grasp the main idea
  • Scanning - moving quickly to find specific information
  • Re-reading - returning to difficult passages for clarification
  • Inferencing - drawing conclusions not directly stated in the text
  • Summarising - restating main points in one's own words

Reading Materials

  • Textbooks and academic journals
  • Newspapers and magazines
  • Novels and short stories
  • Online articles and blogs
  • Reports and manuals
  • Letters and emails

Types of Reading

TypePurposeMethod
IntensiveDeep, detailed understandingSlow, careful, line-by-line
ExtensiveGeneral comprehension and pleasureFast, relaxed, large amounts
SkimmingGet the overall main idea quicklyRead titles, first/last sentences, headings
ScanningLocate specific informationMove eyes rapidly to find target words
CriticalEvaluate, question, and analyse contentActive questioning and judgement
RecreationalEnjoyment and personal interestSelf-paced, relaxed reading for pleasure

Techniques Used by Good Readers

  • Make predictions before and during reading
  • Connect the text to prior knowledge and personal experience
  • Ask questions: who, what, when, where, why, and how
  • Visualise scenes, events, and described concepts
  • Monitor their own comprehension as they read (self-check)
  • Look up unfamiliar words and note them
  • Adjust reading speed to match the difficulty of the text
  • Summarise sections after reading them

Practice Questions

Exam Question 1 - Short Answer

Q: Explain the SQ3R reading strategy and state the purpose of each step.

Sample Answer

SQ3R is a five-step reading strategy designed to improve comprehension and retention of written material. The first step, Survey, involves previewing titles, headings, and the introduction to get an overview. The second step, Question, requires the reader to convert headings into questions that guide focused reading. The third step, Read, means reading the text actively to answer those questions. The fourth step, Recite, involves recalling and stating the answers from memory after each section, without looking at the text. The final step, Review, requires going back over the material to verify and reinforce understanding.

Exam Question 2 - Short Answer

Q: Distinguish between skimming and scanning. Give one use for each.

Sample Answer

Skimming is a technique used to read quickly and identify the main idea or general content of a text without reading every word. A reader might skim a newspaper article to decide whether it is worth reading in full. Scanning, by contrast, is used to locate specific information within a text, such as a date, name, or statistic, by moving the eyes rapidly over the page. For example, a reader might scan a phone directory to find a particular number. The key difference is that skimming seeks an overview, while scanning seeks a specific detail.

02 Writing

Definition

Memorise

Writing is the process of using written symbols - letters, words, and punctuation - to communicate thoughts, ideas, and information in a structured and coherent form for a specific audience and purpose.

Purposes of Writing

  • To inform - present facts, data, and information clearly
  • To persuade - convince the reader to accept a viewpoint or take action
  • To entertain - engage the reader's emotions and imagination
  • To describe - create a vivid picture of a person, place, or thing
  • To explore - examine and develop ideas and questions
  • To express - share personal thoughts, feelings, and experiences

Components of Writing

Content

The ideas, information, and message the writer communicates.

Organisation

The logical structure and arrangement of ideas from beginning to end.

Style / Voice

The writer's individual tone, word choice, and manner of expression.

Mechanics

Correct grammar, spelling, punctuation, and capitalisation.

Types of Writing

TypePurposeExample Form
NarrativeTell a story with a sequence of eventsShort story, autobiography, memoir
DescriptiveDescribe vividly using sensory detailCharacter sketch, travel writing
ExpositoryExplain or inform about a topic objectivelyNews article, how-to guide, report
Persuasive/ArgumentativeConvince the reader to accept a positionEditorial, debate speech, essay
TechnicalConvey specialised or professional informationManual, lab report, business document
CreativeExpress imagination and original ideasPoetry, fiction, screenplay

The Writing Process

Memorise - 5 Stages in Order
  1. Pre-writing - brainstorm, research, outline, and plan before writing
  2. Drafting - write the first version; get ideas down without stopping to perfect them
  3. Revising - improve content, organisation, clarity, and development of ideas
  4. Editing - correct grammar, spelling, punctuation, and sentence structure
  5. Publishing/Presenting - share, submit, or present the final polished piece
Exam Question

Q: Describe the five stages of the writing process.

Sample Answer

The writing process consists of five stages. The first is pre-writing, which involves generating ideas through brainstorming, conducting research, and creating an outline before writing begins. The second stage is drafting, where the writer produces a first version by translating ideas into sentences and paragraphs without pausing to correct mistakes. The third stage, revising, focuses on improving the substance, structure, and flow of the piece. The fourth stage is editing, during which the writer corrects grammatical, spelling, and mechanical errors. The final stage is publishing or presenting, which involves sharing or submitting the completed, polished piece to its intended audience.

03 Summary Writing

Definition & Rules

Memorise - Definition

A summary is a concise, shortened version of a longer text that captures only the main ideas in the reader's own words. It is objective, written in the third person and present tense, and significantly shorter than the original.

Rules for Summary Writing

  • Read the original text carefully at least twice before writing
  • Identify and keep only the main ideas - remove examples, repetition, and minor details
  • Write entirely in your own words - no copying of sentences
  • Maintain objectivity - no personal opinions or reactions
  • Write in the third person and present tense
  • Aim for approximately one-third of the original length
  • Write as a single coherent paragraph unless stated otherwise
  • Do not add information that is not in the original text

Format Template

Academic Format - How to Begin & Structure a Summary

The [article/passage/text] [discusses/argues/examines/explains] that [main idea].
[The author/text] further states that [supporting idea 1].
Additionally, [supporting idea 2].
In conclusion, [the text's final point or recommendation].

Practice Question

Exam Question

Q: Read the following passage and write a summary in no more than 80 words.

"Mobile phones have transformed modern communication. They allow instant contact across continents, provide access to the internet, and serve as cameras, calendars, and entertainment devices. However, excessive phone use raises concerns about mental health, attention span, and social isolation. Studies show that students who spend more than four hours daily on phones perform worse academically. Despite these risks, phones remain indispensable tools in education and work environments worldwide."
Sample Summary (52 words)

The passage discusses how mobile phones have greatly enhanced communication by providing internet access and multiple practical functions. However, it notes that excessive use is linked to mental health concerns and poorer academic performance in students. Despite these drawbacks, the text concludes that mobile phones continue to be essential tools in both education and professional life.

04 Essay

Definition

Memorise

An essay is a piece of extended non-fiction writing that presents an argument, analysis, or discussion on a specific topic, developed through structured paragraphs and supported by evidence and reasoning.

Types of Essay

TypePurpose
Argumentative / PersuasiveConvince the reader to accept a specific position using logic and evidence
ExpositoryExplain, inform, or clarify a topic in an objective, neutral way
DescriptiveCreate a vivid picture of a person, place, object, or experience through sensory language
NarrativeTell a personal or fictional story with a clear sequence of events
AnalyticalBreak down and examine a text, event, or concept to explain how and why it works
Compare and ContrastExamine the similarities and differences between two or more subjects
Cause and EffectExplore the reasons something occurred and the consequences that followed

Parts of an Essay

Academic Format - Essay Structure (Follow This)
Introduction

Hook sentence - Background information - Thesis Statement (last sentence of intro)

Body Paragraph 1

Topic Sentence - Evidence / Explanation - Analysis - Transition to next point

Body Paragraph 2

Topic Sentence - Evidence / Explanation - Analysis - Transition to next point

Body Paragraph 3 (optional - for counterargument)

Counterargument - Rebuttal with evidence

Conclusion

Restate thesis differently - Summarise main points - Closing thought or call to action

Thesis Statement

Memorise - Definition

A thesis statement is a single, clear sentence placed at the end of the introduction that states the essay's main argument or central claim. It tells the reader what position will be argued and why.

Characteristics of a Strong Thesis

  • Makes a specific, arguable claim - not a fact
  • Appears at the end of the introduction paragraph
  • Is one to two sentences long
  • Signals the essay's direction and scope
  • Can be disagreed with - it takes a position
Weak Thesis - Avoid

"Social media is used by many people around the world."
(This is just a fact - not arguable)

Strong Thesis - Model

"Although social media connects people globally, its unrestricted use among students leads to reduced academic performance and heightened anxiety."

Practice Essay Question

Exam Question - Argumentative Essay

Q: "Social media has more negative effects than positive effects on students." Write an argumentative essay discussing this statement.

Model Essay (Annotated - Study the Structure)
Introduction

In the digital age, platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and Facebook have become deeply embedded in student life. While these tools offer convenience and connectivity, their unrestricted use carries serious consequences. [Thesis:] Social media, when used without moderation, poses greater harm than benefit to students by undermining academic performance, damaging mental health, and eroding real-world social skills.

Body Paragraph 1 - Academic Performance

[Topic sentence:] One of the most significant negative effects of social media is its detrimental impact on students' academic performance. Constant notifications and the compulsion to check updates distract students during study time, fragmenting concentration. Research indicates that students who use social media for more than four hours daily score significantly lower on examinations than peers who limit their usage. This demonstrates that time invested in scrolling could otherwise be directed toward productive learning.

Body Paragraph 2 - Mental Health

[Topic sentence:] Furthermore, social media contributes to growing mental health challenges among students. The culture of comparison fostered by image-heavy platforms leads to low self-esteem, anxiety, and feelings of inadequacy. Students who measure their worth against the curated highlights of others' lives frequently report depression and social withdrawal. Clearly, this psychological toll represents a cost that far outweighs the convenience social media offers.

Body Paragraph 3 - Counterargument and Rebuttal

[Counterargument:] Proponents of social media argue that it facilitates collaborative learning, global communication, and access to educational resources. [Rebuttal:] However, these benefits can be achieved through structured, intentional use and are quickly negated when platforms are accessed recreationally without discipline or time limits.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while social media holds some value as a communication and learning tool, its negative effects on academic achievement, mental well-being, and social development are far more substantial. Students, educators, and parents must collaborate to regulate social media use, ensuring it serves as a resource rather than a distraction. Only through mindful, disciplined engagement can students benefit from social media without suffering its consequences.

05 Paragraph

Definition

Memorise

A paragraph is a group of related sentences that develop one central idea. It is the basic unit of extended writing and serves as the building block of essays, reports, and other texts.

Parts of a Paragraph

Academic Format - Paragraph Structure (Follow This)
1. Topic Sentence

The first sentence. States the one main idea of the paragraph clearly and specifically. Never begins with "I" in formal academic writing. Should not be too broad or too narrow.

2. Supporting Sentences

The body of the paragraph. Provides facts, examples, explanations, definitions, or analysis that develops and proves the topic sentence. Usually 3-6 sentences. Use transition words to connect ideas.

3. Concluding Sentence

The final sentence. Restates the main idea in different words OR provides a closing thought that reinforces the paragraph's point. Signals to the reader that the idea is complete.

5-Step Process to Paragraph Development

Memorise - All 5 Steps in Order
  1. Choose a topic - select the broad subject you will write about
  2. Narrow the topic - focus on one specific aspect of that subject
  3. Write a topic sentence - compose one clear sentence stating the main idea
  4. Add supporting details - develop the idea with evidence, examples, and explanation
  5. Write a concluding sentence - wrap up the paragraph by restating the main idea differently

Practice Question

Exam Question

Q: Write a well-developed paragraph about the importance of reading.

Model Paragraph (Annotated)
Topic Sentence

Reading is one of the most valuable habits a person can develop for intellectual and personal growth.

Supporting Sentences

Through consistent reading, individuals expand their vocabulary and improve their ability to communicate clearly in both speech and writing. Regular exposure to diverse texts sharpens critical thinking by presenting a range of ideas, arguments, and perspectives for the reader to evaluate. Furthermore, research has shown that students who read daily perform better academically across all subjects, as reading strengthens concentration, memory, and comprehension. Beyond academic benefits, reading also serves as a form of mental exercise that reduces stress and promotes lifelong learning.

Concluding Sentence

It is evident, therefore, that making reading a daily habit is among the most effective investments a person can make in their own development and future success.

06 Listening

Definition

Memorise

Listening is an active cognitive process of receiving, interpreting, constructing meaning from, and responding to spoken messages. It requires conscious attention and deliberate mental engagement.

Listening vs. Hearing

HearingListening
Passive and involuntaryActive and intentional
A physical process (sound waves enter the ear)A cognitive process (the brain interprets meaning)
Happens automatically without effortRequires effort, focus, and concentration
No interpretation or response requiredInvolves understanding and responding
Example: hearing traffic noise outsideExample: following a lecture and taking notes

Tips for Effective Listening

  • Maintain eye contact with the speaker
  • Avoid interrupting while the speaker is still talking
  • Remove or minimise distractions (phone, noise, side conversations)
  • Take notes on key points to aid memory and focus
  • Ask clarifying questions when needed - after the speaker finishes
  • Use positive body language: nodding, leaning slightly forward
  • Avoid thinking about your response while the speaker is still talking
  • Keep an open mind and withhold judgement until the message is complete

Barriers to Effective Listening

External Barriers

  • Physical noise and background sounds
  • Poor acoustics or audio quality
  • Uncomfortable or distracting environment
  • Visual distractions in the surroundings

Internal Barriers

  • Prejudice or preconceived ideas about the speaker
  • Emotional state (anger, stress, sadness, excitement)
  • Daydreaming or a wandering mind
  • Language differences or unfamiliar accents
  • Lack of interest in the topic
  • Thinking ahead to your own response

Benefits of Effective Listening

  • Leads to better understanding and retention of information
  • Builds stronger personal and professional relationships
  • Reduces misunderstandings and prevents conflict
  • Enhances learning and academic performance
  • Improves problem-solving and decision-making ability
  • Demonstrates respect, empathy, and consideration toward the speaker

Practice Question

Exam Question

Q: Distinguish between listening and hearing and identify three barriers to effective listening.

Sample Answer

Hearing is a passive, automatic physical process that occurs when sound waves enter the ear without any conscious effort or intention. Listening, by contrast, is an active cognitive process that involves paying deliberate attention, interpreting meaning, and formulating a response. While hearing is involuntary, listening demands focus, discipline, and engagement with the speaker's message.

Three barriers to effective listening are: first, physical noise, which includes background sounds or poor acoustics that prevent the listener from clearly receiving the message; second, emotional state, whereby personal feelings such as stress or anger distort the listener's interpretation of what is being said; and third, prejudice or preconceived ideas, where the listener makes judgements about the speaker or topic before the message has been fully delivered, causing selective or biased listening.

07 Speaking

Definition

Memorise

Speaking is the process of verbally conveying information, ideas, thoughts, or emotions to an audience using spoken language, supported by tone of voice, pace, and non-verbal cues such as body language and facial expression.

Ways to Improve English Speaking Skills

  • Practice speaking English daily - even alone, in a mirror, or recording yourself
  • Read aloud regularly to improve pronunciation and fluency
  • Expand vocabulary through reading and active use of a dictionary
  • Listen to fluent English speakers through podcasts, films, and interviews
  • Join speaking groups, debates, or participate actively in class discussions
  • Do not fear making mistakes - learn from them and keep going
  • Think in English rather than mentally translating from your native language
  • Pay attention to rhythm, intonation, and stress patterns in the language

Public Speaking

Memorise - Definition

Public speaking is the formal, structured act of delivering a prepared speech or presentation to a live audience with the purpose of informing, persuading, or entertaining.

Public Speaking Techniques

  • Eye contact - look at the audience to build connection, credibility, and confidence
  • Vocal variety - vary pitch, pace, and volume to maintain audience interest
  • Deliberate pausing - use pauses for emphasis and to allow ideas to settle
  • Natural gestures - use hand and body movements to reinforce spoken points
  • Storytelling - open with a relatable anecdote to capture and hold attention
  • Repetition - restate key ideas to reinforce the central message
  • Audience engagement - ask rhetorical questions; relate to your audience

Public Speaking Guidelines

  • Know your audience and tailor the content to their level and interests
  • Organise the speech clearly: introduction, body, and conclusion
  • Practice the full speech multiple times before delivery
  • Manage nervousness through deep breathing, preparation, and positive self-talk
  • Use visual aids (slides, charts, or props) where appropriate
  • Speak clearly, slowly, and at a volume everyone can hear
  • Stay within the allotted time limit
  • Avoid filler words: "um", "uh", "like", "you know"

Practice Question

Exam Question

Q: Define public speaking and describe five techniques that contribute to an effective public speech.

Sample Answer

Public speaking is the formal process of delivering a structured speech or presentation to a live audience, with the aim of informing, persuading, or entertaining. An effective public speaker employs several key techniques. First, maintaining eye contact with the audience builds trust and keeps listeners engaged. Second, vocal variety - the deliberate change in pitch, pace, and volume - prevents monotony and sustains audience attention throughout the speech. Third, strategic pausing allows important points to be emphasised and absorbed before the speaker continues. Fourth, natural gestures reinforce the spoken message through appropriate body language, making the delivery more dynamic and convincing. Fifth, storytelling makes abstract ideas relatable and memorable by anchoring them in human experience, thereby connecting the speaker to the audience on an emotional level.

08 Grammar

Collocations

Memorise - Definition

A collocation is a pair or group of words that naturally and habitually appear together in English. Collocations sound correct to native speakers; using wrong word combinations makes writing unnatural.

Correct CollocationTypeWrong Version

pay attentionVerb + Noun❌ give attention
heavy rainAdjective + Noun❌ strong rain

Comma Splices

Memorise - Definition

A comma splice is a grammatical error in which two independent clauses (each capable of standing alone as a sentence) are incorrectly joined by only a comma, without a coordinating conjunction.

Comma Splice - Error

"I was tired, I went to sleep."

"She studied hard, she passed the exam."

Three Ways to Fix

1. Add a conjunction: "I was tired, so I went to sleep."

2. Use a period: "I was tired. I went to sleep."

3. Use a semicolon: "I was tired; I went to sleep."

Run-On Sentences

Memorise - Definition

A run-on sentence occurs when two or more independent clauses are joined without any punctuation or conjunction - the clauses run directly into each other with no separation.

Run-On - Error

"She was late she missed the bus she had to call a taxi."

Corrected

"She was late. She missed the bus and had to call a taxi."

Fragments

Memorise - Definition

A sentence fragment is an incomplete sentence that is missing a subject, a verb, or a complete thought. It cannot stand alone as a grammatically correct sentence.

Fragment TypeError ExampleCorrected Sentence
Missing subject"Ran quickly to the door.""She ran quickly to the door."
Missing verb"The tall man in the blue coat.""The tall man in the blue coat smiled."
Incomplete thought"Because he was nervous.""He spoke quietly because he was nervous."
Dangling phrase"Walking slowly down the street.""She was walking slowly down the street."

Conditionals

Memorise - All Four Types
TypeStructureUseExample
Zero Conditional If + present simple, present simple General truths, facts, scientific laws "If you heat water to 100°C, it boils."
First Conditional If + present simple, will + infinitive Real or possible future situations "If it rains, I will stay home."
Second Conditional If + past simple, would + infinitive Unreal or hypothetical present/future "If I had a car, I would drive to work."
Third Conditional If + past perfect, would have + past participle Unreal or hypothetical past - could not be changed "If she had studied, she would have passed."

Practice Questions

Exam Question 1 - Error Correction

Q: Identify the error type and correct each sentence.

  1. "I love reading, it makes me a better student."
  2. "Running as fast as she could without stopping."
  3. "He was angry he left the room he did not say goodbye."
  4. "She make a good decision to study early."
Sample Answers
  1. Comma splice - "I love reading, and it makes me a better student." OR "I love reading. It makes me a better student."
  2. Fragment (missing subject and verb) - "She was running as fast as she could without stopping."
  3. Run-on sentence - "He was angry. He left the room and did not say goodbye."
  4. Wrong collocation - "She made a good decision to study early." (not "make" for past tense, and "make" is the correct verb, not "do")
Exam Question 2 - Conditionals

Q: Identify the conditional type and complete the sentence correctly.

  1. "If I ________ (win) the lottery, I would travel the world."
  2. "If she had left earlier, she ________ (not/miss) the train."
  3. "If you mix red and blue, you ________ (get) purple."
  4. "If it ________ (not/rain) tomorrow, we will go to the beach."
Sample Answers
  1. Second Conditional (hypothetical present) - "If I won the lottery, I would travel the world."
  2. Third Conditional (unreal past) - "If she had left earlier, she would not have missed the train."
  3. Zero Conditional (general truth) - "If you mix red and blue, you get purple."
  4. First Conditional (possible future) - "If it does not rain tomorrow, we will go to the beach."

10 Free Writing

Definition

Memorise

Free writing is a pre-writing or creative writing technique in which the writer writes continuously for a set period without stopping to edit, correct, or judge the output. The goal is to generate ideas and overcome writer's block. In an exam context, "free writing" typically refers to producing an independently composed paragraph or essay on a given or self-chosen topic.

Free Writing - Paragraph Format

When a free writing task calls for a paragraph, follow the standard three-part paragraph structure:

Format to Follow

[Topic Sentence - state one clear main idea]
[3 to 5 Supporting Sentences - evidence, examples, explanations]
[Concluding Sentence - restate the idea differently]

Exam Tips for Paragraph Free Writing

  • Decide on your topic sentence before writing anything else
  • Stay on one idea only - do not drift or add unrelated points
  • Use transition words to connect sentences smoothly
  • Aim for 150-200 words unless the instructions specify otherwise
  • Proofread for grammar and spelling before submitting

Free Writing - Essay Format

When a free writing task calls for an essay, follow the five-part essay structure:

Format to Follow

Introduction     Hook + Background + Thesis Statement
Body Paragraph 1   Topic Sentence + Evidence + Analysis
Body Paragraph 2   Topic Sentence + Evidence + Analysis
Body Paragraph 3   [Optional: Counterargument + Rebuttal]
Conclusion       Restated thesis + Summary + Closing thought

Exam Tips for Essay Free Writing

  • Spend 3-5 minutes planning and outlining your essay before writing
  • Write your thesis statement clearly - your position must be obvious
  • Every body paragraph must have its own distinct topic sentence
  • Aim for at least 400-500 words for a full-length essay
  • Use formal academic language - avoid contractions (don't, can't, it's)
  • Begin your conclusion with a formal phrase: "In conclusion," / "To conclude," / "In summary,"
  • Leave 3-5 minutes at the end to proofread

Quick Reference: Academic Language Dos and Don'ts

Avoid in Academic Writing
  • Contractions: don't, can't, it's, I've
  • Very informal words: "stuff", "things", "a lot"
  • First person in argumentative essays: "I think", "I believe"
  • Starting sentences with: "And", "But", "So"
  • Vague statements without evidence
Use Instead
  • Full forms: do not, cannot, it is, I have
