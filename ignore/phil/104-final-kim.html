Final Exam Pattern: 4 Short (5×4=20) + 4 Long (12.5×4=50)

Short Questions (5 Marks Each)
5M1. Plato's Four Cardinal Virtues

Introduction: Plato, in Republic Books II–IV, identified four cardinal (hinge) virtues essential for a just individual and a just state.

  1. Wisdom (Sophia) - Knowledge of the Good; reason ruling over the soul. In the state, wisdom resides in the rulers/philosopher-kings.
  2. Courage (Andreia) - Preservation of right opinion about what is to be feared. Found in the guardians/soldiers of the state.
  3. Temperance (Sophrosyne) - Self-mastery and harmony among the parts of the soul. All classes agree that reason should rule.
  4. Justice (Dikaiosyne) - Each part of the soul (and each class in the state) performing its proper function without interfering with others.

Connection: Plato's tripartite soul (Reason, Spirit, Appetite) maps directly onto these virtues. Justice is the overarching virtue that results when the other three are in harmony.

🔑 Memorize: "Wisdom in rulers, Courage in guardians, Temperance in all, Justice in harmony." - Plato, Republic IV.
5M2. Goodwill

Definition: Goodwill (Greek: eunoia; Kant: guter Wille) is the sincere desire for the well-being of others, without ulterior motive.

  • Kant's View: In Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Kant states: "Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good without qualification, except a Good Will." A good will acts from duty, not from inclination or self-interest.
  • Characteristics: (a) Universalizable, (b) Autonomous, (c) Respects persons as ends-in-themselves, (d) Not contingent on outcomes.
  • Distinction: Goodwill ≠ mere kindness. A shopkeeper who is honest to maintain reputation lacks goodwill; one who is honest because it is right possesses it.
🔑 Memorize: Kant's exact quote on Good Will being the only thing "good without qualification." Duty > Inclination.
5M3. Connection between Ethics and Sociology

Interdependence: Ethics and sociology are deeply interconnected because morality is a social phenomenon.

  • Sociology studies moral norms: Durkheim showed that moral facts are social facts - customs, laws, and taboos are products of collective consciousness.
  • Ethics evaluates social norms: While sociology describes what IS (descriptive), ethics prescribes what OUGHT to be (normative).
  • Socialization: Individuals internalize moral values through family, religion, education, and peer groups - all sociological institutions.
  • Social change & ethics: Movements like abolition, feminism, and environmentalism show how ethical reflection drives social transformation.
  • Relativism vs Universalism: Sociology reveals moral diversity across cultures; ethics debates whether such diversity implies relativism.

Conclusion: Ethics provides the ideal; sociology provides the empirical reality. Neither is complete without the other.

🔑 Memorize: Sociology = descriptive (what IS); Ethics = normative (what OUGHT TO BE). Durkheim: moral facts = social facts.
5M4. Connection between Ethics and Psychology

Complementary Disciplines: Ethics asks "What should I do?" Psychology asks "Why do I do what I do?"

  • Moral Development: Piaget and Kohlberg studied how children develop moral reasoning (pre-conventional → conventional → post-conventional).
  • Motivation: Psychology explains the psychological mechanisms behind moral behavior (empathy, guilt, conscience, altruism).
  • Free Will: Psychology's determinism (behaviorism, neuroscience) challenges ethics' assumption of moral responsibility.
  • Character & Virtue: Positive psychology (Seligman, Peterson) studies character strengths that overlap with Aristotelian virtues.
  • Moral Emotions: Shame, guilt, pride, and indignation are psychological states with ethical significance (Hume: reason is slave of passions).

Conclusion: Psychology explains the is of moral behavior; ethics prescribes the ought. Together they give a complete picture of moral life.

🔑 Memorize: Kohlberg's 3 levels of moral development. Psychology = mechanism; Ethics = evaluation. Hume: "Reason is the slave of the passions."
5M5. Perfectionism

Definition: Perfectionism is the ethical theory that the good consists in the development and realization of human nature's highest capacities.

  • Aristotle: Human good (eudaimonia) is the activity of the soul in accordance with virtue/excellence (arete). We must perfect our rational nature.
  • Key Features: (a) Objective goods exist, (b) Human nature has a telos (purpose), (c) Morality = self-realization, (d) Not merely pleasure or happiness.
  • Types: (a) Self-perfectionism (individual excellence), (b) Social perfectionism (state promotes human flourishing).
  • Critique: Who defines "perfection"? Risk of elitism. Marxists criticize it as bourgeois ideology. Nietzsche's Übermensch is a radical perfectionist ideal.

Modern relevance: Self-improvement culture, education policy, and human rights discourse all reflect perfectionist intuitions.

🔑 Memorize: Perfectionism = realizing human potential/telos. Aristotle: eudaimonia through arete. Objective goods > subjective preferences.
5MBonus: Nature of Ethics (from Quiz 1)

Definition: Ethics (from Greek ethos = character/custom) is the systematic study of moral values, principles, and conduct. It is a normative discipline concerned with what ought to be, not merely what is.

  • Scope: Examines concepts of right/wrong, good/evil, virtue/vice, justice/injustice.
  • Branches: Meta-ethics (meaning of moral terms), Normative ethics (moral theories), Applied ethics (practical issues).
  • Method: Rational, critical, and reflective - unlike mere custom or religious dogma.
  • Purpose: To guide human conduct toward the good life and just society.

Difference from Psychology: Psychology is descriptive (studies mental processes/behavior as they are); Ethics is normative (prescribes how one ought to act). Psychology explains why people lie; Ethics judges whether lying is wrong.

Long Questions (12.5 Marks Each)
12.5M1. Aristotle's Virtue Ethics, Properties, Certain Virtues & Golden Mean

Introduction: Aristotle's virtue ethics, articulated in Nicomachean Ethics, holds that the highest human good (eudaimonia) is achieved through the cultivation of virtues (arete).

A. Properties of Virtue Ethics
  1. Teleological: Everything has a purpose (telos). Human telos is rational activity in accordance with virtue.
  2. Agent-centered: Focuses on the moral character of the agent, not just rules or consequences.
  3. Habituation: Virtue is acquired through practice - "we become just by doing just acts."
  4. Practical Wisdom (Phronesis): Intellectual virtue that enables one to discern the right action in context.
  5. Eudaimonia: Not mere pleasure, but flourishing through the full exercise of rational capacities.
B. Certain Virtues & The Golden Mean

The Golden Mean (Mesotes) is the doctrine that virtue lies between two extremes - excess and deficiency:

VirtueDeficiencyExcess
CourageCowardiceRashness
TemperanceInsensibilityIntemperance
LiberalityIlliberalityProdigality
MagnificencePettinessVulgarity
TruthfulnessSelf-deprecationBoastfulness
FriendlinessQuarrelsomenessFlattery

Note: The mean is relative to us - what is moderate for one person may not be for another. There is no mean for vices like adultery or murder.

C. Evaluation

Strengths: Holistic, practical, emphasizes character. Weaknesses: Vague guidance, culturally relative, struggles with moral dilemmas.

🔑 Memorize: "Eudaimonia = rational activity + virtue." Golden Mean table. "We become builders by building, just by doing just acts." - Nicomachean Ethics II.1
12.5M2. Free Will and Ethics - Supporting Arguments & Discussion

Introduction: Free will is the capacity to choose between alternatives without external coercion. It is foundational to moral responsibility - without free will, praise and blame seem meaningless.

A. Arguments Supporting Free Will
  1. Phenomenological Argument: We experience ourselves as free agents. The feeling of deliberation and choice is undeniable in lived experience.
  2. Moral Responsibility Argument: If actions are fully determined, holding people morally accountable is unjust. Retributive justice presupposes free will.
  3. Kant's Practical Postulate: Freedom is a necessary presupposition of morality. We must act as if we are free; morality would collapse without this assumption.
  4. Agent Causation (Chisholm): Humans are "prime movers unmoved" - we cause actions without being caused to do so.
B. Determinist Challenges
  • Scientific Determinism: Neuroscience suggests brain states cause decisions before conscious awareness (Libet's experiments).
  • Theological Determinism: Divine omniscience and predestination seem to negate free choice.
  • Psychological Determinism: Freud showed unconscious drives shape behavior; Skinner showed environment conditions responses.
C. Compatibilism (Soft Determinism)

Spinoza, Hume, and modern philosophers argue that free will is compatible with determinism. Freedom = absence of external constraint + internal harmony. We are free when we act according to our own nature/desires, even if those desires are causally determined.

D. Discussion

Free will is essential for ethics because: (1) Moral praise/blame require it, (2) Dignity and autonomy depend on it, (3) Legal systems assume it. However, acknowledging determinism can lead to more compassionate ethics - understanding causes of behavior promotes rehabilitation over punishment.

🔑 Memorize: Kant: "Freedom must be presupposed as a property of the will of all rational beings." Compatibilism = freedom + determinism compatible. Libet's experiment challenges free will.
12.5M3. Philosophy's Connection to Ethics & Necessity in Current Society

Introduction: Ethics is a branch of philosophy. Philosophy provides the conceptual tools, critical methods, and foundational frameworks for ethical inquiry.

A. Philosophy's Connection to Ethics
  1. Epistemology: How do we know moral truths? Intuition? Reason? Experience? Ethics relies on epistemological foundations.
  2. Metaphysics: Do moral facts exist independently (realism) or are they human constructions (anti-realism)?
  3. Logic: Moral reasoning requires logical consistency. Contradictions in moral beliefs must be eliminated.
  4. Philosophy of Mind: What is conscience? Are moral emotions reliable guides? What is the nature of moral motivation?
  5. Political Philosophy: Justice, rights, and liberty are ethical concepts with philosophical foundations (Rawls, Nozick).
B. Necessity of Studying Ethics in Current Society
  • Technological Ethics: AI, genetic engineering, surveillance capitalism - new technologies create unprecedented moral dilemmas.
  • Environmental Crisis: Climate change demands ethical reflection on intergenerational justice and our duties to nature.
  • Globalization: Cultural relativism vs universal human rights requires philosophical ethical analysis.
  • Political Polarization: Ethical literacy helps citizens evaluate propaganda, fake news, and demagoguery.
  • Professional Ethics: Medicine, business, law, journalism all require applied ethical reasoning.
  • Personal Flourishing: Ethics guides individuals toward meaningful lives, authentic relationships, and self-respect.
C. Conclusion

Without philosophical ethics, society would rely on unexamined customs, authoritarian commands, or mere sentiment. Philosophy provides rational, systematic, and critical tools to navigate moral complexity in the 21st century.

🔑 Memorize: Ethics = applied philosophy. Current needs: AI ethics, climate justice, bioethics, professional ethics. Philosophy = critical reflection on assumptions.
12.5M4. Kant: Categorical vs Hypothetical Imperative & 3 Main Formulas

Introduction: Immanuel Kant, in Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785), distinguished two types of imperatives and formulated the Categorical Imperative in three ways.

A. Hypothetical Imperative
  • Conditional: "If you want X, then do Y."
  • Based on desire/inclination.
  • Example: "If you want to be healthy, exercise regularly."
  • Morally neutral - concerned with prudence/efficiency, not duty.
B. Categorical Imperative
  • Unconditional: "Do Y." Period.
  • Based on reason/duty alone, independent of desires.
  • Applies to all rational beings universally.
  • Moral commands are categorical - they command absolutely.
C. Three Main Formulas
  1. Formula of Universal Law (FUL): "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
    Test: Can your action's principle be universalized without contradiction? Example: Lying - if everyone lied, trust would collapse, making lying impossible. Contradiction in conception.
  2. Formula of Humanity (FH): "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means, but always at the same time as an end."
    Test: Does your action respect the rational autonomy and dignity of persons? Exploitation, slavery, and deception violate this formula.
  3. Formula of Autonomy / Kingdom of Ends (FA/FKE): "Act according to maxims of a universally legislating member of a merely possible kingdom of ends."
    Test: Would rational beings collectively legislate this principle? It combines universalizability with the dignity of persons in a community of equals.
D. Evaluation

Strengths: Absolute respect for human dignity, clear rational procedure, anti-consequentialist. Weaknesses: Rigid (no lying even to save a life?), abstract, difficult to apply to complex cases.

🔑 Memorize: Hypothetical = "If...then" (prudence). Categorical = unconditional (duty). 3 Formulas: Universal Law, Humanity as End, Kingdom of Ends. Kant's exact quotes.
12.5M5. Nature of Ethical Dilemma, Current Examples, Reasons & Prevention

Introduction: An ethical dilemma is a situation where moral obligations conflict, and every available option involves violating some moral principle.

A. Nature of Ethical Dilemma
  • Not merely a difficult choice, but a genuine conflict of duties.
  • Features: (1) Two or more moral principles apply, (2) They conflict, (3) No option fully satisfies all, (4) Significant consequences.
  • Types: (a) Epistemic dilemmas (uncertainty about facts), (b) Conflict dilemmas (known conflicting duties).
B. Ethical Dilemmas in Current Times
  1. Whistleblowing: Edward Snowden - duty to country vs duty to expose mass surveillance violating privacy.
  2. Medical Triage: COVID-19 - limited ventilators; who gets treated? Utilitarian (save most lives) vs deontological (equal worth of each person).
  3. AI & Privacy: Facial recognition prevents crime but violates civil liberties.
  4. Environmental vs Economic: Developing nations' right to industrialize vs global climate obligations.
  5. Free Speech vs Hate Speech: Platform moderation - protect expression or prevent harm?
C. Reasons for Ethical Dilemmas
  • Pluralism of values (no single moral theory captures all obligations).
  • Scarcity of resources (time, money, organs, energy).
  • Technological change outpacing moral frameworks.
  • Globalization creating cross-cultural value conflicts.
  • Institutional failures and lack of transparency.
D. Prevention & Resolution
  • Ethical Education: Training in moral reasoning and critical thinking.
  • Codes of Ethics: Clear professional guidelines (Hippocratic Oath, engineering codes).
  • Deliberative Democracy: Public forums for moral consensus-building.
  • Institutional Design: Systems that reduce scarcity and conflict (universal healthcare, renewable energy).
  • Ethics Committees: Hospital IRBs, corporate ethics boards for structured deliberation.
🔑 Memorize: Dilemma = genuine conflict of duties, not just hard choice. Current examples: whistleblowing, triage, AI privacy, climate vs economy. Prevention: education, codes, deliberation, institutional design.
12.5M6. Crime and Punishment through Ethics - Doctrines & Death Penalty

Introduction: Ethics provides frameworks for understanding why crimes are wrong and how society should respond to them.

A. Doctrines of Punishment
  1. Retributivism: Punishment is deserved because the offender has done wrong. "Just deserts." (Kant: "Judicial punishment can never be used merely as a means.") Proportionality matters - eye for an eye.
  2. Utilitarianism (Deterrence): Punishment is justified if it prevents future crime by deterring the offender and others. Bentham: punishment should maximize social welfare.
  3. Rehabilitation: Punishment should reform the offender, addressing root causes (poverty, mental health, education). Humanistic approach.
  4. Incapacitation: Remove dangerous individuals from society to protect others. Prisons as containment.
  5. Restorative Justice: Focus on repairing harm to victims and community, not just punishing offenders. Reconciliation over retribution.
B. Death Penalty Debate
Arguments FORArguments AGAINST
Ultimate deterrence for heinous crimesNo conclusive evidence it deters more than life imprisonment
Retributive justice for murder (life for life)Irreversible - risk of executing innocents
Closure for victims' familiesViolates human dignity / right to life
Cost of lifelong incarcerationMore expensive due to lengthy appeals process
Applied disproportionately to poor/minorities
Kant himself opposed death penalty as state murder
C. Personal Position (Sample)

Against the Death Penalty: (1) The risk of executing innocents is unacceptable in a fallible justice system. (2) It violates the Kantian principle of treating persons as ends - the state should not instrumentalize human life. (3) Rehabilitation and restorative justice offer more ethical alternatives. (4) International human rights standards (UN, EU) increasingly reject it. However, I acknowledge the retributive intuition that some crimes seem to "deserve" the ultimate punishment - but ethical consistency demands we resist this emotion in favor of human dignity.

🔑 Memorize: 5 doctrines: Retributivism, Deterrence, Rehabilitation, Incapacitation, Restorative. Death penalty: Kant opposed it (state murder), risk of innocence, violates dignity. Personal stance = against, with acknowledgment of retributive intuition.
12.5M7. Hedonism in Utilitarianism - Mill vs Bentham Comparison

Introduction: Utilitarianism is a form of Universalistic/Altruistic Hedonism - it holds that the right action maximizes pleasure/happiness for the greatest number.

A. What Kind of Hedonism is Utilitarianism?
  • Universalistic/Altruistic Hedonism: Unlike Egoistic Hedonism (Epicurus, Hobbes), utilitarianism considers everyone's happiness equally. The agent's own pleasure counts no more than anyone else's.
  • Consequentialist: Rightness depends on outcomes, not intentions or character.
  • Quantitative vs Qualitative: This is where Bentham and Mill diverge.
B. Bentham's Utilitarianism
  • Hedonic Calculus: Pleasure can be measured by seven dimensions: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent.
  • Quantitative: All pleasures are equal - "pushpin is as good as poetry."
  • Psychological Hedonism: Humans naturally seek pleasure and avoid pain.
  • Legal Reform: Applied to prison reform, legislation, and social policy.
C. Mill's Utilitarianism
  • Qualitative Distinction: "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied." Higher pleasures (intellectual, moral, aesthetic) are superior to lower pleasures (physical, sensual).
  • Competent Judges: Those who have experienced both types of pleasure prefer the higher - this is empirical evidence of qualitative superiority.
  • Rule Utilitarianism: We should follow rules that generally maximize happiness, not calculate each act.
  • Liberty: Individual freedom is essential for human development and higher pleasures (On Liberty).
D. Comparative Table
AspectBenthamMill
PleasureQuantitative (all equal)Qualitative (higher > lower)
MethodHedonic calculusCompetent judges' preference
FocusLegislation/social reformIndividual liberty/development
Human naturePsychological hedonistCapable of noble sentiments
RulesAct utilitarianismRule utilitarianism
E. Which is More Modern?

Mill is more modern because: (1) Qualitative distinctions align with human rights discourse and dignity, (2) Rule utilitarianism addresses the "tyranny of the majority," (3) His emphasis on liberty resonates with democratic values, (4) Modern welfare economics (Sen, Nussbaum) incorporates capabilities and flourishing beyond mere pleasure. However, Bentham's quantitative approach remains influential in cost-benefit analysis and public policy.

🔑 Memorize: Utilitarianism = Universalistic Hedonism. Bentham = quantitative, "pushpin = poetry." Mill = qualitative, "better Socrates dissatisfied." Mill is more modern. Hedonic calculus 7 dimensions.
12.5M8. Standards of Morality - Divine Command & Divine Law as Standard of Hedonism

Introduction: A "standard of morality" is the ultimate criterion by which actions are judged right or wrong. Various standards have been proposed throughout history.

A. Divine Command Theory (DCT)
  • Definition: An action is morally right because God commands it; wrong because God forbids it.
  • Sources: Scripture (Bible, Quran, Torah), religious tradition, revelation.
  • Supporters: St. Augustine, St. Thomas Aquinas (in part), William of Ockham, modern evangelical theologians.
  • Strengths: Provides objective, absolute moral standards; gives meaning and purpose; motivates through divine reward/punishment.
  • Weaknesses: Euthyphro dilemma (see Q9); makes morality arbitrary (if God commanded murder, would it be right?); excludes non-believers from moral community.
B. Divine Law as Standard of Hedonism/Utilitarianism

Some theologians attempt to reconcile divine law with hedonistic/utilitarian standards:

  • Aquinas: Natural law (accessible to reason) and divine law (revealed in scripture) both aim at human flourishing. Eternal law = God's rational plan; Natural law = human participation in eternal law through reason.
  • Paley: "The will of God = the general happiness." Divine law and utilitarianism converge because God designed the world so that virtue promotes happiness.
  • Christian Hedonism (Piper): "God is most glorified in us when we are most satisfied in Him." Seeking pleasure in God is the highest moral good.
C. Other Standards of Morality
  1. Hedonism: Pleasure/happiness as the standard (Epicurus, Bentham, Mill).
  2. Perfectionism: Realization of human potential (Aristotle, Marx, Nietzsche).
  3. Intuitionism: Self-evident moral truths known by intuition (Moore, Ross, Prichard).
  4. Kantian Rationalism: Universalizability and respect for persons.
  5. Contractualism: What rational agents would agree to (Rawls, Scanlon).
D. Conclusion

Divine command theory offers an objective standard but faces the Euthyphro problem. Attempts to synthesize divine law with hedonism (Paley, Aquinas) show that religious and secular ethics can converge on human flourishing, though their foundations differ fundamentally.

🔑 Memorize: DCT = right because God commands it. Aquinas: eternal law → natural law → human law. Paley: God's will = general happiness. Standards: DCT, Hedonism, Perfectionism, Intuitionism, Kantian, Contractualism.
12.5M9. The Euthyphro Dilemma (Plato) & Solutions

Introduction: In Plato's dialogue Euthyphro, Socrates asks: "Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?" This becomes the foundational challenge to Divine Command Theory.

A. The Dilemma Stated
  1. First Horn: God commands X because X is good. → Goodness exists independently of God. God is not the source of morality; He merely recognizes it. This undermines God's sovereignty and uniqueness.
  2. Second Horn: X is good because God commands it. → Morality is arbitrary. If God commanded cruelty, cruelty would be good. This makes morality seem like divine caprice, not reason.

Both horns seem problematic: the first limits God's power; the second makes morality arbitrary.

B. Proposed Solutions
  1. Divine Nature Theory (Modified DCT): God commands according to His necessarily good nature. God cannot command evil because it contradicts His essential nature. (Augustine, Aquinas, Adams) - This avoids arbitrariness while maintaining God's centrality.
  2. Natural Law Theory: Moral law is part of the rational structure of the universe (eternal law), which humans can discover through reason. God's will and reason converge. (Aquinas)
  3. Autonomy Thesis: Morality is independent of God altogether. God may exemplify goodness, but does not constitute it. (Plato himself seems to favor this - the Form of the Good is prior to the gods.)
  4. Kantian Solution: Morality is grounded in practical reason, not divine will. Religion is moral faith, not moral foundation.
  5. Constructivism: Morality is constructed by rational agents; God is irrelevant to moral truth. (Rawls, Scanlon)
C. Evaluation

The Divine Nature Theory is the most sophisticated theistic response, but it concedes that God's nature (not His will) is the ultimate standard - which is closer to the first horn. The Euthyphro dilemma remains a powerful argument for grounding ethics in reason rather than divine fiat.

🔑 Memorize: Euthyphro: "Is X good because God loves it, or does God love it because it's good?" Two horns = independence vs arbitrariness. Best solution: Divine Nature Theory (God's nature is necessarily good). Aquinas = natural law. Plato = Form of the Good.
12.5M10. Intuitionism as a Standard of Morality

Introduction: Ethical Intuitionism holds that some moral truths are self-evident and known through intuition, not inference from other truths or empirical observation.

A. Historical Development
  • Early Intuitionists: Samuel Clarke, Richard Price, Joseph Butler (18th century) - moral truths are as certain as mathematical truths.
  • G.E. Moore (Principia Ethica, 1903): "Good" is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property. The "Naturalistic Fallacy" = defining "good" in natural terms (pleasure, happiness, etc.). We intuitively recognize goodness.
  • W.D. Ross (The Right and the Good, 1930): Identified prima facie duties - self-evident moral obligations that are binding unless overridden by stronger duties.
B. Ross's Prima Facie Duties
  1. Fidelity: Keeping promises and telling the truth.
  2. Reparation: Making amends for wrongs done.
  3. Gratitude: Returning kindness received.
  4. Justice: Distributing benefits fairly.
  5. Beneficence: Improving the condition of others.
  6. Non-maleficence: Not harming others.
  7. Self-improvement: Developing one's own talents/virtues.

These are not absolute duties but prima facie (at first sight) - in concrete situations, they may conflict, and we must use intuition to determine which duty is actual (all-things-considered).

C. Key Arguments for Intuitionism
  1. Self-evidence: Basic moral principles ("torturing innocents is wrong") are known immediately, like "2+2=4."
  2. Against Skepticism: If we doubt all moral knowledge, we fall into moral paralysis.
  3. Moral Experience: We genuinely seem to perceive moral properties (wrongness of cruelty).
D. Criticisms
  • Moral Disagreement: If intuitions were reliable, why do cultures disagree so radically?
  • Obscurity: What exactly is "intuition"? It seems like unexamined prejudice.
  • No Decision Procedure: When intuitions conflict, intuitionism offers no method for resolution.
  • Naturalistic Fallacy challenged: Some argue Moore's argument begs the question against naturalism.
E. Conclusion

Intuitionism captures the phenomenology of moral experience - we do seem to grasp some moral truths directly. However, its inability to resolve conflicts and its vulnerability to the disagreement problem make it incomplete as a standalone moral theory. It works best as a complement to other ethical frameworks.

🔑 Memorize: Moore: "Good" is simple, indefinable, non-natural. Naturalistic Fallacy. Ross: 7 prima facie duties (Fidelity, Reparation, Gratitude, Justice, Beneficence, Non-maleficence, Self-improvement). Intuitionism = self-evident moral truths.

Quiz 1Nature of Ethics & Difference from Psychology

Nature of Ethics: Ethics is the systematic, rational study of moral values, principles, and conduct. It is a normative discipline concerned with what ought to be - right/wrong, good/evil, virtue/vice. It involves critical reflection, logical analysis, and justification of moral beliefs. Branches: Meta-ethics (meaning of moral language), Normative ethics (moral theories), Applied ethics (practical issues like bioethics, business ethics).

Difference from Psychology:

Connection: Psychology provides empirical data about human behavior that ethics must account for; ethics provides the normative framework that psychology lacks.

Quiz 2Kant's Categorical Imperative & Free Will

Kant's Categorical Imperative: An unconditional moral law that applies to all rational beings. Unlike hypothetical imperatives ("If you want X, do Y"), categorical imperatives command absolutely: "Do Y." Three formulas: (1) Universal Law - act only on maxims you can will as universal law, (2) Humanity as End - never treat persons merely as means, (3) Kingdom of Ends - act as a legislating member of a community of ends.

Relationship with Free Will: For Kant, freedom of the will is a practical postulate of morality - we must presuppose it to act morally. A categorical imperative requires that we could have done otherwise; otherwise, moral obligation is meaningless. In the Critique of Practical Reason, Kant argues: "Freedom and unconditional practical law reciprocally imply each other." We are free because we are subject to moral law; we know moral law because we experience ourselves as free. This is the "fact of reason." Without free will, there is no autonomy, no dignity, and no moral responsibility.

Quiz 3Hedonism - Characteristics & Types

Definition: Hedonism (from Greek hedone = pleasure) is the theory that pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only intrinsic evil.

Characteristics as Standard of Morality:

  1. Consequentialist: Rightness depends on outcomes (pleasure produced).
  2. Universal: Pleasure/pain are the common currency of all moral calculation.
  3. Quantifiable: Bentham's hedonic calculus attempts to measure pleasure.
  4. Psychological basis: Humans naturally seek pleasure and avoid pain.
  5. Forward-looking: Focuses on future consequences, not past intentions.

Difference between Egoistic and Universalistic/Altruistic Hedonism:

Additional Presentable Questions
5MExtra Short: Define Meta-ethics, Normative Ethics, Applied Ethics
  1. Meta-ethics: Studies the nature, meaning, and status of moral statements. Questions: Are moral facts real? What do moral words mean? (Cognitivism vs Non-cognitivism, Realism vs Anti-realism).
  2. Normative Ethics: Provides general moral principles and theories to guide conduct. Major theories: Utilitarianism, Deontology, Virtue Ethics, Contractualism.
  3. Applied Ethics: Applies normative theories to specific practical issues. Examples: Bioethics, business ethics, environmental ethics, AI ethics, criminal justice ethics.
5MExtra Short: What is the Naturalistic Fallacy?
G.E. Moore's critique of defining "good" in natural terms (pleasure, happiness, evolutionary fitness, etc.). Moore argued that "good" is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property. Any attempt to define it commits the "open question" fallacy - we can always ask "But is X really good?" showing that X and "good" are not identical. This undermines ethical naturalism and supports intuitionism.
12.5MExtra Long: Egoism vs Altruism in Ethics

Psychological Egoism: Humans are descriptively motivated only by self-interest (Hobbes). Even "altruistic" acts seek self-satisfaction. Ethical Egoism: Humans ought to pursue self-interest (Ayn Rand). Psychological Altruism: Humans naturally care for others. Ethical Altruism: We ought to prioritize others' welfare (Christian ethics, utilitarianism). Discussion: Evolutionary biology supports reciprocal altruism; Kant and Christianity demand pure altruism; Aristotle's friendship balances self and other.

12.5MExtra Long: Relativism vs Universalism in Ethics

Cultural Relativism: Moral truths are relative to cultures (Herskovits). No universal moral standards exist. Problems: Cannot criticize Nazi Germany; makes moral progress meaningless; confuses descriptive diversity with normative relativism. Moral Universalism: Some moral truths apply to all humans (human rights, genocide is wrong). Supported by: overlapping moral consensus across cultures, rational argument, international law. Moderate position: Some values are universal (don't murder), others are culturally variable (dietary practices).

12.5MExtra Long: Social Contract Theory (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Rawls)

Hobbes: State of nature = "war of all against all." People surrender freedom to a sovereign for security (Leviathan). Locke: State of nature has natural rights (life, liberty, property). Government by consent, right to revolution. Rousseau: General will = true freedom through collective self-legislation. Rawls: Original position + veil of ignorance → principles of justice (equal liberty, difference principle). Contractualism as ethical standard: morality = what rational agents would agree to.

5MExtra Short: What is Conscience?
Conscience is the inner faculty that judges the moral quality of one's actions. Butler: Conscience is the supreme faculty, given by God. Freud: Conscience = superego, internalized parental/social authority. Existentialism: Conscience calls us to authentic self-choice (Heidegger). Modern view: Conscience is shaped by socialization but involves genuine moral reasoning. It is fallible - conscience can be mistaken (Nazi soldiers had "clear consciences").
