Q1. Plato's Four Cardinal Virtues

Answer (5 Marks Format):

In Plato's masterpiece The Republic, virtues are moral excellence essential for individual soul harmony and the ideal state (Polis). Plato identifies four Cardinal Virtues, corresponding directly to his tripartite division of the human soul:

  1. Wisdom (Prudence / Sophrosyne): The virtue of the Rational Part of the soul. It provides sound judgment, practical insight, and moral knowledge.
  2. Courage (Fortitude / Andreia): The virtue of the Spirited Part. It enables an individual to endure fear and pain to uphold righteous principles.
  3. Temperance (Moderation): The virtue of the Appetitive Part. It represents self-control, restraining physical desires and instincts.
  4. Justice (Dikaiosyne): The supreme unifying virtue achieved when all three parts of the soul perform their proper duties harmoniously without interfering with one another.
"Justice in the individual is the state where Reason rules, Spirit supports Reason, and Appetite obeys." — Plato

Q2. Goodwill in Kantian Ethics

Answer (5 Marks Format):

Immanuel Kant opens his Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals with the famous statement that nothing in the world can be called good without qualification except a Good Will.

  • Intrinsic Value: Talents of mind (intelligence), qualities of temperament (courage), or gifts of fortune (wealth, power) are conditionally good—they can become evil if guided by a bad will. Goodwill alone is intrinsically good in itself.
  • Motivated by Duty: A goodwill is not good because of its consequences, outcomes, or utility. It acts purely out of respect for the moral law (Duty for Duty's sake), regardless of personal inclinations or desires.
  • Rational Autonomy: A goodwill self-legislates moral laws through rational reflection, embodying moral freedom.

Q3. Connection Between Ethics and Sociology

Answer (5 Marks Format):

Ethics and Sociology are deeply interconnected social sciences, yet they operate from distinct philosophical standpoints:

  • Sociology (Descriptive): Studies social structures, customs, institutions, and how humans actually behave in groups ("What is").
  • Ethics (Normative): Evaluates human behavior, moral values, and ideal conduct ("What ought to be").
  • Interdependence:
    • Sociology provides Ethics with empirical background—showing social realities, customs, and practical contexts.
    • Ethics provides Sociology with normative standards—critiquing unjust social practices (e.g., discrimination, caste systems) and guiding social reform.

Conclusion: Sociology without Ethics lacks moral orientation; Ethics without Sociology lacks practical application.

Q4. Connection Between Ethics and Psychology

Answer (5 Marks Format):

Ethics and Psychology both analyze human action, mind, and behavior, but differ in scope:

  • Psychology (Positive/Descriptive Science): Investigates the origin, nature, and mental mechanics of human behavior, motives, desires, and emotions.
  • Ethics (Normative Science): Judges whether voluntary human actions are morally right or wrong, good or bad.
  • Key Interconnections:
    • Ethics relies on Psychology to understand volition, intentions, free will, and moral development. Only voluntary actions studied by psychology fall under moral judgment.
    • Psychology aids Ethics in evaluating psychological states such as moral remorse, guilt, and empathy.

Distinction: Psychology explains why people act; Ethics evaluates whether they should act that way.

Q5. Perfectionism (Self-Realization Theory)

Answer (5 Marks Format):

Perfectionism (also known as Self-Realization Ethics or Eudaimonism) is a moral theory advocated by thinkers like Aristotle, Spinoza, Hegel, T.H. Green, and F.H. Bradley.

  • Core Premise: The supreme moral good is the complete self-realization and harmonious development of human nature (intellectual, physical, emotional, and moral capacities).
  • Synthesis of Extremes: Rejects both extreme Hedonism (pursuit of raw pleasure) and extreme Asceticism (suppression of desires). It advocates for the rational integration of all human faculties.
  • Social Aspect: An individual realizes their true self not in isolation, but as an active member of an organized moral community ("My Station and Its Duties").
3. Long Essay Questions (12.5 Marks Each) 12.5 Marks

Q1. Aristotle's Virtue Ethics, Properties, Key Virtues & The Golden Mean

1. Introduction to Virtue Ethics:

Aristotle's moral theory in Nicomachean Ethics is a teleological, agent-centered ethics. Unlike modern deontological or consequentialist theories that ask "What ought I to do?", Aristotle asks, "What kind of person should I be?". The ultimate end (Telos) of human life is Eudaimonia (flourishing, well-being, living well).

2. Properties of Virtue Ethics:

  • Habitual Character (Hexis): Virtue (Arête) is not a single action or emotion, but a settled disposition of character acquired through practice and habituation.
  • Role of Reason: Human flourishing consists in performing the unique human function (Ergon), which is activity of the soul in accordance with reason.
  • Division of Virtues:
    • Intellectual Virtues: Acquired through teaching and instruction (e.g., Phronesis or practical wisdom).
    • Moral Virtues: Acquired through habituation (Ethos) and practice (e.g., courage, temperance).

3. The Doctrine of the Golden Mean:

Aristotle defines moral virtue as a mean state between two extremes—a vice of deficiency and a vice of excess, relative to us and determined by rational principle.

Deficiency (Too Little) Golden Mean (Virtue) Excess (Too Much)
Cowardice Courage Rashness
Insensibility Temperance Licentiousness
Stinginess / Meanness Generosity Extravagance
Pusillanimity (Smallness of soul) Magnanimity Vanity / Arrogance
Surliness / Rudeness Friendliness Flattery

4. Critical Evaluation:

Strengths: Holistic, integrates emotion with reason, emphasizes life-long moral growth and character.
Weaknesses: Lacks precise decision rules in moral dilemmas; the "mean" is subjective and culturally relative; some actions (e.g., murder, theft) have no mean state and are intrinsically evil.

Q2. Free Will and Ethics: Supporting Arguments & Discussion

1. Introduction & The Problem:

Ethics is the evaluation of moral responsibility. The fundamental premise of all ethical systems is Free Will—the capacity of human agents to make uncoerced, conscious choices among alternatives. If determinism fully controls human actions like clockwork, praise, blame, guilt, and moral law lose all meaning.

2. Philosophical Positions:

  • Hard Determinism: Universal causation governs all physical and mental events. Free will is an illusion; moral responsibility does not exist.
  • Libertarianism: Humans possess genuine agency and causal power independent of prior physical causes.
  • Compatibilism (Soft Determinism): Free will is compatible with determinism as long as an agent acts according to internal desires without external compulsion.

3. Arguments Supporting Free Will in Ethics:

  1. Argument from Moral Experience & Consciousness: Humans possess an undeniable immediate experience of deliberation, choice, and post-action moral feelings (guilt, remorse, or moral pride).
  2. Kant's Postulate: "Ought Implies Can": Immanuel Kant argued that if moral duty mandates "You ought to do X," it logically presupposes "You can do X." Moral obligation is meaningless without the freedom to choose.
  3. Foundation of Moral & Legal Responsibility: Justice systems, criminal law, and social rewards/punishments rest on the concept of voluntary action (mens rea). Punishment without free will is unjust.
  4. Argument from Rational Deliberation: Weighing reasons, seeking ethical advice, and self-correction demonstrate that conscious thought causally influences behavior.

4. Conclusion: Free will is the necessary postulate of practical reason. Without assuming moral agency, ethics collapses into mechanical biology.

Q3. Philosophy's Connection in Ethics & Necessity of Ethics in Current Society

1. Ethics as a Core Branch of Philosophy:

Ethics (Moral Philosophy) is one of the main branches of Philosophy, alongside Metaphysics (study of reality), Epistemology (study of knowledge), and Logic. Philosophy provides the foundational methodology for ethics:

  • Epistemological Link: How do we know what is morally right? Through intuition, reason, divine revelation, or empirical observation?
  • Metaphysical Link: Do objective moral facts exist? What is the nature of human agency and soul?

2. Necessity of Studying Ethics in Current Society:

Modern society faces unprecedented ethical crises driven by technological, biological, and political shifts:

  1. Artificial Intelligence & Data Ethics: Automated decision-making, algorithmic bias, surveillance, autonomous weapons, and privacy rights demand rigorous ethical frameworks.
  2. Bioethics & Environmental Crisis: Gene editing (CRISPR), climate change, resource depletion, and animal rights require balancing human progress with ecological survival.
  3. Political Polarization & Governance: Rising corruption, erosion of democratic norms, and disinformation require a rebirth of public virtue, human rights, and civic duty.
  4. Moral Relativism & Nihilism: In a post-modern, hyper-connected world, ethics grounds individuals against moral confusion, restoring purpose and social cohesion.

Conclusion: Science and technology give us power; Ethics gives us wisdom to use that power for human flourishing.

Q4. Kant's Categorical vs. Hypothetical Imperative & The 3 Formulations

1. Categorical vs. Hypothetical Imperatives:

Hypothetical Imperative Categorical Imperative
Conditional command ("If you want X, do Y"). Unconditional command ("Do Y").
Based on desire, interest, or consequences. Based purely on pure practical reason and duty.
Example: "Study hard if you want good grades." Example: "Do not tell lies." (Applies universally).

2. The Three Main Formulas of the Categorical Imperative:

  1. Formula of Universal Law: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
    • Test: Can your action's rule be universalized without logical contradiction? (e.g., making a lying promise fails because if everyone lied, the institution of promising destroys itself).
  2. Formula of Humanity as an End-in-Itself: "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end."
    • Prohibits exploitation, manipulation, and treating rational beings as disposable instruments.
  3. Formula of Autonomy / Kingdom of Ends: "Act according to maxims of a universally legislating member of a merely possible kingdom of ends."
    • Conceives a systematic union of rational beings through common objective moral laws where every person is both lawgiver and subject.

3. Relationship with Freedom of the Will: Kant demonstrates that rational autonomy is the sole principle of morality. Free will and moral law are reciprocal concepts—a free will is one under moral law.

Q5. Ethical Dilemmas: Nature, Contemporary Examples, Reasons & Prevention

1. Nature of Ethical Dilemma:

An ethical dilemma occurs when an agent is placed in a complex situation where two or more moral imperatives conflict, and choosing one option inevitably leads to violating another moral obligation. Neither option is completely morally flawless.

2. Contemporary Ethical Dilemmas:

  • Privacy vs. Public Safety (Tech Ethics): Government mass surveillance and data encryption backdoors to stop crime versus protecting civil rights and digital privacy.
  • Medical Triage & Resource Allocation: Allocating scarce medical equipment (e.g., ICU beds, ventilators) during health pandemics—saving younger patients vs. prioritizing critical care by arrival.
  • Economic Development vs. Environmental Protection: Developing nations industrializing to eliminate poverty vs. strict international carbon emission caps.

3. Reasons for Ethical Dilemmas:

  • Rapid technological advancements creating unprecedented choices outstripping moral/legal traditions.
  • Pluralistic societies with competing value systems (e.g., Utilitarian outcome focus vs. Deontological rights focus).
  • Resource scarcity and incomplete empirical information.

4. Prevention and Resolution Frameworks:

  • Establishing proactive Institutional Ethics Review Boards and clear Codes of Conduct.
  • Utilizing structured moral reasoning frameworks (weighing rights, duties, outcomes, and virtues).
  • Fostering transparent multi-stakeholder dialogue before implementing major technological changes.

Q6. Crime & Punishment in Ethics: Doctrines & Capital Punishment Debate

1. Ethical Basis of Crime and Punishment:

A crime is a breach of moral-legal obligation that disrupts social order. Punishment is state-sanctioned pain/deprivation imposed on an offender. Ethically, inflicting pain is wrong unless justified by a higher moral theory.

2. Three Major Doctrines of Punishment:

  1. Retributive Theory (Kant, Hegel): Backward-looking (*Jus Talionis*). Punishment is an end in itself, deserved because of the crime ("An eye for an eye"). Restores moral equilibrium.
  2. Deterrent / Preventive Theory (Bentham, Utilitarianism): Forward-looking. Punishment is justified only if it prevents future crime by deterring others and incapacitating offenders. Pain of punishment must outweigh pleasure of crime.
  3. Reformative / Rehabilitative Theory: Humanistic view. Considers crime as a psychological illness or social dysfunction. Focuses on re-educating, treating, and restoring the criminal.

3. The Death Penalty (Capital Punishment) Debate:

  • Arguments Supporting Capital Punishment:
    • Retribution: Murder forfeits the murderer's right to life; ultimate justice requires ultimate penalty.
    • Deterrence & Protection: Permanently protects society from dangerous individuals.
  • Arguments Opposing Capital Punishment:
    • Inviolability of Life: Human dignity is absolute; the state shouldn't execute citizens.
    • Irreversibility & Miscarriages of Justice: Risk of executing innocent people due to flawed legal systems.
    • Lack of Empirical Deterrence: Studies show life imprisonment deters crime equally effectively.
Principled Exam Position: Capital Punishment should be rejected in modern ethical legal frameworks in favor of Reformative Justice and Life Imprisonment without Parole, honoring human dignity while securing society.

Q7. Utilitarianism & Hedonism: Bentham vs. Mill & Modern Analysis

1. Utilitarianism as Hedonism:

Utilitarianism is a consequentialist, universalistic form of Hedonism. It holds that pleasure/happiness is the sole intrinsic good and pain is the sole intrinsic evil. Its fundamental mandate is the "Greatest Happiness Principle" (maximizing utility for the greatest number).

2. Bentham vs. Mill Comparison:

Jeremy Bentham John Stuart Mill
Quantitative Hedonism: All pleasures are equal in quality. "Push-pin is as good as poetry." Qualitative Hedonism: Pleasures differ in quality (Higher mental vs. Lower bodily pleasures).
Calculated via Hedonic Calculus (Intensity, Duration, Certainty, etc.). Evaluated by "Competent Judges" who have experienced both.
Act Utilitarianism: Assess each specific action directly. Foundation for Rule Utilitarianism: Follow general rules that produce greatest long-term good.
"It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." — J.S. Mill

3. Which is More Modern? Detailed Thesis:

John Stuart Mill's Qualitative Utilitarianism is far more modern and viable. Bentham's quantitative calculus leads to the "tyranny of the majority" and justifies immoral acts (e.g., sacrificing an innocent person if it pleases a mob). Mill's framework protects individual rights, liberty (*On Liberty*), intellectual cultivation, and aligns with modern liberal constitutional democracy and human rights ethics.

Q8. Standards of Morality: Divine Command Theory & Divine Law in Utilitarianism

1. Divine Command Theory (Theological Voluntarism):

Divine Command Theory posits that moral truths depend directly on God's commands. An act is morally right if God commands it, and wrong if God forbids it.

  • Strengths: Provides objective, authoritative, and universal moral obligations with clear cosmic accountability.
  • Critiques: Leaves morality vulnerable to religious interpretation and subject to the Euthyphro Dilemma.

2. Divine Law as a Standard in Hedonism / Utilitarianism (Theistic Utilitarianism):

Developed by thinkers like William Paley and John Gay, Theological Utilitarianism bridges divine law and utilitarianism:

  • Premise: God is infinitely good and benevolent, desiring the ultimate happiness of all His creation.
  • Synthesis: God's Divine Laws are not arbitrary commands, but practical guidelines designed to maximize universal human happiness and eliminate suffering.
  • Motivation: Divine rewards (heaven) and punishments (hell) solve the problem of egoism by providing rational motivation for individuals to act for the "greatest happiness of others."

Q9. The Euthyphro Dilemma (Plato) & Its Solution

1. Origin & Statement of Dilemma:

In Plato's dialogue Euthyphro, Socrates asks Euthyphro: "Is the pious (morally good) loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?"

2. Analysis of the Two Horns:

Horn 1: Voluntarism (It is good because God commands it)

• Problem: Morality becomes arbitrary. If God commanded murder or torture, it would become morally good overnight. It reduces God's goodness to a empty statement ("God commands what God commands").

Horn 2: Intellectualism (God commands it because it is intrinsically good)

• Problem: Morality exists independently of God. Goodness becomes an external standard to which God must conform, limiting God's sovereignty and omnipotence.

3. Solutions to the Dilemma:

Philosophers like Thomas Aquinas and modern thinkers like Robert Adams (Modified Divine Command Theory) offer a Third Way / Essentialist Solution:

  • Split the False Dilemma: Reject both horns by anchoring morality in God's Immutable Nature.
  • Essentialism: God does not command things arbitrarily (rejecting Horn 1), nor does God obey an external law (rejecting Horn 2). God's essential nature IS the ultimate standard of Goodness. God commands what is good because His commands are a necessary reflection of His intrinsically loving, just, and perfect character.

Q10. Intuitionism as a Standard of Morality in Ethics

1. Introduction to Intuitionism:

Intuitionism is a non-naturalist cognitivist theory holding that fundamental moral truths (such as "Goodness", "Duty") are self-evident and known directly through rational moral intuition, without reliance on empirical observation or logical deduction.

2. Key Proponents & Concepts:

  • G.E. Moore (Principia Ethica):
    • Argued "Good" is a simple, unanalyzable, non-natural quality (analogous to the color "Yellow").
    • Formulated the Naturalistic Fallacy: Attempting to define moral "Good" in terms of natural properties (e.g., pleasure, evolution, survival) is logically flawed. Demonstrated via the Open Question Argument.
  • W.D. Ross (Prima Facie Duties): Moral duties are self-evident intuitionist duties that apply "at first glance" unless conflicted by a weightier duty. Included: Fidelity, Reparation, Gratitude, Justice, Beneficence, Self-improvement, Non-maleficence.

3. Strengths & Criticisms:

  • Strengths: Preserves objective moral truths; matches everyday moral experience where basic rules feel self-evident.
  • Criticisms: Fails to resolve conflicting intuitions among different people/cultures; provides no objective decision mechanism when intuitions clash.
4. Past Quiz Exams Review Quiz Revision

Exam 1: Nature of Ethics & Differences between Ethics and Psychology

• Nature of Ethics: Ethics is the normative, reflective, and systematic branch of philosophy that investigates moral principles governing right and wrong human conduct. It is non-empirical, normative, and aims at human good (Eudaimonia).

• Differences: Psychology is a descriptive science that explains mental mechanics and behavior ("what is"). Ethics is a normative science that judges voluntary conduct against moral standards ("what ought to be").

Exam 2: Kant's Categorical Imperative & Freedom of the Will

• Explanation: Categorical Imperative is the central unconditionally binding moral law discovered through practical reason. (See Long Q4 for formulas).

• Relationship with Freedom: Freedom is the precondition of the Categorical Imperative. To obey moral law autonomously means acting free from sensory impulses, making autonomy and freedom identical in Kant's moral metaphysics.

Exam 3: Hedonism, Characteristics & Egoistic vs Altruistic Hedonism

• Hedonism: The ethical doctrine that pleasure is the sole intrinsic good and pain the sole evil.

• Egoistic Hedonism (Epicurus, Hobbes): Focuses on maximizing the individual's personal pleasure.

• Universalistic / Altruistic Hedonism (Utilitarianism - Bentham, Mill): Focuses on maximizing pleasure for all sentient beings ("Greatest happiness of the greatest number").

Exam 4: Crime and Punishment Synthesis

• Synthesis: Ethical justification of state punishment requires balancing Retribution (desert for past acts), Deterrence (prevention of future crime), and Rehabilitation (re-education of offender). (See Long Q6).
