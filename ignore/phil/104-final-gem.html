1. Technical Terminology & Core Formulas

Memory Matrix
Key Philosophical Concepts for Exam Citation
  • Eudaimonia (Aristotle): Objective human flourishing/well-being achieved through character excellence (Arête), not temporary subjective pleasure.
  • Categorical Imperative (Kant): An unconditional, non-hypothetical moral command grounded in pure practical reason, binding on all rational beings regardless of inclination.
  • Euthyphro Dilemma (Plato): The meta-ethical challenge asking whether an action is morally right because God commands it (Voluntarism) or whether God commands it because it is intrinsically right (Intellectualism).
  • Naturalistic Fallacy (G.E. Moore): The logical error of defining non-natural moral properties (like "Good") in terms of natural or physical properties (like "Pleasure" or "Evolution").
  • Jus Talionis (Kant/Retributivism): The principle of exact proportionality in retributive justice ("eye for an eye"), respecting the offender's rational agency.

2. Short Questions (5 Marks Each)

Target: ~200 Words
Short Question • 5 Marks

1. Plato's Four Cardinal Virtues

Undergraduate Standard Answer:

In Plato's seminal dialogue The Republic (Book IV), virtue (Arête) is conceptualized as the harmonious functional integrity of a structured system. Plato identifies four Cardinal Virtues—Wisdom, Courage, Temperance, and Justice—and explicitly grounds them in his Tripartite Model of the Human Soul (Psyche) and the parallel structure of the ideal city-state (Polis):

  1. Wisdom (Prudence / Sophia): The virtue of the Rational Part (Logistikon). It represents rational governance, moral foresight, and knowledge of the Good. In the state, it corresponds to the Guardian-Kings.
  2. Courage (Fortitude / Andreia): The virtue of the Spirited Part (Thumos). It is the unyielding preservation of rational convictions regarding what ought to be feared or endured. In the state, it characterizes the Auxiliaries/Soldiers.
  3. Temperance (Moderation / Sophrosyne): The virtue of the Appetitive Part (Epithumetikon). It is the harmonious agreement between higher and lower elements, ensuring bodily desires obey reason.
  4. Justice (Dikaiosyne): The supreme structural virtue. Plato defines justice as "doing one's own proper work and not meddling in the affairs of others." It is achieved when Reason rules, Spirit executes Reason's mandates, and Appetite submits.
"Justice is not a mere external performance, but an internal ordering of the soul where each part fulfills its natural function." — Plato, Republic
Short Question • 5 Marks

2. Goodwill in Kantian Deontology

Undergraduate Standard Answer:

Immanuel Kant opens Section I of the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals with the central thesis of deontology: "It is impossible to think of anything at all in the world... that could be considered good without limitation except a Good Will."

  • Unconditional vs. Conditional Value: Intellectual talents (wisdom), psychological qualities (courage), and material fortune (wealth, power) possess only conditional value. If guided by an evil intention, they become exceptionally harmful. A Goodwill possesses unconditional intrinsic value; its goodness is self-contained.
  • Action from Duty (Achtung): A Goodwill is not evaluated by its fruits, consequences, or utility. It is a will that acts purely from duty (out of reverence for the moral law), explicitly contrasted with acting merely in accordance with duty out of self-interest or immediate inclination.
  • Rational Autonomy: The Goodwill represents practical reason self-legislating moral principles independently of sensory impulses (heteronomy).
Short Question • 5 Marks

3. Connection Between Ethics and Sociology

Undergraduate Standard Answer:

Ethics and Sociology are complementary social sciences that study human conduct from distinct methodological standpoints:

  • Methodological Distinction (Descriptive vs. Normative): Sociology is an empirical, descriptive science that investigates social structures, group behaviors, customs, and institutions as factual occurrences ("What is"). Ethics is a philosophical, normative science that critically evaluates human conduct against ideal standards of rightness and justice ("What ought to be").
  • Interdependence & Fact-Value Dialectic:
    • Sociology's contribution to Ethics: Sociology provides essential empirical data regarding social contexts, institutional structures, and cultural norms. Without empirical awareness, ethical theories remain abstract and unviable.
    • Ethics' contribution to Sociology: Sociology without normative critique degenerates into uncritical moral relativism. Ethics provides the normative tools required to critique oppressive social institutions (e.g., systemic racism, feudalism) and guide legislative reform.
Short Question • 5 Marks

4. Connection Between Ethics and Psychology

Undergraduate Standard Answer:

Ethics and Psychology share a core interest in human action, mental states, and volition, yet operate under different scientific objectives:

  • Positive Science vs. Normative Science: Psychology is a positive/empirical science analyzing the psychological mechanisms of behavior, emotional drives, habit formation, and cognition. Ethics evaluates voluntary human conduct through normative standards of moral good and evil.
  • Points of Intersection:
    • Volition and Agency: Moral responsibility presupposes psychological agency. Ethics relies on psychology to establish whether an action was voluntary, intentional, or performed under cognitive impairment.
    • Moral Psychology: Ethical theories require psychological plausibility regarding moral motivation, guilt, empathy, and moral development (e.g., Kohlberg's stages).

Analytical Summary: Psychology explains the causal antecedents of human choice; Ethics evaluates the justification of those choices.

Short Question • 5 Marks

5. Perfectionism (Self-Realization Theory)

Undergraduate Standard Answer:

Perfectionism (Self-Realization Ethics) is a teleological moral theory endorsed by Aristotle, Spinoza, Hegel, T.H. Green, and F.H. Bradley. It posits that the supreme moral good consists in the complete, harmonious realization of human nature and potential.

  • Synthesis of Extremes: Perfectionism explicitly rejects both Hedonism (which reduces human good to sensory pleasure) and Asceticism (which suppresses fundamental human drives). It advocates for the rational integration and optimal expression of intellectual, physical, emotional, and social capacities.
  • Individual and Social Ideal: Self-realization is not an egoistic pursuit. As T.H. Green and F.H. Bradley argued, an individual realizes their true rational self through active participation in a moral social organism ("My Station and Its Duties").

3. Long Essay Questions (12.5 Marks Each)

Target: ~500-600 Words
Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

1. Aristotle's Virtue Ethics, Properties, and the Doctrine of the Golden Mean

1. Teleological & Agent-Centered Framework:

In the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle formulates a teleological, agent-centered normative framework. Unlike modern act-centered theories (Deontology and Utilitarianism) that focus on evaluating isolated actions, Aristotle asks: "What constitutes a flourishing human life?" Every human action aims at some good, culminating in an ultimate, self-sufficient end (Telos): Eudaimonia (flourishing or living well).

2. Function Argument (Ergon) & Definition of Virtue:

Aristotle derives the human good through the Function Argument. The distinct function of human beings—distinguishing them from plants (nutritive) and animals (perceptual)—is the activity of the soul in accordance with reason. Therefore, human excellence (Arête) is performing this rational function excellently over a complete lifespan.

  • Intellectual Virtues: Acquired via instruction; includes Sophia (theoretical wisdom) and Phronesis (practical wisdom).
  • Moral Virtues: Acquired through habituation (Ethos); stable character dispositions (Hexis) to feel and act appropriately.

3. The Doctrine of the Golden Mean (Mesotes):

Aristotle defines moral virtue as a settled state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean between two extremes—a vice of excess and a vice of deficiency—relative to us, as determined by a rational principle formulated by a person of practical wisdom (Phronimos).

Deficiency (Vice) Golden Mean (Virtue) Excess (Vice)
Cowardice (Fear rules) Courage (Andreia) Rashness (Blind confidence)
Insensibility (Suppression) Temperance (Sophrosyne) Licentiousness (Overindulgence)
Stinginess / Meanness Generosity (Eleutheriotes) Extravagance / Profligacy
Pusillanimity (Smallness of mind) Magnanimity (Megalopsychia) Vanity / Arrogance

4. Philosophical Evaluation & Critique:

Strengths: Integrates cognitive reasoning with moral emotions; recognizes that ethics requires context-sensitive practical wisdom rather than rigid algorithmics.
Critiques: Lacks concrete decision procedures for moral dilemmas; the concept of the "mean" is susceptible to cultural relativism; Aristotle explicitly notes that certain actions (e.g., murder, adultery) are intrinsically evil and possess no Golden Mean.

Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

2. Free Will and Ethics: Supporting Arguments, Philosophical Positions, and Moral Agency

1. The Metaphysical Foundation of Moral Responsibility:

Free will—the capacity of rational agents to choose between genuine alternatives without external coercion—is the indispensable postulate of moral philosophy. If human choices are merely deterministic outcomes of physical causation, moral evaluation (praise, blame, reward, and punishment) becomes logically incoherent.

2. Comparative Philosophical Spectrum:

  • Hard Determinism (Incompatibilism): Universal physical laws and prior causal states dictate all events, including human choices. Free will is an illusion; moral responsibility is rejected.
  • Libertarianism (Incompatibilism): Human agents possess non-causal or agent-causal power to choose independently of prior physical antecedents. Genuine free will exists.
  • Compatibilism / Soft Determinism (Hobbes, Hume): Free will is compatible with physical determinism. An action is free as long as the agent acts according to internal desires without external compulsion, even if those desires are causally conditioned.

3. Arguments Establishing Free Will as an Ethical Postulate:

  1. Kant's Postulate: "Ought Implies Can" (Sollen fordert Können): Immanuel Kant argued that a moral command ("You ought to perform action X") logically implies that the agent possesses the operational freedom to perform or refrain from X. Prescribing duty to a deterministic automaton is a category mistake.
  2. Phenomenological Argument from Moral Consciousness: Human decision-making involves immediate experiential awareness of rational deliberation, choice, and subsequent post-action feelings of remorse or moral satisfaction.
  3. Legal and Penological Necessity: Criminal jurisprudence relies on the doctrine of Mens Rea (guilty mind). Society holds individuals liable only when actions are voluntary.
Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

3. Philosophy's Connection in Ethics & Necessity of Studying Ethics in Contemporary Society

1. Ethics as Meta-Philosophical Inquiry:

Ethics is not an isolated domain; it is deeply embedded within the core branches of Philosophy:

  • Epistemological Connection: Investigates moral cognition—how do we justify moral knowledge? (Via Rationalism, Empiricism, Intuitionism, or Divine Revelation?).
  • Metaphysical Connection: Examines the ontological status of moral facts (Moral Realism vs. Anti-Realism) and the nature of human personal identity and agency.

2. The Imperative Necessity of Applied Ethics in Current Society:

Modern society faces complex disruptions where technological advancement outpaces traditional moral standards:

  1. Artificial Intelligence & Algorithmic Governance: Autonomous systems require ethical programming concerning algorithmic bias, data privacy, accountability in autonomous weapons, and technological displacement.
  2. Bioethics & Genetic Engineering: Gene editing technologies (CRISPR), reproductive technologies, and end-of-life care mandate clear deontological and utilitarian guidelines to preserve human dignity.
  3. Environmental Crisis & Intergenerational Justice: Climate change necessitates expanding traditional anthropocentric ethics to include ecological preservation, animal rights, and duty to future human generations.
  4. Pluralism & Moral Relativism: Globalized, multicultural societies require rigorous normative frameworks to negotiate ideological conflict, defend universal human rights, and reinforce civic virtue.
Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

4. Kant's Categorical Imperative vs. Hypothetical Imperative & The Three Formulations

1. Categorical vs. Hypothetical Imperatives:

Hypothetical Imperative Categorical Imperative
Conditional command ("If you desire X, do Y"). Unconditional command ("Act X").
Grounded in subjective desire, utility, or end goals. Grounded in pure practical reason binding on all agents.
Instrumental value (Means to an end). Intrinsic moral obligation (Duty in itself).

2. The Three Formulations of the Categorical Imperative:

  1. Formula of Universal Law: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
    • Analytical Test: Requires evaluating if a action's maxim causes a Contradiction in Conception (e.g., making a lying promise destroys the concept of promising) or a Contradiction in the Will (e.g., refusing to help others in need).
  2. Formula of Humanity as an End-in-Itself: "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end."
    • Asserts the absolute, non-instrumental dignity (Würde) of rational agents, forbidding coercion and exploitation.
  3. Formula of Autonomy / Kingdom of Ends: "Act according to maxims of a universally legislating member of a merely possible kingdom of ends."
    • Envisions a community of rational beings where every individual acts as both lawgiver and subject under universal moral laws.
Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

5. Nature of Ethical Dilemmas, Contemporary Manifestations, Causes, and Resolution Frameworks

1. Theoretical Nature of an Ethical Dilemma:

A genuine ethical dilemma occurs when a moral agent is bound by two or more conflicting moral obligations, but can fulfill only one. Fulfilling obligation A forces the violation of obligation B, resulting in unavoidable moral residue or distress regardless of the choice made.

2. Contemporary Case Studies:

  • Data Privacy vs. National Security: Mass digital surveillance vs. individual rights to privacy and autonomy.
  • Medical Resource Allocation (Triage Ethics): Allocating scarce life-saving resources (ICU beds, organs) during health emergencies—Utilitarian maximization of lives vs. Deontological equal rights to care.
  • Economic Growth vs. Climate Mitigation: Industrializing developing nations to eliminate poverty vs. strict global carbon budgets.

3. Underlying Causes:

Dilemmas stem from pluralistic value systems (clash between Utilitarian, Deontological, and Virtue principles), technological innovation outpacing policy, and structural resource limits.

4. Systemic Resolution Principles:

Dilemmas are resolved by applying structured decision models: establishing clear duty hierarchies (e.g., Ross's Prima Facie duties), conducting impact analyses, enforcing institutional ethics boards, and maintaining institutional transparency.

Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

6. Ethical Theories of Punishment & The Capital Punishment Debate

1. The Justification Problem of Legal Punishment:

Punishment involves state-sanctioned deprivation of liberty, rights, or life. Ethically, inflicting harm is prima facie wrong unless justified by a sound normative framework.

2. The Three Classic Doctrines of Punishment:

  1. Retributive Theory (Kant, Hegel - Retrospective): Punishment is justified because the offender deserves it (Jus Talionis). It is a moral end in itself that balances the scales of justice and respects the criminal's rational agency.
  2. Deterrent Theory (Bentham, Utilitarianism - Prospective): Punishment is an evil justified only if it prevents greater future crime through general deterrence, individual deterrence, and incapacitation.
  3. Reformative / Rehabilitative Theory (Humanistic Model): Views crime as a symptom of psychological or social pathology. Focuses on moral education, therapy, and social integration.

3. Academic Argumentation on Capital Punishment:

  • Pro-Abolitionist Position (Recommended Academic Stance): Capital punishment violates absolute human dignity, risks irreversible miscarriages of justice, and lacks empirical evidence of superior deterrence over life imprisonment.
  • Retentionist Counter-Argument: Extreme heinous acts forfeit the right to life; retributive justice demands proportional retribution.
Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

7. Utilitarianism as Hedonism: Bentham vs. Mill Comparative Analysis

1. Utilitarianism's Hedonistic Foundations:

Utilitarianism is a consequentialist, universalistic hedonism holding that pleasure (happiness) is the sole intrinsic good, and pain the sole intrinsic evil. It mandates maximizing the net balance of pleasure for the greatest number.

2. Bentham vs. Mill Comparative Analysis:

Dimension Jeremy Bentham (Quantitative) John Stuart Mill (Qualitative)
Nature of Pleasure Strictly quantitative. "Quantity of pleasure being equal, push-pin is as good as poetry." Qualitative distinction between Higher (intellectual/moral) and Lower (bodily) pleasures.
Measurement Hedonic Calculus (Intensity, Duration, Certainty, Propinquity, Fecundity, Purity, Extent). Evaluation by "Competent Judges" with experience in both.
Ethical Model Act Utilitarianism: Assess utility for each individual action. Foundation for Rule Utilitarianism: Adhere to moral rules that maximize utility over time.

3. Thesis on Modern Relevance:

John Stuart Mill's Qualitative Utilitarianism is far more viable in modern political and legal ethics. Bentham's quantitative approach risks justifying the tyranny of the majority (e.g., oppressing a minority to pleasure a crowd). Mill's framework integrates individual liberty (On Liberty), constitutional rights, and intellectual cultivation into utility, aligning with modern human rights ethics.

Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

8. Divine Command Theory & Theological Utilitarianism

1. Divine Command Theory (Theological Voluntarism):

Divine Command Theory holds that moral values are derived directly from divine commands. Moral rightness equals obedience to God's will; moral wrongness equals disobedience.

2. Theological Utilitarianism (Paley & Gay):

Theological Utilitarianism bridges divine law and hedonism:

  • Premise: God is omnibenevolent and wills the happiness of all creation.
  • Synthesis: Divine commands are not arbitrary laws, but moral guidelines designed to promote general human happiness. Divine sanctions (heavenly rewards / hellish punishments) provide rational motivation for individuals to align self-interest with the public good.
Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

9. Plato's Euthyphro Dilemma & The Essentialist Solution

1. The Dilemma Stated:

In Plato's dialogue Euthyphro, Socrates asks: "Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?"

2. Analytical Critique of Both Horns:

  • Horn 1: Voluntarism (It is good because God commands it):
    • Fatal Flaw: Morality becomes arbitrary. If God commanded murder, it would become morally obligatory. Reduces "God is good" to a tautology ("God commands what He commands").
  • Horn 2: Intellectualism (God commands it because it is good):
    • Fatal Flaw: Morality is independent of God. The Good becomes an external standard to which God must conform, compromising divine sovereignty.

3. The Essentialist / Aquinas Solution:

Philosophers resolve the dilemma by rejecting both horns: Morality is neither dependent on arbitrary divine commands nor external to God. Morality is grounded in God's Immutable Essential Nature. God commands what is good because His nature is the ultimate standard of Goodness itself.

Essay Question • 12.5 Marks

10. Intuitionism: G.E. Moore's Naturalistic Fallacy & W.D. Ross's Prima Facie Duties

1. Epistemological Premise of Intuitionism:

Intuitionism is a non-naturalist cognitivist meta-ethical theory holding that basic moral truths are self-evident and known directly through rational intuition, independent of empirical observation or deduction.

2. G.E. Moore & The Naturalistic Fallacy:

In Principia Ethica (1903), G.E. Moore argued that "Good" is a simple, unanalyzable, non-natural property (analogous to "Yellow"). Attempting to define "Good" in terms of natural properties (e.g., "Pleasure" or "Evolution") commits the Naturalistic Fallacy, as demonstrated by the Open Question Argument.

3. W.D. Ross & Prima Facie Duties:

In The Right and the Good (1930), W.D. Ross addressed moral conflict by distinguishing between:

  • Prima Facie Duties: Conditional duties binding at first glance (Fidelity, Reparation, Gratitude, Justice, Beneficence, Self-Improvement, Non-Maleficence).
  • Actual Duty: The concrete obligation determined by weighing competing prima facie duties in a specific context.
