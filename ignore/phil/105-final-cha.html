1. Core Logic: Basic Knowledge You Should Know

Big picture: Logic studies arguments and the standards by which arguments can be evaluated as correct or incorrect. It is not primarily the study of whether the subject matter is true; it is the study of whether conclusions follow from premises in the required way.

Argument

An argument is a group of propositions in which one proposition, the conclusion, is claimed to follow from one or more other propositions, the premises.

Signal words for premises: because, since, for, as, given that.

Signal words for conclusions: therefore, hence, so, thus, consequently.

Proposition

A proposition is what is asserted by a declarative sentence and is capable of being true or false.

Questions, commands, and exclamations are not propositions because they do not assert something that is true or false.

Deduction vs. Induction

Deductive inferenceInductive inference
Claims that the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises.Claims that the premises make the conclusion probable or well supported.
Evaluated mainly by validity (and, for a sound argument, truth of premises).Evaluated by the degree of inductive strength / support.
Example: All humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore Socrates is mortal.Example: The observed crows have been black; therefore the next crow observed will probably be black.
Exam habit: Never write “valid = true.” Validity belongs to arguments; truth/falsity belongs to propositions.

2. What Is Logic? Discuss the Central Problem

Exam-ready answer

According to Copi, logic is the study of the methods and principles used to distinguish correct reasoning from incorrect reasoning. Logic therefore deals with reasoning rather than with any one particular subject matter. Its concern is to provide standards by which arguments may be analyzed and evaluated.

The central problem of logic is the problem of determining whether a conclusion follows from its premises in the appropriate way. In a deductive argument, the key question is whether the premises entail the conclusion; in an inductive argument, the question is whether the premises provide sufficient or probable support for the conclusion.

Core distinction: Logic asks “Does the conclusion follow from the premises?” It does not merely ask “Are the statements true?”

Three basic functions of logic

  1. To clarify and analyze reasoning: identify premises, conclusion, and logical structure.
  2. To provide standards for evaluation: give criteria for distinguishing good reasoning from bad reasoning.
  3. To detect and avoid error: expose invalid inference and common fallacies so that reasoning can be improved.
5-mark version: Define logic in one sentence, explain that its central problem is the relation between premises and conclusion, then state the three functions above with one sentence each.

4. Relation Between Premises and Conclusion: Truth vs. Validity

Premise-conclusion relation

In an argument, the premises are the propositions offered as reasons for accepting the conclusion. The logical question is whether the conclusion follows from those premises. That relation is called validity in deductive logic.

Truth

Truth/falsity is a property of propositions.

Example: “Dhaka is the capital of Bangladesh” is either true or false.

Validity

Validity/invalidity is a property of arguments.

An argument is valid when there is no possible case in which all its premises are true and its conclusion is false.

Example showing why truth and validity are different

All fish are mammals. All whales are fish. Therefore, all whales are mammals.

The form is valid. But the first premise is false. So the argument is valid but unsound.

All birds are animals. All dogs are animals. Therefore, all dogs are birds.

Here the premises may be true, but the conclusion does not follow. The argument is invalid.

All mammals are animals. All whales are mammals. Therefore, all whales are animals.

This is valid and sound, because the inference is valid and the premises are true.

Key sentence to memorize: “Truth is a property of propositions; validity is a property of arguments. A valid argument may have false premises, whereas a proposition cannot be called valid or invalid.”

5. Standard-Form Categorical Propositions

A standard-form categorical proposition has a subject term (S), a predicate term (P), a quantifier/copula pattern, and one of four forms:

FormPatternNameQualityQuantityDistribution
AAll S are P.Universal affirmativeAffirmativeUniversalS distributed; P undistributed
ENo S are P.Universal negativeNegativeUniversalS distributed; P distributed
ISome S are P.Particular affirmativeAffirmativeParticularS undistributed; P undistributed
OSome S are not P.Particular negativeNegativeParticularS undistributed; P distributed

Quality

Affirmative propositions assert inclusion or agreement between S and P. Negative propositions deny that relation.

Quantity

Universal propositions speak about all members of the subject class. Particular propositions speak about some member(s).

Distribution

A term is distributed if the proposition is about every member of that term's class.

Question: “All voters are citizens.” → A proposition. Voters: distributed. Citizens: undistributed.
Question: “Some citizens are not voters.” → O proposition. Citizens: undistributed. Voters: distributed.

Mnemonic

A: Subject only. E: Both. I: Neither. O: Predicate only.

6. Conversion, Obversion & Contraposition

Conversion

Swap S and P.

E and I: valid.

A: valid by limitation in the traditional treatment when existential assumptions permit.

O: invalid.

Obversion

Keep subject, change quality, and replace predicate by its complement.

Valid for A, E, I, O.

Example: All S are P → No S are non-P.

Contraposition

Interchange subject and predicate and replace both by their complements in the appropriate way.

A and O: valid.

E and I: generally invalid.

OriginalConversionObversionContraposition
All S are P.All P are S. (not generally valid)No S are non-P. (valid)All non-P are non-S. (valid)
No S are P.No P are S. (valid)All S are non-P. (valid)Not generally valid as a simple categorical inference
Some S are P.Some P are S. (valid)Some S are not non-P. (valid)Not generally valid
Some S are not P.Some P are not S. (invalid)Some S are non-P. (valid)Some non-P are not non-S. (valid)
Do not memorize only the names. In an exam, show the operation: what was swapped, what was negated/complemented, and whether the inference is valid.

Existential import and Boolean interpretation

Existential import concerns whether a proposition asserts or presupposes that its subject class has members. Under the traditional interpretation, universal categorical propositions were often understood to carry existential import. Under the Boolean interpretation, universal A and E propositions do not imply that their subject classes actually have members.

This difference matters especially for immediate inference rules such as conversion by limitation and for syllogistic validity involving empty classes.

7. Standard-Form Categorical Syllogism & Venn Diagram Technique

Definition

A standard-form categorical syllogism is a deductive argument consisting of exactly three categorical propositions: two premises and one conclusion, involving exactly three terms. Each term occurs twice.

Three terms

TermMeaning
Major term (P)Predicate of the conclusion.
Minor term (S)Subject of the conclusion.
Middle term (M)Occurs in both premises but not in the conclusion.

Characteristics of a syllogism

  • Three categorical propositions: two premises and one conclusion.
  • Exactly three terms: S, P, and M.
  • Each term occurs twice.
  • The middle term links the minor and major terms in the premises.
  • The syllogism has a mood (A/E/I/O pattern) and a figure (based on the position of M).

Figures

FigureMajor premiseMinor premiseConclusion
1M-PS-MS-P
2P-MS-MS-P
3M-PM-SS-P
4P-MM-SS-P

Venn Diagram technique - exam procedure

  1. Draw three overlapping circles labelled S, P, and M.
  2. Represent the premises only: shade regions that must be empty and place an X where existence is asserted.
  3. Do not diagram the conclusion initially.
  4. Check whether the conclusion is already forced by the completed premise diagram.
  5. If the conclusion is forced, the syllogism is valid; if not, it is invalid.

Requested examples

IAI-3

Figure 3: Major = I, minor = A, conclusion = I.

Read as: Some M are P; All M are S; therefore, Some S are P.

From “Some M are P,” place an X in the M∩P region. “All M are S” forces every M to be inside S. Therefore that existing M∩P object must also lie in S∩P. The conclusion “Some S are P” is forced.

Result: VALID.

EAE-3

Read as: No M are P; All M are S; therefore, No S are P.

The premises only restrict M. S may still contain members outside M that are also P. Therefore the conclusion is not forced.

Result: INVALID.

8. Fallacies - Types, Definitions & Examples

Definition: A fallacy is a kind of erroneous reasoning. A fallacious argument may look persuasive while failing to provide the required logical support.

A. Fallacies of Relevance

FallacyWhat happensExample
Argumentum ad Baculum / Appeal to ForceThreat or force is used instead of relevant reasons.“Agree with my proposal or you will lose your job.”
Argumentum ad Hominem - AbusiveAttacks the person rather than the argument.“His economic argument is false because he is stupid.”
Argumentum ad Hominem - CircumstantialDismisses a claim by pointing to the speaker’s circumstances or interests.“You support environmental rules only because you work for an environmental group.”
Argumentum ad Hominem - Tu QuoqueRejects criticism because the critic is accused of doing the same thing.“You cannot say cheating is wrong; you cheated last year.”
Argumentum ad Ignorantiam / Appeal to IgnoranceClaims a proposition is true merely because it has not been proved false, or false because it has not been proved true.“Nobody has proved aliens do not exist, so they must exist.”
Argumentum ad Misericordiam / Appeal to PityUses pity as a substitute for relevant evidence.“Please give me full marks; my family will be disappointed.”
Argumentum ad Populum / Appeal to the PeopleUses popularity, emotion, or crowd approval instead of relevant support.“Millions believe it, so it must be true.”
Red HerringIntroduces an irrelevant issue that diverts attention from the original point.Question: “Did you falsify the report?” Answer: “Our company has created thousands of jobs.”
Missing the Point / Ignoring the QuestionThe premises may support a different conclusion from the one being argued for.“We need stricter traffic enforcement, therefore we should close the city park.”
Straw ManReplaces an opponent’s real claim with a weaker or distorted version, then attacks that version.“She wants fewer exams, so she wants students to learn nothing.”

B. Fallacies of Presumption

FallacyWhat happensExample
AccidentApplies a general rule to an exceptional case where the rule does not properly fit.“Freedom of speech is always good; therefore a person should be allowed to shout a fire alarm falsely in a crowded theater.”
Converse Accident / Hasty GeneralizationMoves from an insufficient or unrepresentative sample to a broad generalization.“Two tourists were rude, so tourists are rude.”
False CauseTreats an inadequate or merely correlated cause as the true cause.“I wore this shirt and we won the match; the shirt caused the victory.”
Begging the QuestionThe conclusion is assumed, directly or indirectly, in the premises.“This law is just because it is a fair law.”
Complex QuestionTwo or more questions are packed into one so that answering it appears to concede an unjustified assumption.“Have you stopped cheating on exams?”

C. Fallacies of Ambiguity

FallacyWhat happensExample
EquivocationA key word or phrase changes meaning during the argument.“A bank is on the river; a river bank is money-related; therefore...”
AmphibolyAmbiguity comes from grammatical structure rather than a single word.“I saw the student with the telescope” used with two different possible structures.
AccentMeaning changes because emphasis or wording is shifted in a way that alters interpretation.Quoting only the emphasized fragment of a sentence to imply a different claim.
CompositionInfers something about the whole from properties of parts.“Every player is excellent; therefore the team must be excellent.”
DivisionInfers something about parts merely because it is true of the whole.“The company is rich; therefore every employee is rich.”

D. Weak or defective inductive reasoning

For Copi-style exam preparation, remember that inductive fallacies arise when the evidence is insufficient, unrepresentative, irrelevant, or misinterpreted. The names above are the ones most likely to appear when the course asks specifically for “fallacies.”

How to answer a fallacy question: (1) define the fallacy, (2) show the defective reasoning pattern, (3) give a fresh example, (4) explain in one sentence why the premises fail to justify the conclusion.

9. Inductive Inference & Mill's Methods of Experimental Enquiry

Inductive argument

An inductive argument is one in which the premises are intended to make the conclusion probable rather than guarantee it. Its conclusion goes beyond the information explicitly contained in the premises.

Mill's five methods

MethodCore ideaSimple illustration
Method of AgreementFind a factor common to all cases in which the phenomenon occurs.Several people become ill after different meals; the common food item is suspected.
Method of DifferenceCompare a case where the phenomenon occurs with a similar case where it does not; the differing factor is treated as causally relevant.Two otherwise similar groups, one receiving a treatment and one not.
Joint Method of Agreement and DifferenceCombines the two methods: the suspected factor is present in positive cases and absent in negative cases.Factor appears in every sick case and never in comparable healthy cases.
Method of ResiduesAfter known causes explain known portions of an effect, the remaining portion is attributed to the remaining causal factor.Subtract known contributors from a measured total to identify a residual cause.
Method of Concomitant VariationsChanges in one factor are associated with regular changes in another.As temperature rises, a physical measurement changes systematically.

Limitations of Mill's methods

  • Real phenomena often have multiple interacting causes.
  • It may be impossible to control or hold all other factors constant.
  • A correlation or regular variation does not by itself prove a unique causal explanation.
  • Experimental situations can be too complex for clean agreement/difference comparisons.
  • Results depend on how the cases are selected and described; hidden conditions can mislead the inquiry.
  • These methods are best understood as systematic procedures for discovering and testing causal hypotheses, not as magical guarantees of certainty.

10. Probability & Analogical Argument

Analogical argument

An analogical argument reasons from similarity. It points out that two or more cases resemble one another in relevant respects and then infers that they are likely to resemble one another in another respect as well.

Object A has properties p, q, r, and s. Object B has properties p, q, and r. Therefore, B probably has property s.

Strength depends on: the number of relevant similarities, their degree of relevance, the absence of important dissimilarities, and the diversity/representativeness of the comparison cases.

Theories / interpretations of probability

ApproachCore ideaMain strengthMain limitation
Classical / mathematicalProbability is based on ratios among equally possible outcomes.Clear for symmetric games and finite equally likely cases.“Equally possible” can be difficult to justify outside idealized cases.
Statistical / relative-frequencyProbability is understood through relative frequency in a sufficiently large class or sequence of cases.Connects probability to empirical data.Single-case probabilities and the exact long-run interpretation can be difficult to handle.

Coin problems

For a fair coin: P(T on one toss) = 1/2 P(TTT in 3 tosses) = (1/2)^3 = 1/8 P(at least one H in 3 tosses) = 1 - P(no H) = 1 - (1/2)^3 = 7/8

The complement method is usually the quickest: “at least one head” = 1 - “no heads.”

11. Previous-Year Questions - Separate Full Answers

These are written in an exam-ready style. For long-mark questions, start with a direct definition, then develop the explanation, then give the example/evaluation.

Group A - 5-mark questions

1. What is Logic? Explain after Copi, the central problem with which logic deals.

Answer: Logic, according to Copi, is the study of the methods and principles used to distinguish correct reasoning from incorrect reasoning. Logic therefore concerns the evaluation of arguments rather than any particular subject matter. Its central problem is to determine whether a conclusion follows from its premises in the appropriate logical way. In deductive reasoning, the issue is whether the premises entail the conclusion; in inductive reasoning, the issue is whether the premises provide adequate probable support. Logic thus supplies standards by which reasoning can be examined, criticized, and improved.

2. Discuss briefly, the three basic functions of logic.

Answer: The three basic functions may be stated as: (1) analysis - identify the propositions, premises, conclusion, and logical structure of reasoning; (2) evaluation - apply logical standards to determine whether an inference is correct, valid, strong, or defective; and (3) error detection and correction - recognize fallacies and other defects so that reasoning can be made more reliable.

3. What do you mean by analogical argument?

Answer: An analogical argument is an inductive argument in which similarities between two or more cases are used as evidence for a further similarity. If object A and object B share several relevant properties, and A has another property, the argument infers that B probably has that property too. Its strength depends on the number and relevance of the similarities, the lack of significant dissimilarities, and the representativeness of the comparison.

Group B - Long questions

1. According to Copi, explain the relation between premises and conclusion. Discuss truth and validity. (12.5 marks)

Answer: An argument consists of premises and a conclusion. The premises are propositions put forward as reasons for accepting the conclusion, while the conclusion is the proposition the argument seeks to establish. In a deductive argument, the essential logical relation is that the conclusion must follow from the premises in such a way that it is impossible for all the premises to be true while the conclusion is false. This property is called validity.

Truth and validity must be carefully distinguished. Truth is a property of propositions; a proposition is true when it corresponds to the facts, and false otherwise. Validity is a property of arguments; it concerns the logical relation between premises and conclusion. Therefore an argument can be valid even if one or more premises are false.

Example: “All fish are mammals. All whales are fish. Therefore, all whales are mammals.” The form is valid, but the first premise is false, so the argument is unsound. Conversely, an argument can have true premises and a true conclusion yet still be invalid if the conclusion does not follow from those premises.

Thus, truth concerns individual propositions, while validity concerns the structure of an argument. A sound deductive argument requires both valid reasoning and true premises.

2. What is Logical Definition? Explain with examples. (3.5 marks)

Answer: A logical definition is a definition designed to state the meaning of a term with precision by identifying the attributes that are essential and applying to all and only the objects denoted by the term. A good logical definition should be neither too broad nor too narrow.

Example: “A triangle is a plane figure bounded by three straight lines.” This aims to identify the essential characteristics of a triangle rather than merely describing one example.

3. Explain the rules of definition and the fallacies involved in violating them. (9.5 marks)

Answer:

  1. A definition should state essential attributes where an intensional definition is intended. Violation can produce a definition by accidental characteristics rather than the defining ones.
  2. A definition should not be circular. The defining expression should not depend on the very term being defined. Otherwise the explanation does not genuinely explain the term. Fallacy: circular definition.
  3. A definition should be equivalent in denotation to the term defined. It should not be too broad or too narrow. Fallacies: overly broad definition and overly narrow definition.
  4. A definition should not be obscure or ambiguous. Replacing one unclear expression by another does not increase understanding. Fallacy: obscure/ambiguous definition.
  5. A definition should not normally be figurative when literal precision is required. A metaphor may be memorable but fail to state exact meaning. Fallacy: figurative definition used as a logical definition.
  6. A definition should not be negative when an affirmative definition is available. Saying what a thing is not may fail to identify what it is. Fallacy: improper negative definition.

4. What is fallacy? Discuss, with examples, the fallacies of relevance. (12.5 marks)

Answer: A fallacy is a kind of erroneous reasoning. In a fallacious argument, the reasoning fails to provide the logical support required for the conclusion, even though it may seem persuasive.

Fallacies of relevance occur when premises are not logically relevant to the conclusion, even though they may create the appearance of support. Important examples include:

  • Appeal to force: replaces reasons with threat or coercion.
  • Abusive ad hominem: attacks the person rather than the person's argument.
  • Circumstantial ad hominem: rejects an argument because of the speaker's circumstances or interests.
  • Tu quoque: answers criticism by accusing the critic of similar conduct.
  • Appeal to ignorance: treats lack of proof as proof of the opposite.
  • Appeal to pity: uses pity instead of relevant evidence.
  • Appeal to the people: relies on popularity or crowd emotion rather than relevant grounds.
  • Red herring: diverts attention to an irrelevant issue.
  • Missing the point: premises support a different conclusion from the one actually offered.

Example: “You should accept my political argument because thousands of people agree with me.” The popularity of a belief does not itself establish its truth, so the argument may commit an appeal to the people.

Answer: A standard-form categorical proposition is composed of a subject term and a predicate term connected in one of four standard patterns: A, E, I, and O. Its quality is affirmative or negative; its quantity is universal or particular; and its distribution indicates whether a term is used to refer to every member of the class it denotes.

6. Conversion, obversion, contraposition; existential import and Boolean interpretation. (12.5 marks)

Answer: Conversion interchanges subject and predicate. “No S are P” converts validly to “No P are S”; “Some S are P” converts validly to “Some P are S.” Under traditional treatment, the universal affirmative may convert by limitation when existential import is assumed.

Obversion changes the quality of the proposition and replaces the predicate by its complement. It is valid for all four forms. For example, “All S are P” becomes “No S are non-P.”

Contraposition interchanges the subject and predicate while complementing both. It is valid for A and O forms in the standard treatment.

Existential import concerns whether a universal proposition presupposes that its subject class has members. Under the Boolean interpretation, universal propositions do not carry existential import. Thus “All S are P” can be true even when there are no S's.

7. What is a standard-form categorical syllogism? Mention characteristics. (7.5 marks)

Answer: A standard-form categorical syllogism is a deductive argument consisting of two premises and one conclusion, all categorical propositions, containing exactly three terms. The terms are the major term (predicate of the conclusion), minor term (subject of the conclusion), and middle term (the term appearing in both premises but not in the conclusion). Each term occurs twice. A syllogism also has a mood, determined by the A/E/I/O forms of its propositions, and a figure, determined by the position of the middle term.

8. Test IAI-3 and EAE-3 using Venn Diagram Technique. (5 marks)

IAI-3: Some M are P; All M are S; therefore Some S are P. The X required by the particular premise lies inside M∩P, and the universal premise places all M inside S. Therefore the X must also be inside S∩P. VALID.

9. Mill's method of experimental enquiry and limitations. (12.5 marks)

Answer: J. S. Mill formulated five methods for investigating causal relations:

  1. Agreement: identify a factor common to all cases where the phenomenon occurs.
  2. Difference: compare a positive case with a similar negative case; the crucial difference is treated as causally relevant.
  3. Joint agreement and difference: combine the two procedures.
  4. Residues: subtract known causal contributions from a total effect to identify the remaining cause.
  5. Concomitant variations: infer causal connection from regular variation between factors.

Limitations: natural phenomena often have several interacting causes; complete control is difficult; cases may contain hidden differences; regular association is not automatically proof of unique causation; and complex systems may not fit the simplified conditions assumed by the methods. Therefore Mill's methods are powerful tools for causal inquiry but are not infallible substitutes for careful evidence and theory.

10. Explain and evaluate theories of probability. Probability questions. (12.5 marks)

Answer: The classical interpretation treats probability as a ratio based on equally possible outcomes. Its advantage is clarity in symmetrical, finite situations such as fair dice or coins, but its limitation is the difficulty of establishing genuine equal possibility in many real cases.

The statistical or relative-frequency interpretation connects probability with the frequency of occurrence in a sufficiently large class or sequence. Its strength is its empirical character, but it raises questions about single-case probabilities and the exact interpretation of limiting frequency.

Three tosses, tails every time: for a fair coin, each tail has probability 1/2, and the tosses are independent, so \(P(TTT)=(1/2)^3=1/8\).

At least one head in three tosses: use the complement. \(P(\text{at least one H})=1-P(\text{no H})=1-(1/2)^3=7/8\).

How to Write High-Scoring Logic Answers

  1. Define first. Start with one clean textbook-style sentence.
  2. Give the structure. Use headings such as meaning, explanation, example, evaluation.
  3. Use the exact logical vocabulary. Example: say “valid argument,” not “true argument.”
  4. For categorical logic, write the form. A/E/I/O plus distribution.
  5. For fallacies, always include an example. The example shows the examiner that you understand the error.
  6. For long questions, end with a conclusion. One final sentence should state the exact result.
Golden rule: In logic exams, a definition + precise explanation + example + correct terminology is much stronger than a vague paragraph of general discussion.
