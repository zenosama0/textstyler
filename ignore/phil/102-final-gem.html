Muslim Philosophy Exam Study Guide

Based on Muslim Philosophy by Syed Abdul Hai and History of Islamic Philosophy by Majid Fakhry. Standard solutions for all previous year questions and practice questions.

1. Foundational Framework Overview

Muslim Philosophy vs. Islamic Philosophy:

  • Muslim Philosophy: Broader term encompassing all philosophical output produced by Muslim scholars or within Islamic civilization, including Hellenistic (Peripatetic/Mashsha'i), scientific, and socio-political thought.
  • Islamic Philosophy: Strictly refers to philosophy rooted directly in Islamic revelation (Quran and Sunnah), focused primarily on theological alignment (Kalam), divine unity, and prophetic revelation.

Major Categorization of Schools:

  • Theological Schools (Kalam): Mu'tazilites, Ash'arites, Maturidites, Tahawites, Jabrites, Qadarites, Murji'ites, Sifatiyas.
  • Philosophical Schools (Falsafa/Mashsha'i): Rationalist Hellenistic thinkers (Al-Kindi, Al-Farabi, Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd).
  • Political-Religious Movements: Kharijites, Shia (Ithna Ashariya), Sunni.
  • Mystical Tradition (Tasawwuf): Sufi doctrines of divine love, self-annihilation (Fana), and spiritual path (Tariqah).
2. Previous Year Questions (5 Marks) Short Notes
Q1. Discuss different aspects of Islam as a complete code of life.

Islam is categorized not merely as a set of personal rituals, but as a holistic system (Din) governing all spheres of human existence:

  • Spiritual & Doctrinal (Aqidah): Establishes monotheism (Tawhid), belief in prophets, scriptures, angels, and the Day of Judgment.
  • Moral & Ethical (Akhlaq): Regulates individual character, virtue, honesty, modesty, and social righteousness.
  • Legal & Transactional (Mu'amalat): Provides detailed Jurisprudence (Fiqh) for contracts, trade, marriage, inheritance, and criminal justice.
  • Socio-Political (Siyasah): Outlines principles of governance, justice ('Adl), consultation (Shura), and communal duty.
Q2. Who are Murjias? Describe in short their standpoint on the status of Muslims who commit sin.

The Murji'as (from Irja, meaning "postponement") emerged during the early political civil wars (Fitna) as a theological party advocating neutrality.

  • Core Standpoint on Sinners: In sharp contrast to the Kharijites (who declared grave sinners to be apostates/Kafir), the Murji'ites held that a Muslim who commits a grave sin (Kabirah) remains a true believer (Mu'min).
  • Postponement of Judgment: They argued that human action ('Amal) is secondary to internal faith (Iman). Therefore, the ultimate status of a grave sinner is postponed to Allah alone on the Day of Judgment.
Q3. Who are the Ithna Ashariya (The Twelvers)?

The Ithna Ashariya (Twelver Shia) represents the largest branch of Shi'ism.

  • Definition: They derive their name from their belief in twelve divinely ordained spiritual and political leaders (Imams) originating from the Prophet's family (Ahl al-Bayt), beginning with Ali ibn Abi Talib.
  • Core Beliefs: They hold that the 12th Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, entered a state of Occultation (Ghaybah) in 874 CE and will return before Judgment Day to restore justice.
  • Doctrinal Pillars: Absolute emphasis on divine justice ('Adl), the infallibility ('Ismah) of the Imams, and divine appointment (Nass) of leadership.
Q4. Describe in short the classifications of different schools of thought in Muslim philosophy.

Muslim philosophy and theology are broadly classified into four major intellectual traditions:

  • Kalam (Scholastic Theology): Rational defense of Islamic tenets. Divided into rationalist (Mu'tazilite) and orthodox-traditionalist schools (Ash'arite, Maturidite, Tahawite).
  • Falsafa (Peripatetic Philosophy): Greco-Arabic rationalism synthesized with monotheism (Al-Kindi, Al-Farabi, Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd).
  • Political-Theological Sects: Factions emerging from early political succession disputes (Sunni, Shia, Kharijites).
  • Tasawwuf (Sufi Mysticism): Experiential, esoteric knowledge (Irfan) seeking direct proximity to God.
3. Previous Year Questions (12.5 Marks) Broad Essays
Q1. Define Muslim philosophy. In this regard, analyze the definitions of Muslim philosophy given by various philosophers.

1. Definition & Scope: Muslim philosophy represents the systematic inquiry into metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, and theology conducted within Islamic civilization, utilizing both rational argumentation and divine revelation.

2. Analytical Perspectives across Philosophers:

  • Al-Kindi (First Arab Philosopher): Defined philosophy as "the knowledge of the reality of things according to human capacity." He argued that truth is universal and harmonious with prophetic revelation.
  • Al-Farabi (The Second Teacher): Viewed philosophy as the highest intellectual discipline, providing absolute demonstrative proof (Burhan), whereas religion translates these ultimate truths into symbolic imagery for the masses.
  • Ibn Sina (Avicenna): Defined it as the perfection of the human soul through the conceptualization of things and verification of theoretical and practical truths.
  • Ibn Rushd (Averroes): Defined philosophy as the investigation of existing beings and their reflection as evidence of the Creator, asserting that Quranic mandate obligates Muslims to study philosophy.
  • Syed Abdul Hai & Majid Fakhry (Modern Historians): Emphasize that Muslim philosophy includes both purely rationalistic Hellenistic adaptations (Falsafa) and internal theological debates (Kalam).
Q2. State the main points of differences among the political philosophy of Shia, Sunni and Kharijis.

The early political split over the Caliphate after Prophet Muhammad's death shaped three distinct political doctrines:

Feature Sunni Political Theory Shia Political Theory Khariji Political Theory
Source of Authority Community consensus (Ijma) & Consultation (Shura). Divine appointment (Nass) via Prophet. Radical piety; strictly election by any righteous believers.
Qualifications of Ruler Just Muslim, capable administrator, traditionally Qurayshi. Direct descent from Ahl al-Bayt (Ali's lineage). Any pious Muslim, even an Abyssinian slave.
Nature of Leader Fallible, political protector of Islamic law. Infallible (Ma'sum), possesses hidden spiritual knowledge. Fallible; must be immediately deposed if he sins.
Rebellion/Dissent Generally discouraged to prevent chaos (Fitna). Legitimate under guidance of true Imam or quietism during Ghaybah. Obligatory against any ruler who commits a grave sin.
Q3. Compare between Mu'tazilite and Ash'arite views on 'Attributes of Allah', 'Beatific Vision' (Beautification of Allah), and 'Createdness of Quran' in the discussion of unity of Allah.

The debate on Tawhid (Unity of God) formed the primary battleground between the rationalist Mu'tazilites and orthodox Ash'arites:

1. Attributes of Allah (Sifat):

  • Mu'tazilite View: Denied distinct eternal attributes existing alongside God's essence. They argued that separate co-eternal attributes introduce polytheism (plurality of eternals). Allah knows by His Essence, not by an added attribute of Knowledge.
  • Ash'arite View: Maintained that Allah has real, co-eternal attributes (Knowledge, Power, Life, etc.) distinct from His Essence, yet inseparable from it.

2. Beatific Vision (Ru'yat Allah):

  • Mu'tazilite View: Denied the physical or visual seeing of Allah in Paradise, arguing that visibility implies spatial direction, body, and distance, violating God's incorporeality. References in revelation are interpreted allegorically (metaphorical insight).
  • Ash'arite View: Affirmed that believers will see Allah in the Hereafter, but without spatial location, direction, or physical modality (Bi-la Kayf - without asking "how").

3. Createdness of the Quran (Khalq al-Quran):

  • Mu'tazilite View: The Quran is the created word of God brought into existence in time. If the Quran were uncreated, it would exist as a second eternal entity alongside God.
  • Ash'arite View: The Quran is the uncreated, eternal speech of God (Kalam Nafsi). However, the physical ink, paper, and recited sounds are created in time.
Q4. Discuss with comparison, the views of Jabriya and Qadariya on freedom of will.

The debate on free will vs. predestination represents one of the earliest theological dilemmas in Islam:

1. Jabriya (Absolute Determinism):

  • Proponents: Jahm ibn Safwan and followers.
  • Doctrine: Man possesses no free will, choice, or power whatsoever. Actions are entirely created and forced by Allah. Human responsibility is purely metaphorical—man acts as a feather swept by the wind. All events are strictly predestined.

2. Qadariya (Absolute Free Will):

  • Proponents: Ma'bad al-Juhani, Ghaylan al-Dimashqi.
  • Doctrine: Man has absolute free will and power (Qadar) over his own actions. Allah does not predetermine human choices; individuals create their good and bad deeds independently, establishing true divine justice in reward and punishment.

3. Synthesis (The Ash'arite Compromise - Kasb):

  • Ash'arism later synthesized these extremes through the doctrine of Kasb (Acquisition): Allah creates the action and power, but human beings "acquire" moral responsibility through their conscious intention.
Q5. Discuss the fundamental principles of Sufism. In this regard, describe the Sufi Path.

1. Fundamental Principles of Sufism (Tasawwuf):

  • Tawhid (Ultimate Unity): Progression from verbal affirmation of God to experiencing divine presence in all existence (Wahdat al-Wujud).
  • Zuhd (Asceticism) & Ihsan: Purification of the inner self (Tazkiyat al-Nafs) and worshiping Allah as if seeing Him.
  • Mahabbah (Divine Love): Seeking God out of pure love rather than fear of Hell or desire for Paradise (e.g., Rabia al-Adawiyya).
  • Fana and Baqa: Annihilation of the individual ego (Fana) leading to eternal subsistence in divine consciousness (Baqa).

2. The Sufi Path (Tariqah): The spiritual journey consists of earned stages (Maqamat) and bestowed spiritual states (Ahwal):

  • Taubah (Repentance): Sincere turning away from worldly attachment and sin.
  • Wara' (Watchfulness) & Zuhd: Abstain from doubtful matters and material distractions.
  • Faqr (Spiritual Poverty): Recognizing total dependency upon Allah alone.
  • Sabr (Patience) & Shukr (Gratitude): Enduring tribulations and thanking God in all conditions.
  • Tawakkul (Complete Trust): Absolute reliance on divine providence.
  • Rida (Contentment): Joyful acceptance of God's divine decree.
Q6. Explain and evaluate Al-Farabi's Political Philosophy.

1. Overview: Al-Farabi outlined his political philosophy principally in Al-Madina al-Fadila (The Virtuous City), synthesizing Plato's Republic with Islamic prophetic doctrine.

2. The Virtuous City (Al-Madina al-Fadila):

  • Organic Model: Farabi compares the state to a healthy human body where organs function harmoniously under the leadership of the heart.
  • The Ideal Ruler (First Chief): Must be both a Supreme Philosopher and a Prophet. He receives divine illumination from the Active Intellect (Al-'Aql al-Fa'al) and possesses supreme intellect, moral perfection, and political governance skill.

3. Imperfect / Ignorant Cities (Non-Ideal States):

  • Madina al-Jahiliyya (Ignorant City): Unaware of true spiritual happiness; pursues wealth, pleasure, or conquest.
  • Madina al-Fasiqa (Immoral City): Knows ultimate truth theoretically, but acts corruptly.
  • Madina al-Dalla (Erring City): Adopts false cosmological beliefs about God and higher realities.

4. Evaluation: Farabi successfully reconciled Greek political philosophy with Islamic governance, elevating philosophy to a public civil utility. Critics note his ideal ruler is practically unattainable in post-prophetic history.

Q7. Mention some important aspects of the philosophy of Ibn Sina, illustrate his views regarding the soul.

1. Major Aspects of Ibn Sina's Philosophy:

  • Metaphysics of Being: Distinction between Necessary Being (Wajib al-Wujud—Allah) whose existence is essential to Its essence, and Contingent Being (Mumkin al-Wujud) which depends on an external cause.
  • Emanationism: Creation proceeds from God via necessary intellectual emanation across ten celestial intellects.

2. Doctrine of the Soul (Nafs):

  • Substantiality & Incorporeality: The soul is an independent immaterial substance, not merely a physical form bound to the body. The body is an instrument used by the soul.
  • The "Floating Man" Thought Experiment (Al-Insan al-Ta'ir): Ibn Sina asks us to imagine a fully formed human created suspended in mid-air with eyes covered and limbs separated, receiving no sensory input. Even without sensory perception of his body, he remains directly aware of his own self/consciousness. This proves self-awareness is independent of physical sensation and body.
  • Faculties of Soul: Plant (vegetative), Animal (locomotive/perceptive), and Human (Rational—divided into Practical and Theoretical Intellect).
  • Immortality: The human rational soul survives bodily death, retaining individual selfhood.
4. Philosopher Profiles Eastern & Western

Al-Kindi (801–873)

Eastern • "Philosopher of the Arabs"
  • Works: On First Philosophy
  • Reconciled Greek philosophy with Islamic revelation.
  • Defended creation ex nihilo.

Al-Farabi (872–950)

Eastern • "The Second Teacher"
  • Works: Al-Madina al-Fadila
  • Systematized Neoplatonic emanations.
  • Synthesized Plato and Aristotle.

Ibn Sina (980–1037)

Eastern • "Avicenna"
  • Works: The Shifa, Canon of Medicine
  • Floating Man argument for soul.
  • Wajib al-Wujud (Necessary Being).

Al-Ghazali (1058–1111)

Eastern • "Algazel"
  • Works: Tahafut al-Falasifa, Ihya 'Ulum al-Din
  • Critiqued philosophers on eternity, omniscience, resurrection.
  • Revived Sufism into orthodoxy.

Ibn Bajjah (1085–1138)

Western (Andalusia) • "Avempace"
  • Works: Governance of the Solitary
  • Focused on the isolation of the philosopher in imperfect states.

Ibn Tufail (1110–1185)

Western (Andalusia)
  • Works: Hayy ibn Yaqzan
  • Philosophical novel showing truth discovered via pure reason matches revelation.

Ibn Rushd (1126–1198)

Western (Andalusia) • "Averroes"
  • Works: Tahafut al-Tahafut, Fasl al-Maqal
  • Defended philosophy against Al-Ghazali. Pure Aristotelianism.

Ibn Miskawayh (932–1030)

Eastern Ethics Scholar
  • Works: Tahdhib al-Akhlaq
  • Systematized Islamic ethical philosophy combining Aristotle and Islam.

Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406)

North Africa • Father of Sociology
  • Works: Muqaddimah
  • Concepts of 'Asabiyyah (Social Cohesion) and cyclic rise/fall of empires.

Allama Iqbal (1877–1938)

Modern Thinker
  • Works: The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam
  • Concept of Khudi (Selfhood), dynamic interpretation of Ijtihad.

Q1 (5 Marks): What were Al-Ghazali's 3 major condemnations of the philosophers in Tahafut al-Falasifa?

In Tahafut al-Falasifa (Incoherence of the Philosophers), Al-Ghazali declared thinkers like Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina to have departed from Islam on 3 specific issues:

  1. Eternity of the World: Philosophers asserted the universe was eternal, opposing the doctrine of creation ex nihilo.
  2. God's Knowledge of Particulars: Philosophers held God knows universals, but not individual temporal particulars.
  3. Bodily Resurrection: Philosophers claimed resurrection is purely spiritual, denying physical bodily resurrection.
Q2 (5 Marks): Define the Sufi terms 'Fana' and 'Baqa'.

Fana: Spiritual annihilation or passing away of human ego, desires, and self-will into Divine consciousness.

Baqa: Spiritual subsistence or eternal abiding in Allah following Fana, where the mystic lives in the world guided fully by divine will.

Q3 (12.5 Marks): Explain Ibn Khaldun's concept of 'Asabiyyah' and his theory of the rise and fall of civilizations.

1. Definition of 'Asabiyyah: Social solidarity, group cohesion, or tribal loyalty that binds communities together.

2. Role in State Formation: Nomadic tribes possess strong 'Asabiyyah due to harsh desert conditions, allowing them to overcome weak urban dynasties and establish new empires.

3. Five-Stage Cycle of Dynasties:

  • Stage 1: Conquest through strong 'Asabiyyah.
  • Stage 2: Consolidation of central royal authority.
  • Stage 3: Prosperity, luxury, and cultural flourishing.
  • Stage 4: Complacency, decay of 'Asabiyyah, and over-taxation.
  • Stage 5: Extravagance, vulnerability, and eventual collapse to a new nomadic group with stronger 'Asabiyyah.
Q4 (12.5 Marks): Describe Allama Iqbal's philosophy of 'Khudi' (Selfhood) and his view on Ijtihad.

1. Concept of Khudi (Selfhood): Iqbal rejected passive mysticism that preaches self-effacement. Instead, he defined Khudi as dynamic, creative self-realization where human personality is strengthened through active engagement with reality.

2. Principle of Movement (Ijtihad): Iqbal termed Ijtihad the "principle of movement in the structure of Islam." He urged modern Muslims to re-open Ijtihad to reinterpret eternal Islamic principles in light of modern scientific and social advances, rejecting intellectual stagnation.
