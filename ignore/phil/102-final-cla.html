Define Muslim Philosophy. Analyze the definitions given by various philosophers.

⚡ Exam tip: Open with a concise definition, then attribute definitions to named scholars, then synthesize. This question is worth 12.5 marks — write at least 5–6 paragraphs.

Introduction

Muslim Philosophy refers to the philosophical thinking developed by Muslim thinkers who were influenced by Islamic revelation, Greek thought, and their own intellectual tradition. It is a broad discipline that encompasses theology (Kalam), metaphysics, ethics, political theory, and logic.

Definitions by Various Philosophers

1. Syed Abdul Hai

According to Syed Abdul Hai (the author of Muslim Philosophy), Muslim Philosophy is the systematic rational inquiry into the problems of existence, knowledge, and value conducted by thinkers who were Muslim by faith and culture. It is distinct from pure Islamic theology because it employs Greek-style rationalism while remaining rooted in Islamic values.

2. Majid Fakhry

Majid Fakhry, in his History of Islamic Philosophy, defines it as the intellectual tradition that emerged when Muslim scholars encountered Greek philosophical texts — especially Plato and Aristotle — and tried to reconcile rational philosophy with Quranic revelation. He distinguishes between Falsafa (Greek-inspired philosophy) and Kalam (theological reasoning).

3. De Boer

De Boer defines it as the philosophy that arose in the Islamic cultural world and was shaped by both the Islamic faith and Hellenistic learning. He sees it primarily as a transmission belt for Greek philosophy into medieval Europe.

4. Max Horten

Horten views Muslim Philosophy as an original intellectual achievement — not merely a translation of Greek ideas — where Muslim thinkers added their own insights on God, creation, soul, and ethics.

5. M.M. Sharif

In his History of Muslim Philosophy, Sharif defines it as "philosophy as pursued by men who are Muslims." This is deliberately broad — it includes all systematic rational thought by Muslim intellectuals regardless of whether it directly draws on Quranic texts.

Nature of Muslim Philosophy

  • Rational-Revelatory Synthesis: Muslim philosophy tries to harmonize reason (Aql) with revelation (Wahy).
  • Eclectic yet original: It absorbed Greek, Persian, and Indian ideas but transformed them within an Islamic framework.
  • Encyclopedic scope: It covered metaphysics, ethics, logic, political theory, natural science, and mysticism.
  • Apologetic function: Early Muslim philosophers used philosophy to defend Islam against critics.

Sources of Muslim Philosophy

  • The Quran — The primary divine source; philosophical reflection on its verses (Tafakkur).
  • Hadith — Sayings and practices of Prophet Muhammad (PBUH).
  • Greek Philosophy — Works of Plato, Aristotle, Plotinus (often mistakenly attributed to Aristotle as "Theology of Aristotle").
  • Persian and Indian Traditions — Zoroastrian ethics, Indian logic and mathematics.
  • Ijtihad — Independent reasoning of Muslim scholars.

Scope of Muslim Philosophy

Muslim Philosophy covers: metaphysics (existence and being), epistemology (knowledge and its sources), ethics (moral conduct), political philosophy (ideal state and leadership), cosmology (origin and nature of the universe), and mysticism (relationship between man and God).

Muslim Philosophy vs. Islamic Philosophy

Key Distinction: "Islamic Philosophy" refers specifically to philosophy derived from and consistent with Islamic scriptural sources (Quran and Sunnah). "Muslim Philosophy" is broader — it includes all philosophical thought by Muslim thinkers, even when they engage with non-Islamic Greek or Persian ideas. All Islamic Philosophy is Muslim Philosophy, but not all Muslim Philosophy is strictly Islamic Philosophy.

Conclusion

Muslim Philosophy is a rich and sophisticated intellectual tradition that cannot be reduced to mere imitation of Greek thought. It was a genuine creative enterprise through which Muslim thinkers grappled with ultimate questions of reality, God, soul, ethics, and politics — leaving a permanent mark on both Eastern and Western intellectual history.

Islam as a Complete Code of Life (Deen)

Islam is not merely a religion of ritual worship but a comprehensive Deen — a complete way of life that provides guidance for every dimension of human existence.

1. Spiritual/Religious Dimension

Islam prescribes the Five Pillars: Shahadah (declaration of faith), Salah (prayer five times daily), Zakat (charity), Sawm (fasting in Ramadan), and Hajj (pilgrimage). These maintain man's connection with Allah and purify the soul.

2. Moral and Ethical Dimension

Islam provides a detailed moral code based on Quran and Hadith. It promotes justice, honesty, compassion, and prohibits lying, oppression, and moral corruption. The Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) said: "I have been sent to perfect moral character."

3. Social Dimension

Islam regulates family life (marriage, divorce, inheritance), social relationships, and community obligations. It establishes clear rights for men, women, children, and the elderly.

4. Legal Dimension (Shariah)

The Shariah is the Islamic legal system derived from the Quran, Sunnah, Ijma (consensus), and Qiyas (analogy). It governs criminal law, civil law, trade, and personal conduct.

5. Economic Dimension

Islam forbids usury (Riba), promotes fair trade, mandates Zakat (2.5% of wealth for the poor), and encourages equitable distribution of wealth.

6. Political Dimension

Islam provides principles of governance based on Shura (consultation), justice, and accountability. The ruler is a trustee of the community, not an absolute sovereign.

Conclusion: Islam addresses every sphere of life — spiritual, ethical, social, legal, economic, and political — making it a comprehensive and self-sufficient system for human civilization.

Classification of Schools in Muslim Philosophy

Muslim philosophical schools can be classified into three broad categories:

I. Theological/Philosophical Schools (Kalam Schools)

These schools debated rational questions about God, free will, and faith:

  • Qadarites: Advocated free will — man creates his own actions.
  • Jabarites (Jabrites): Advocated predestination — man is compelled by God.
  • Mutazilites: Rationalist school emphasizing divine unity and justice.
  • Asharites: Moderate predestinarian school; founded by Al-Ashari.
  • Maturidis: School of Abu Mansur al-Maturidi; moderate between Mutazilites and Asharites.
  • Tahawis: Followers of Imam al-Tahawi; a Hanafi theological school.

II. Religious-Political Schools

These schools debated questions of legitimate political authority and community membership:

  • Sunnis: The majority; caliphate determined by election/consensus.
  • Shia: Leadership must be from the Prophet's family (Ahl al-Bayt); Imam is divinely guided.
  • Kharijis: Extremist view; any pious Muslim can be Caliph; sinners are apostates.
  • Murji'as: Suspended judgment on sinners; faith alone determines Muslim identity.

III. Mystical/Spiritual School

  • Sufis: Focus on inner spiritual development, love of God, and the quest for divine union (Fana). They developed a rich tradition of spiritual stations (Maqamat) and states (Ahwal).
Note: Additionally, we have the Falsafa tradition — philosophers like Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina who engaged directly with Greek philosophical methods.

Historical Development of Muslim Philosophy

Phase 1: The Translation Movement (8th–9th Century CE)

Under the Abbasid Caliph al-Mansur and especially Harun al-Rashid and al-Ma'mun, Greek texts (Aristotle, Plato, Galen, Euclid) were systematically translated into Arabic at the famous Bayt al-Hikmah (House of Wisdom) in Baghdad. This sparked systematic philosophical thinking among Muslims.

Phase 2: The Founding Period (9th–10th Century)

Al-Kindi (the "first Muslim philosopher") began synthesizing Greek and Islamic thought. Al-Farabi developed a comprehensive Neo-Platonic system and political philosophy. This period saw the birth of Kalam (scholastic theology) including the Mutazilites and Asharites.

Phase 3: The Golden Age (10th–12th Century)

Ibn Sina (Avicenna) produced encyclopedic works on metaphysics, medicine, and logic. Al-Ghazali launched a devastating critique of philosophers in Tahafut al-Falasifa. This period saw Muslim philosophy reach its greatest sophistication.

Phase 4: Western Islamic Philosophy (11th–12th Century)

In Muslim Spain (Al-Andalus): Ibn Bajjah, Ibn Tufayl, and Ibn Rushd (Averroes) produced major works. Ibn Rushd's commentaries on Aristotle were so important that he was called "The Commentator" in medieval Europe.

Phase 5: Later Developments

Ibn Khaldun (14th century) founded sociology and philosophy of history. Modern period: Allama Iqbal (20th century) developed a philosophy of the self (Khudi) integrating Islamic thought with Western philosophy.

Introduction: The Problem of Free Will in Islam

One of the most fundamental debates in Muslim theology concerns human freedom and divine determination. The question is: Does God predetermine all human actions, or does man possess genuine free will? Two schools formed opposing positions: the Jabariya (Compulsionists) and the Qadariya (Free-Willists).

The Jabariya (Jabrites) — Predestinarian School

Origin and Founders

The term Jabr means compulsion or constraint. Jahm ibn Safwan (d. 746 CE) is the most prominent figure associated with extreme Jabarism. The school argued that humans have no real freedom — God alone is the agent of all actions.

Main Doctrine

  • Man is like a feather in the wind — utterly passive and compelled by divine decree.
  • All human actions — good and evil — are created and determined by Allah.
  • Man's "will" is an illusion; there is only the will of God.
  • Human responsibility is thus minimized or eliminated — a person cannot truly be blamed for sin since God determined that sin.

Theological Basis

The Jabariya cited Quranic verses such as: "And God creates you and what you do" (37:96) and "You cannot will except what Allah wills" (81:29). They interpreted divine omnipotence (Qudra) to mean absolute causation of all events including human acts.

Types of Jabariya

  • Extreme Jabariya (Jabr Mahd): Man has absolutely no will or power — represented by followers of Jahm ibn Safwan.
  • Moderate Jabariya: Man has a form of acquired action (Kasb) but no real efficacy — this later influenced the Asharites.

The Qadariya — Free Will School

Origin and Founders

The term Qadar in this context means the power/capacity of man (though later it paradoxically came to mean divine decree). Ma'bad al-Juhani (d. 699 CE) and Ghaylan al-Dimashqi are early figures. The Mutazilites later became the greatest defenders of this position.

Main Doctrine

  • Man is the creator (khaliq) of his own actions through the free will God has given him.
  • Human beings have genuine moral agency — they can choose between good and evil.
  • Without genuine free will, moral accountability, divine reward, and divine punishment would be unjust.
  • God is just (Adl) — He would not command what He also prevents; so man must be free to obey or disobey.

Theological Basis

The Qadariya cited verses like: "Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it" (99:7-8). They argued divine justice (Adl) requires human freedom — God would be unjust to punish humans for actions He compelled them to perform.

This synthesis, while not fully satisfying all philosophical objections, became mainstream Sunni theology.

Key Conclusion: The free will debate in Islam is not merely theological but deeply ethical and political. The Qadariya position supports moral responsibility and justice; the Jabariya supports radical divine sovereignty. The mainstream Sunni resolution lies between these extremes.

Introduction: Mutazilites and Asharites

The Mutazilites (founded in Basra, 8th century, by Wasil ibn Ata after separating from Hasan al-Basri) were the rationalist school of Islamic theology. Their five principles were: Divine Unity (Tawhid), Divine Justice (Adl), Promise and Threat, the Intermediate Position, and Commanding Right and Forbidding Wrong. The Asharites were founded by Abu al-Hasan al-Ashari (874–936 CE) who was himself a former Mutazilite. They represent the mainstream Sunni theological tradition and tried to defend traditional doctrine using rational methods.

I. Attributes of Allah (Sifat Allah)

Mutazilite View:

The Mutazilites denied the reality of God's attributes as entities distinct from His essence. To them, affirming that God has real, eternal attributes (like Knowledge, Power, Life) separate from His essence would amount to Shirk (associating partners with God) — a form of polytheism. God's knowledge IS His essence; His power IS His essence. They were called Mu'attila (those who strip attributes) by their opponents. Their formula: God's attributes are identical to His essence — there is no real distinction.

Asharite View:

The Asharites affirmed that God has real, eternal attributes (Sifat) that are distinct from, yet inseparable from, His essence. These are seven core attributes: Life (Hayat), Knowledge (Ilm), Power (Qudra), Will (Irada), Hearing (Sama), Sight (Basar), and Speech (Kalam). They insisted these are real and eternal but rejected any comparison with human attributes — "without asking how" (bila kayf). The formula: attributes are neither identical to nor separate from the divine essence.

II. Beatification/Vision of Allah (Ru'yat Allah)

Mutazilite View:

The Mutazilites denied the possibility of seeing Allah in the afterlife. Their argument: seeing requires that the seen object occupies a direction or place (jiha), and God is beyond all spatial limitations. Since God cannot be in a direction, He cannot be seen. To claim otherwise would be to make God corporeal (having a body), which is an anthropomorphism they strongly rejected. They interpreted Quranic passages about "seeing" as metaphorical.

Asharite View:

The Asharites affirmed that believers will see Allah in Paradise as stated in the Quran ("Faces that Day will be radiant, looking at their Lord" — 75:22-23) and in Hadith. However, they denied that this seeing is like ordinary sensory perception that requires spatiality. God will be seen in a manner befitting His majesty — bila kayf (without asking how). The vision is real but its modality is beyond human understanding.

III. Createdness of the Quran (Khalq al-Quran)

Mutazilite View:

This was the Mutazilites' most famous and controversial position. They argued that the Quran is created (Makhluk) — it was brought into existence in time by God. Their reasoning: (a) The Quran is expressed in letters, sounds, and ink — all of which are temporal/created phenomena. (b) To say the Quran is eternal would be to place something alongside God as co-eternal, which violates divine unity (Tawhid). Under Caliph al-Ma'mun, the Mutazilite doctrine was enforced as state policy through the Mihna (Inquisition, 833–848 CE), and scholars who denied it were persecuted — including Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal who suffered imprisonment and flogging for refusing to accept it.

Asharite View:

The Asharites distinguished between two senses of the Quran: (a) The Kalam Nafsi — the eternal divine speech that exists in God's essence, which is uncreated and eternal. (b) The Kalam Lafzi — the letters, sounds, words, and ink through which that eternal speech is expressed, which are created temporal phenomena. So the Quran as the literal word of God in His essence is eternal; the physical book and its linguistic expression are created. This distinction resolved the controversy for mainstream Sunni theology.

The Murji'as

Origin and Name

The term Murji'a comes from the Arabic root Arja'a meaning "to defer" or "to postpone." They were an early Islamic theological school that emerged in the late 7th and early 8th centuries, partly as a response to the crisis caused by the first civil wars (Fitna) in the Muslim community.

Historical Context

The Murji'a arose in opposition to the Kharijites, who declared that grave sinners are no longer Muslims (apostates). The Murji'a took the opposite position — they refused to pass judgment on sinners and "deferred" (irja) that judgment to God.

Main Doctrinal Position

On the question of the status of a Muslim who commits a grave sin (kabira), the Murji'as held:

  • Faith (Iman) alone determines Muslim identity — not deeds. As long as a person believes in God and the Prophet, they remain a Muslim regardless of how grave their sins are.
  • Suspension of judgment: They deferred judgment on sinners entirely to God on the Day of Judgment — humans cannot and should not declare another Muslim to be an unbeliever (Kafir).
  • Sin does not nullify faith: A sinner retains the name "Muslim" and the full status of a believer. Sin and faith coexist.
  • The logical consequence: Even a ruler who is unjust or sinful must be obeyed, since he is still a Muslim — this made the Murji'a politically useful to Umayyad rulers.

Sub-groups

  • Extreme Murji'a: Held that as long as one has faith, no sin can harm the soul at all.
  • Moderate Murji'a (closer to Sunni position): Recognized that sins may be punished but ultimately God may forgive the faithful sinner.
Contrast with Kharijites: Kharijites said grave sinners are apostates (kafirs). Mutazilites said they occupy an intermediate position. Murji'as said they are full Muslims — judgment belongs only to God.

Al-Tahawi and Al-Maturidi — their theological positions
4 marks

Al-Tahawi (853–933 CE)

Abu Ja'far al-Tahawi was an Egyptian Hanafi scholar. His Aqida al-Tahawiyya (Tahawite Creed) became one of the most widely accepted statements of Sunni belief. Key positions:

  • Affirmed God's attributes without interpretation (Tafwid) — accepting them as stated without asking how.
  • Quran is the eternal uncreated word of God.
  • Believers will see God in the afterlife.
  • Human acts are created by God but acquired by humans (anticipating the Kasb concept).
  • Did not use extensive rationalist methodology — relied on scriptural tradition.

Al-Maturidi (853–944 CE)

Abu Mansur al-Maturidi of Samarkand founded the Maturidi school, one of the two main Sunni theological schools (alongside Asharites). Key positions:

  • Gave more scope to human reason than the Asharites — believed reason alone can establish God's existence and basic moral truths.
  • Affirmed divine attributes as real and eternal.
  • On free will: God creates the power (Qudra) but man exercises genuine choice (Ikhtiyar) — more room for human agency than Asharite Kasb.
  • The Quran is uncreated (eternal word of God) in its inner meaning (Kalam Nafsi).
  • Dominant theology in Hanafi-majority regions (Central Asia, South Asia, Turkey).
Al-Maturidi gave more autonomy to human reason; Al-Ashari gave more weight to transmitted authority. Both are recognized as orthodox Sunni theologians.

The Sifatiyas

The Sifatiyas (from Sifa = attribute) are those who affirmed the reality of divine attributes — the opposite of the Mu'attila (deniers of attributes, i.e., Mutazilites). The Sifatiyas affirm that God genuinely possesses real, eternal attributes like Knowledge, Power, Life, Will, Hearing, Sight, and Speech.

Main Positions

  • God's attributes are real and distinct from His essence, though not separate from it — they subsist in the divine essence.
  • Affirming attributes does not compromise Tawhid (divine unity) because the attributes are not separate beings — they are inseparable from God.
  • The Asharites represent the most sophisticated Sifatiya position.

Sub-groups within Sifatiyas

  • Anthropomorphists (Mujassima/Mushabbiha): Extreme group that interpreted Quranic anthropomorphic expressions literally (God's hand, face, etc.), implying God has a body similar to humans. Considered heretical by mainstream scholars.
  • Moderate Sifatiyas (Asharites, Maturidis): Affirm real attributes but deny any likeness to creation — Laysa kamithlihi shay' ("There is nothing like Him," 42:11). They use bila kayf — attributes are affirmed without asking how.
The Sifatiya-Mu'attila debate is essentially the Asharite-Mutazilite debate on divine attributes. Mainstream Sunni theology follows the Sifatiya position in its moderate Asharite/Maturidi form.

State the main points of difference in political philosophy of Shia, Sunni, and Kharijis.

Introduction

The question of political authority — who should lead the Muslim community after the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) — was the most divisive issue in early Islamic history. It gave rise to three major religious-political traditions: Sunni, Shia, and Kharijis. Each has a distinctive political theology.

I. Sunni Political Philosophy

Caliphate and Legitimacy

The Sunnis believe that after the Prophet's death, leadership of the Muslim community (Caliphate) should be determined by election or consensus (Ijma) of leading Muslims. The Caliph need not be from any particular family — he should be a qualified, pious Muslim man, ideally from the Quraysh tribe.

Key Principles

  • Election/Bay'a: The Caliph is chosen through bay'a (pledge of allegiance) by community leaders (Ahl al-Hall wa al-Aqd — "those who bind and loose").
  • Human institution: The Caliphate is a human political institution, not divinely appointed. The Prophet did not designate a successor — this was left to the community.
  • Limited scope of authority: The Caliph's role is to implement the Shariah, defend the community, and maintain order. He is not infallible (Ma'sum).
  • Legitimacy through function: Even an imperfect Caliph should generally be obeyed to prevent civil strife (Fitna) — scholars like Al-Mawardi developed a theory of de facto legitimacy.
  • Shura (consultation): The ruler should consult scholars and community leaders.

On sinful rulers:

Sunni mainstream position: obey even an unjust ruler to avoid chaos, unless he commands clear disobedience to God. Rebellion (Khuruj) against a Muslim ruler is generally forbidden.

II. Shia Political Philosophy

Imama and Divine Appointment

The Shia (from Shi'at Ali — "partisans of Ali") believe that leadership (Imama) must be held by a direct descendant of the Prophet through his daughter Fatima and cousin/son-in-law Ali ibn Abi Talib. This is not a human arrangement but a divine appointment.

Key Principles

  • Divine appointment (Nass): The Prophet designated Ali at Ghadir Khumm as his successor. Imams are appointed by God through the Prophet and each preceding Imam.
  • Infallibility (Isma): The Imam is divinely protected from sin and error — he has perfect knowledge of religion and is the authoritative interpreter of the Quran and Sunnah.
  • Spiritual authority: The Imam possesses esoteric (Batin) knowledge beyond ordinary understanding. Obedience to the Imam is a religious obligation.
  • Political legitimacy: Any government not led by a legitimate Imam is, strictly speaking, illegitimate — though Shia jurists developed theories for navigating unjust political realities.

Ithna Ashariya (The Twelvers) — Most important Shia sub-group

They recognize twelve Imams starting with Ali and ending with Muhammad al-Mahdi, who went into occultation (Ghayba) in 874 CE and is expected to return at the end of times. During occultation, authority is exercised by qualified jurists (Wilayat al-Faqih in modern Shia political theory).

III. Kharijite Political Philosophy

Radical Egalitarianism

The Kharijites (from Kharaja — "to go out/rebel") were the earliest extremist group, emerging when they abandoned Ali's camp during the Battle of Siffin (657 CE), objecting to arbitration. Their political philosophy is the most radical of the three.

Key Principles

  • Any pious Muslim can be Caliph: Leadership is not restricted to Quraysh tribe (against Sunni view) or the Prophet's family (against Shia view). Even a non-Arab slave can be Caliph if he is pious.
  • Election by community: The Caliph is chosen by the community and can be deposed or killed if he commits sin.
  • Grave sinners are apostates (Takfir): This is their most extreme position — anyone who commits a major sin leaves the fold of Islam entirely. This justified declaring war on other Muslims.
  • "Judgment belongs only to God" (La Hukma illa lillah): This was their famous slogan, used to reject the arbitration at Siffin as illegitimate human compromise on a divine matter.
  • Jihad against sinful rulers: It is an obligation to rebel against and fight an unjust or sinful Muslim ruler.

Ithna Ashariya — The Twelver Shia

The Ithna Ashariya (Arabic: اثنا عشرية, "the Twelvers") are the largest branch of Shia Islam — today comprising the majority of Shia Muslims, especially in Iran, Iraq, Bahrain, and significant minorities across the Muslim world.

The Twelve Imams

They recognize twelve Imams as the legitimate successors of the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH), all from his family (Ahl al-Bayt):

  1. Ali ibn Abi Talib (d. 661)
  2. Hasan ibn Ali (d. 670)
  3. Husayn ibn Ali (d. 680) — Martyred at Karbala
  4. Ali ibn Husayn (Zayn al-Abidin, d. 713)
  5. Muhammad al-Baqir (d. 733)
  6. Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765)
  7. Musa al-Kadhim (d. 799)
  8. Ali al-Ridha (d. 818)

Definition and Origin of Sufism

Sufism (Tasawwuf) is the inner, spiritual, and mystical dimension of Islam. The term is most likely derived from Suf (wool) — referring to the coarse woolen garments worn by early Muslim ascetics as a sign of renunciation of worldly pleasures. Sufis are those who pursue the inner reality (Haqiqa) behind the outer law (Shariah), seeking direct experiential knowledge of God (Ma'rifa) and ultimately union with the Divine.

Sufism emerged in the 8th century CE as a reaction to the increasing worldliness and materialism of the expanding Islamic empire. Early Sufis like Hasan al-Basri (d. 728), Rabia al-Adawiyya (d. 801 — the great female Sufi who introduced the concept of divine love), and Ibrahim ibn Adham emphasized asceticism, repentance, and love of God.

Fundamental Principles/Doctrines of Sufism

1. Tawhid (Divine Unity) — The Metaphysical Foundation

While orthodox Islam holds that God and creation are distinct, many Sufis — especially the monistic ones — developed doctrines of the oneness of being. Ibn Arabi's famous doctrine of Wahdat al-Wujud (Unity of Being) holds that ultimately there is only one reality — God — and everything else is a manifestation or "epiphany" (Tajalli) of the Divine. This is not pantheism but rather the understanding that no being has independent existence apart from God.

2. Mahabbah (Divine Love)

Love of God is the central motive of the Sufi path. Rabia al-Adawiyya is credited with transforming early Sufi asceticism (fear of God's punishment) into a path of love (love of God for His own sake, not for Paradise or out of fear of Hell). The Sufi aspires to be the beloved of God, and the lover who loses himself in the Beloved.

3. Ma'rifa (Gnosis/Mystical Knowledge)

Ma'rifa is direct, experiential knowledge of God — not merely intellectual or theological knowledge. It is a gift from God achieved through purification of the heart, spiritual discipline, and divine grace. The Sufi seeks to move from Ilm (ordinary knowledge) to Ma'rifa (experiential knowledge of God).

4. Fana (Annihilation) and Baqa (Subsistence)

Fana refers to the annihilation or extinction of the ego-self (Nafs) in the Divine. The individual self dissolves in the overwhelming reality of God. Baqa is the state that follows — subsistence in God — where the mystic continues to exist but his existence is entirely through and in God. Abu Yazid al-Bistami (d. 874) expressed this ecstatically; al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910) gave it a more sober formulation.

5. Zuhd (Asceticism) and Tawakkul (Trust in God)

Early Sufis emphasized renunciation of worldly attachments. The Sufi trusts entirely in God (Tawakkul), recognizing that all provision comes from Him. This is not laziness but a state of inner freedom from worldly anxiety.

6. The Shaykh (Spiritual Master)

The Sufi path cannot be traveled alone. Every seeker (Murid) must have a spiritual guide (Shaykh or Pir) who has already traversed the path. The Shaykh-Murid relationship is central to Sufi pedagogy — the disciple submits to the master's guidance as a patient to a physician.

7. Dhikr (Remembrance of God)

The practice of continually remembering and invoking God's name — based on the Quranic verse "Verily in the remembrance of God do hearts find rest" (13:28). Dhikr is the primary spiritual practice of most Sufi orders.

The Sufi Path — Tariqah

The Sufi journey is often described as moving through Shariah (the outer law), Tariqah (the inner path), and Haqiqah (the ultimate truth). The path involves progression through stages and states:

A. Maqamat (Stations) — Permanent Spiritual Attainments

Maqamat are stages that the Sufi achieves through sustained effort and discipline. They are permanent — once a station is achieved, it is not lost. Different Sufis enumerate different stations, but a common list includes:

  1. Tawba (Repentance): The beginning — turning away from sin and toward God.
  2. Wara' (Scrupulousness): Avoiding all doubtful matters.
  3. Zuhd (Renunciation): Detachment from worldly things.
  4. Faqr (Spiritual Poverty): Total dependence on God; absence of ego.
  5. Sabr (Patience): Steadfast endurance of spiritual trials.
  6. Tawakkul (Trust in God): Absolute reliance on God's will.
  7. Ridha (Contentment): Complete contentment with God's decrees — even hardship is accepted with joy.

B. Ahwal (States) — Temporary Divine Gifts

Ahwal (sing. Hal) are transient spiritual states that come and go as divine gifts — they cannot be achieved by effort alone but are bestowed by God's grace. They include:

  • Muraqaba: Watchful meditation — awareness of God's presence.
  • Qurb: Nearness to God.
  • Mahabbah: Intense divine love.
  • Khawf and Raja': Fear of God and hope in His mercy.
  • Shawq: Longing and yearning for God.
  • Uns: Intimacy and familiarity with God.
  • Hayba: Awe and reverence before God's majesty.
  • Ghaybah: Absence — being so absorbed in God that one becomes unaware of the world.

Major Sufi Orders (Tariqas)

From the 12th century onward, Sufism organized into formal brotherhoods (Tariqas): the Qadiriyya (founded by Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani), the Naqshbandiyya, the Chishtiyya (influential in South Asia), the Shadhiliyya, and the Mawlawiyya (the famous "whirling dervishes" of Rumi). Each has distinctive practices and spiritual lineages.

Criticism and Response

Sufism has been criticized by orthodox scholars (especially Hanbali and later Wahhabi scholars) for alleged innovations (Bid'a), saint veneration, and monistic doctrines. Sufis respond that their practices are rooted in Quran and Hadith and that Fana does not mean literal merger with God but annihilation of the ego.

Conclusion: Sufism represents Islam's rich inner life — a tradition of love, discipline, and spiritual transformation that has produced great poets (Rumi, Hafiz), scholars, and saints, and has been a major force in the spread of Islam across the world.

Life

Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi, known as the "Philosopher of the Arabs," was the first Muslim philosopher in the strict sense. Al-Farabi — Life and Works

Abu Nasr Muhammad al-Farabi (c. 870–950 CE) was born in Farab (in present-day Kazakhstan or Uzbekistan) and spent much of his life in Baghdad and Damascus. Considered the "Second Teacher" (al-Mu'allim al-Thani) after Aristotle, he was a profound logician, philosopher, political theorist, and musician. His major works include: The Opinions of the Inhabitants of the Virtuous City (Ara' Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila), The Attainment of Happiness (Tahsil al-Sa'ada), The Political Regime (Al-Siyasa al-Madaniyya), and On the Perfect State.

Metaphysical Foundation of his Political Philosophy

Al-Farabi's political philosophy cannot be understood without his metaphysics. He developed a Neo-Platonic emanation theory: from God (the First Cause, Pure Intellect) emanate ten celestial intellects in a hierarchical chain. The Tenth (Active) Intellect governs the terrestrial world and is the source of human intellectual illumination. This cosmic hierarchy mirrors the ideal social hierarchy.

The Virtuous City (Al-Madina al-Fadila)

Al-Farabi's most famous political concept is the Madina Fadila — the Virtuous/Ideal City. It is modeled on the healthy human body:

  • Just as the body has organs each performing specific functions for the whole, the city has citizens each fulfilling their proper roles.
  • Just as the heart rules the body, the Philosopher-Ruler/Imam rules the city — as the supreme organ from which all order flows.
  • The Virtuous City is one organized for the achievement of true happiness — both worldly and otherworldly.

The Philosopher-King / Philosopher-Imam

The ruler of the Virtuous City is the most important concept in Al-Farabi's political philosophy. He possesses twelve qualities (or conditions):

  • Sound bodily constitution; naturally retentive memory; love of learning and truth; love of honesty and hatred of lies; disdain for sensual pleasures; love of justice and hatred of injustice; strong determination; love of noble character; disregard for wealth (for its own sake); love of justice; ability to guide people to the truth through persuasion; and physical courage.

Crucially, the Philosopher-King must have his intellect connected to the Active Intellect (the Tenth Intellect) — receiving divine illumination. This makes him simultaneously a philosopher, a prophet-like figure, and a legislator. He is the supreme human through whom divine wisdom flows to the community.

Al-Farabi synthesizes Plato's Philosopher-King with the Islamic concept of the Prophet and Imam: the ideal ruler is one whose intellect receives revelation from the Active Intellect — which in Islamic terms is the angel Gabriel (the medium of revelation).

Function of Religion and Philosophy

Al-Farabi makes a striking distinction: Philosophy conveys truth through demonstrative proof (Burhan — for the intellectual elite). Religion conveys the same truth through symbols, images, and persuasion — accessible to the common people. Both serve the same goal of guiding humans to happiness. The prophet and the philosopher are, in ideal cases, the same person — or the philosopher is the successor to the prophet in guiding the community.

Types of Cities (Imperfect Political Communities)

Al-Farabi contrasts the Virtuous City with four types of deficient/wicked cities:

  1. City of Necessity (Daruriyya): Organized merely for material survival and basic needs.
  2. City of Vileness (Nidhaliyya): Organized for wealth — dominated by money and material gain.
  3. City of Baseness (Khissa): Organized for sensual pleasure and licentiousness.
  4. City of Honor (Karama): Organized for domination and glory — where the ruler seeks fame.
  5. Tyrannical City (Taghallubiyya): Organized for conquest and subjugation of others.
  6. Democratic City (Jama'iyya): Allows each person to pursue their own ends — pluralistic but can contain both virtue and vice. Al-Farabi saw democracy as the best of the deficient cities because it can contain virtuous persons within it.

Critical Evaluation

Strengths:

  • Brilliant synthesis of Greek (Platonic-Aristotelian) political philosophy with Islamic thought.
  • Sophisticated theory of the relationship between religion and philosophy.
  • Recognition that political order requires both wisdom and popular legitimacy.
  • His classification of cities anticipates later political science.

Weaknesses/Criticism:

  • The ideal of a philosopher-prophet-ruler is utopian — history shows it rarely (if ever) exists.
  • The Neo-Platonic emanation metaphysics is philosophically problematic and not fully consistent with Islamic theology (particularly regarding creation).
  • Al-Ghazali criticized the overall philosophical project as insufficiently grounded in revelation.
  • The elitist distinction between philosophy (for the few) and religion (for the many) raises questions about equality and truth.
Historical Impact: Al-Farabi's political philosophy was enormously influential on Ibn Sina and through him on medieval European political thought. His synthesis of reason and revelation in political life remains a model of philosophical ambition.

Ibn Sina — Life and Works

Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Sina (980–1037 CE), known in the West as Avicenna, was born near Bukhara (present-day Uzbekistan). A child prodigy who memorized the Quran by age 10 and mastered medicine by 18, he became the greatest philosopher-physician of the medieval world. His major works: Kitab al-Shifa (The Book of Healing — an encyclopedic work on logic, natural science, mathematics, and metaphysics), Kitab al-Najat (The Book of Salvation), and Al-Qanun fi al-Tibb (The Canon of Medicine — used as a medical textbook in Europe until the 17th century).

Major Aspects of Ibn Sina's Philosophy

1. God — Necessary Being (Wajib al-Wujud)

Ibn Sina's most celebrated metaphysical contribution is the distinction between Wajib al-Wujud (Necessary Being — God) and Mumkin al-Wujud (Possible/Contingent Being — everything else). God's essence entails His existence — He cannot not exist. All other beings are possible; they might or might not exist and require an external cause for their existence. This cosmological argument for God's existence was highly influential in medieval European philosophy (Thomas Aquinas adapted it).

2. Emanation and Cosmology

Following Al-Farabi, Ibn Sina developed a Neo-Platonic emanation scheme: from God (Pure Intellect, Pure Being) emanates a series of ten celestial intellects, each associated with a celestial sphere. The Tenth (Active) Intellect governs the terrestrial world — it is the universal mind that illuminates human intellects and is the source of all forms in the material world.

3. Epistemology — Theory of Knowledge

Ibn Sina developed a sophisticated theory of knowledge: the intellect progresses from potentiality to actuality through contact with the Active Intellect. His concept of "floating man" (see below) showed that self-awareness is primary and prior to all sensory knowledge.

4. Prophecy and Religion

The Prophet is the highest form of the philosopher — one whose intellect is directly illuminated by the Active Intellect without intermediary. Religion translates philosophical truth into symbolic, imaginative language for the masses.

Ibn Sina's Theory of the Soul

Nature of the Soul

Ibn Sina defined the soul as "the first perfection of a natural body possessing organs" — borrowing and modifying Aristotle's definition. The soul is the organizing principle that gives life and form to the body. However, Ibn Sina goes beyond Aristotle: for him, the soul is a spiritual (immaterial) substance that is not a property or form of the body — it has its own independent existence.

The Flying/Floating Man Argument

This is Ibn Sina's most famous thought experiment — a direct anticipation of Descartes' Cogito by six centuries:

Imagine a person created fully formed, floating in empty space, with eyes covered, unable to feel, hear, or touch anything — with no sensory input whatsoever. Even in this condition, that person would be aware of his own existence. This proves that the soul (the self) is not identical to the body or dependent on the senses for its self-awareness. The soul knows itself directly and immediately — without any mediation of the senses. Therefore, the soul is a distinct, self-subsisting substance.

Faculties (Powers) of the Soul

Ibn Sina recognized three types of souls, each with its own faculties:

  • Vegetative Soul (Nabatiyya): Found in plants — powers of nutrition, growth, and reproduction.
  • Animal Soul (Hayawaniyya): Found in animals and humans — powers of sensation (five external senses + internal senses) and voluntary movement.
  • Rational/Human Soul (Insaniyya/Natiqah): Unique to humans — power of theoretical and practical intellect.

Internal Senses

One of Ibn Sina's major contributions to psychology: he elaborated a theory of five internal senses (located in the brain's ventricles):

  1. Common Sense (Hiss Mushtarak): Integrates data from the five external senses.
  2. Imagination (Khayaliyya): Retains images after the external object is absent.
  3. Compositive/Estimative Imagination: Combines and separates retained images.
  4. Estimation (Wahm): Perceives non-sensory intentions in sensory things (e.g., the wolf is dangerous — grasped by the sheep, not through senses alone).
  5. Memory (Dhakira): Stores the judgments of estimation.

Immortality of the Soul

Ibn Sina argued that the rational soul is immortal because: (a) It is an immaterial substance that does not depend on the body for its existence (as proven by the Flying Man argument). (b) Immaterial substances cannot be destroyed — only material things composed of parts can decompose and perish. (c) The soul's activity (intellectual knowledge) transcends bodily functions — it does not use the body as an organ but rather the body as a tool. Therefore the soul survives the body's death.

Ibn Sina's soul theory is one of the most sophisticated in medieval philosophy. His Flying Man argument, his theory of internal senses, and his proof of the soul's immortality were all enormously influential — both in the Islamic world and in medieval European scholasticism through Latin translations.

Al-Ghazali — Life, Works, and Philosophy

Al-Ghazali (1058–1111 CE)

Life

Abu Hamid Muhammad al-Ghazali, known in the West as Algazel, was born in Tus (Khorasan, present-day Iran). He studied in Nishapur under Al-Juwaini and was appointed head of the Nizamiyya Academy in Baghdad — the most prestigious academic post of his time — at just 34. After a profound spiritual crisis (1095 CE), he abandoned his position, underwent a Sufi spiritual journey of about 10 years, and returned to reconcile rationalism, orthodoxy, and mysticism. Died in Tus.

Major Works

  • Tahafut al-Falasifa (The Incoherence of the Philosophers) — devastating critique of Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina
  • Ihya Ulum al-Din (Revival of the Religious Sciences) — encyclopedic work on Islamic ethics and mysticism
  • Deliverance from Error (Al-Munqidh min al-Dalal) — his spiritual autobiography
  • The Incoherence of the Incoherence — (answered later by Ibn Rushd)

Key Philosophical Positions

  • Critique of Philosophy: In Tahafut, he identified 20 positions of the philosophers (mainly Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina) as erroneous, and 3 as outright heretical: (1) the eternity of the world, (2) God's knowledge does not extend to particulars, and (3) the bodily resurrection is impossible (philosophers said only the soul is immortal).
  • Occasionalism: Al-Ghazali argued that there is no necessary causal connection between events in nature — God directly causes all events at every moment. Fire does not burn by its own nature; God creates burning whenever He wills.
  • Sufism and Ethics: In Ihya, he provided a comprehensive ethical-mystical framework — the most influential work in Islamic spirituality. He reconciled Shariah (law) with Haqiqa (mystical truth).
  • Epistemology: He prioritized mystical knowledge (Kashf/Dhawq) as the highest form of certainty — beyond both sense perception and rational demonstration.
Impact: Al-Ghazali is arguably the most influential Muslim thinker after the Prophet himself. He shaped orthodox Sunni theology, unified law and mysticism, and his critique of philosophy redirected Islamic intellectual history.

Ibn Rushd (Averroes) — The Commentator
4 marks

Ibn Rushd (1126–1198 CE)

Life

Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Rushd, known in medieval Europe as Averroes, was born in Córdoba (Muslim Spain). He served as a judge (Qadi) and court physician to the Almohad rulers. Near the end of his life he was exiled due to political circumstances but was recalled before his death.

Major Works

  • Extensive Commentaries on Aristotle (short, middle, and long commentaries) — earning him the title "The Commentator" in medieval Europe
  • Tahafut al-Tahafut (The Incoherence of the Incoherence) — a point-by-point refutation of Al-Ghazali's critique of philosophy
  • Fasl al-Maqal (The Decisive Treatise) — on the relationship between philosophy and religious law

Key Philosophical Positions

  • Defense of Philosophy: In Tahafut al-Tahafut, he systematically rebutted Al-Ghazali's critique. He insisted that the study of philosophy is an Islamic religious obligation for those capable of it.
  • Harmony of Philosophy and Religion: In Fasl al-Maqal, he argued philosophy and religion (Shariah) cannot contradict each other since both lead to truth. Apparent contradictions should be resolved through allegorical interpretation (Ta'wil).
  • Eternal World: Against Al-Ghazali, he defended the Aristotelian view that the world is eternal (though created in a special sense).
  • Intellect: Controversial view that the Active Intellect is a single universal intellect shared by all humans — which critics said implies the soul is not individually immortal.
European Impact: Ibn Rushd's Latin commentaries on Aristotle catalyzed the European scholastic tradition. Thomas Aquinas engaged deeply with "the Commentator." He played a crucial role in the European Renaissance of rational thought.

Ibn Khaldun — Sociology, Philosophy of History, and Asabiyyah
4 marks

Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406 CE)

Life

Abd al-Rahman ibn Khaldun was born in Tunis to an Andalusian Arab family. He lived a turbulent political life serving various North African and Andalusian rulers, was repeatedly imprisoned and exiled, and eventually settled in Egypt as Chief Maliki Judge. He met Tamerlane (Timur) in 1401.

Major Works

His masterwork is the Muqaddimah (Introduction/Prolegomena) — the introduction to his universal history Kitab al-Ibar. The Muqaddimah is widely regarded as the founding text of sociology, historiography, and philosophy of history.

Key Concepts

  • Asabiyyah (Social Solidarity/Group Feeling): This is Ibn Khaldun's most famous concept. It refers to the bond of cohesion, solidarity, and collective identity that unites a social group — especially tribal or rural communities. Asabiyyah is the engine of history: strong Asabiyyah enables groups to conquer and establish dynasties; weakening Asabiyyah leads to decline and conquest by a new group with stronger Asabiyyah.
  • Cyclical Theory of History: Dynasties (Dawla) follow a cyclical pattern of approximately 3–4 generations: (1) Rise through strong Asabiyyah and conquest; (2) Consolidation and prosperity; (3) Luxury and weakening of group feeling; (4) Decline and replacement by a new dynasty with stronger Asabiyyah.
  • Philosophy of History: Ibn Khaldun insisted that historical causation operates through natural social laws — not through arbitrary divine intervention in human affairs. He was thus a pioneer of scientific history.
  • Economic thought: He identified the importance of labor as the source of value, supply and demand effects on prices — anticipating modern economics.
Arnold Toynbee called the Muqaddimah "the greatest work of its kind that has ever yet been created by any mind in any time or place." Ibn Khaldun is rightly considered the father of sociology and historiography.

Ibn Tufayl — Hayy ibn Yaqzan and the Autodidact Philosopher

Ibn Tufayl (c. 1105–1185 CE)

Life

Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Tufayl was born near Granada (Muslim Spain). He served as physician to the Almohad ruler Abu Ya'qub Yusuf and is credited with introducing Ibn Rushd to the court.

Major Work: Hayy ibn Yaqzan

His philosophical novel Hayy ibn Yaqzan is one of the most celebrated works in Islamic philosophy. It tells the story of Hayy — a child who grows up alone on a deserted island (either born spontaneously or raised by a doe) and, through pure natural reason and observation, arrives independently at all the truths of philosophy and even mystical knowledge of God — without any teacher, language, or revelation.

Philosophical Message

  • Natural Reason and Revelation: The novel argues that pure unaided human reason (represented by Hayy) can reach the same truths as revealed religion — showing the harmony between philosophy and revelation.
  • Epistemology: Knowledge proceeds from observation → abstraction → philosophical reasoning → mystical illumination. The highest knowledge is direct experiential (Sufi) knowledge of God.
  • Political dimension: When Hayy encounters a religious society (represented by Absal and Salaman), he realizes that most people cannot handle philosophical truth directly and need religious symbols and law.
Historical influence: Hayy ibn Yaqzan was translated into Latin and English and influenced Daniel Defoe's Robinson Crusoe and John Locke's empiricism.

Ibn Bajjah (c. 1085–1138 CE)

Life

Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Bajjah, known in the West as Avempace, was born in Zaragoza (Muslim Spain). He served various Almoravid rulers as a physician, musician, and philosopher. He died in Fez (Morocco) — allegedly poisoned by jealous rivals.

Major Works

  • Tadbir al-Mutawahhid (The Regime of the Solitary) — his most important political-philosophical work
  • Risalat al-Wada (Epistle of Farewell)
  • Works on logic, natural science, and music

Key Philosophical Ideas

  • The Solitary Individual (Al-Mutawahhid): Since the ideal Virtuous City (as described by Al-Farabi) does not exist in reality, the wise philosopher must live as a "spiritual stranger" in an imperfect society. He should isolate himself from the corrupt norms of society and cultivate his intellect through philosophy.
  • Union with the Active Intellect: The highest goal of the philosopher is intellectual union with the Active Intellect — achieving the highest form of knowledge and happiness through philosophy alone.
  • Soul and Intellect: He developed Al-Farabi's and Ibn Sina's theories, emphasizing the intellect's capacity to attain a state of pure spiritual illumination.
  • Theory of Forms: His original contribution to the theory of perception and intellection.
Ibn Bajjah influenced both Ibn Tufayl (who referenced him in Hayy ibn Yaqzan) and Ibn Rushd. His concept of the philosopher as a "spiritual stranger" in society is a remarkable forerunner of later European ideas about the individual and society.

Ibn Maskawaih (Miskawayh) — Ethics and Character Formation
4 marks

Ibn Maskawaih / Miskawayh (932–1030 CE)

Life

Ahmad ibn Muhammad Miskawayh was an Iranian philosopher, historian, and court administrator who served under the Buyid dynasty in Baghdad and Persia. He was the librarian and advisor to several Buyid rulers.

Major Works

  • Tahdhib al-Akhlaq wa Tathir al-A'raq (Refinement of Character) — his philosophical masterwork on ethics
  • Tajarib al-Umam (Experiences of Nations) — historical chronicles

Ethical Philosophy

  • Aristotelian Ethics: Miskawayh adapted Aristotle's virtue ethics for an Islamic context. Virtue (Fadila) is the mean between two extremes (excess and deficiency).
  • The Soul and Its Faculties: He identified three faculties of the soul: the rational faculty (Natiqah), the irascible faculty (Ghadabiyya), and the appetitive faculty (Shahwaniyya). The virtues corresponding to these are: Wisdom (Hikma), Courage (Shuja'a), and Temperance (Iffah). Justice (Adl) is the highest virtue, harmonizing all three.
  • Character can be changed: Against those who believed character is fixed, Miskawayh argued that character (Khuluq) can be reformed through education, practice, and conscious moral effort — making him a pioneer of moral psychology.
  • Social Ethics: He emphasized that humans are social beings — we need each other not just materially but for moral development.
Miskawayh is often considered the first great Muslim philosopher to focus primarily on ethics. His work Tahdhib al-Akhlaq is a landmark in Islamic moral philosophy.

Allama Iqbal — Philosophy of Khudi (Self) and Reconstruction of Islamic Thought
4 marks

Allama Iqbal (1877–1938 CE)

Life

Sir Muhammad Iqbal was born in Sialkot (British India, now Pakistan). He studied in Lahore, then in Cambridge, and received his PhD from the University of Munich. He became the greatest Muslim poet-philosopher of the modern era and is revered as the "Spiritual Father of Pakistan." He wrote in Urdu, Persian, and English.

Major Works

  • The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam (1930) — his most important philosophical prose work (English)
  • Asrar-e-Khudi (Secrets of the Self) — Persian poetry on his philosophy of the self
  • Rumuz-e-Bekhudi (Hints of Selflessness)
  • Bal-e-Jibril, Zarb-e-Kalim, Bang-e-Dara — Urdu poetry collections

Philosophy of Khudi (Self/Ego)

Iqbal's central philosophical concept is Khudi — the self or ego. Against pessimistic Eastern mysticism (especially the Sufi doctrine of Fana — annihilation of self) and Western materialism, Iqbal argued for the affirmation and strengthening of the self.

  • The Self is real and precious: God is the Ultimate Ego; each human ego is a spark of divine creativity. Humans must develop and strengthen their individual selfhood.
  • The Perfect Man (Mard-e-Mu'min): The ideal is the perfect Muslim believer who, like the Prophet, develops his self to its highest potential — creative, autonomous, and God-conscious.
  • Against Fana: Iqbal criticized the Sufi doctrine of self-annihilation as leading to passivity and decline. The goal is not to lose the self in God but to develop the self to such perfection that it becomes a mirror of divine attributes — this is Baqa (subsistence) rather than Fana.
  • Dynamic Islam: Through Ijtihad, Islamic thought must be dynamically reconstructed for each age — not stagnant repetition of the past.
  • Influence: Influenced by Rumi, Nietzsche's will to power (which he critiques and Islamizes), Henri Bergson's creative evolution, and Fichte's philosophy of the self.
Historical role: Iqbal's "Allahabad Address" of 1930 first articulated the idea of a separate Muslim state in northwestern India — the intellectual foundation of Pakistan. He is Pakistan's national poet.

Ibn Khaldun: Life and Context

Abd al-Rahman ibn Khaldun (1332–1406 CE) was born in Tunis and lived through one of the most turbulent periods of Islamic history — the Black Death, the decline of Andalusian civilization, and the Mongol-Timurid devastation. His Muqaddimah (the prolegomena to his universal history Kitab al-Ibar) is one of the most original works in human intellectual history — founding sociology, philosophy of history, and what we might today call political science and economics.

The New Science of Human Civilization (Umran)

Ibn Khaldun's fundamental insight was that history cannot be properly understood without understanding the social laws that govern human civilization (Umran). Previous historians simply recorded events; Ibn Khaldun sought to explain why civilizations rise and fall through causal analysis. He called this new science the science of Umran (civilization/society) — making him the forefather of sociology.

The Concept of Asabiyyah (Social Solidarity)

Asabiyyah is the cornerstone of Ibn Khaldun's system. It means group feeling, social cohesion, tribal solidarity, and collective identity. It is the force that enables a group to act together — to fight, to cooperate, to conquer, and to govern.

  • Sources of Asabiyyah: Common ancestry, shared danger, common values and religion, and prolonged co-habitation.
  • Bedouins vs. Urban populations: Desert/rural groups (Bedouins) have stronger Asabiyyah because shared hardship, necessity, and simplicity forge stronger bonds. Urban populations weaken Asabiyyah through luxury, division of labor, and softness.
  • Role of Religion: Prophetic religion can strengthen Asabiyyah enormously by adding a transcendent spiritual dimension to group solidarity — explaining the rapid early Islamic conquests.

The Cyclical Theory of Dynasties

Ibn Khaldun described a cyclical pattern for dynasties (Dawla) lasting roughly 3–4 generations (~120 years):

  1. 1st Generation — The Conquerors: Strong Asabiyyah; simple, tough, heroic; conquer urban civilization.
  2. 2nd Generation — Consolidation: Establish administration; Asabiyyah still strong; prosperous.
  3. 3rd Generation — Prosperity and Softness: Luxury weakens group cohesion; rulers become autocratic; people lose martial virtues.
  4. 4th Generation — Decline: Asabiyyah severely weakened; dynasty becomes dependent on mercenaries; vulnerable to a new group with stronger Asabiyyah that conquers them — and the cycle begins again.

Economic Thought

Ibn Khaldun made remarkable contributions anticipating modern economics: he recognized that labor is the source of value (anticipating Adam Smith and Marx), described how division of labor increases productivity, explained supply and demand effects on prices, and showed how excessive taxation destroys economic activity — anticipating the Laffer Curve by 600 years.

Geography and Climate

Ibn Khaldun also analyzed how geography and climate affect human character and social organization — anticipating Montesquieu's theory of climate and character by three centuries.

Critical Evaluation

Strengths:

  • Pioneered a scientific, causal approach to history — explaining social phenomena through natural laws, not arbitrary divine intervention.
  • The Asabiyyah concept remains a powerful analytical tool.
  • Remarkably modern in economic reasoning and sociological analysis.

Limitations:

  • The cyclical model oversimplifies — not all dynasties follow this pattern.
  • His analysis was drawn primarily from North African and Arab history — may not universally apply.
  • He underestimated the role of technology, ideas, and long-term structural change.
Conclusion: Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah is rightly described by Arnold Toynbee as "the greatest work of its kind." He founded the sociology of knowledge, philosophy of history, and economic sociology — all while writing in 14th-century Tunisia. His concept of Asabiyyah remains analytically powerful for understanding collective action, nationalism, and political change today.

Al-Ghazali's Critique — Background

Al-Ghazali (1058–1111 CE) first mastered the philosophical tradition — reading all the works of Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina — before critiquing them. He described his method in Deliverance from Error: "I knew that to refute a system before understanding it is to act as a blind man." His Tahafut al-Falasifa (The Incoherence of the Philosophers, 1095 CE) is a systematic demolition of twenty philosophical positions held by Muslim Aristotelians.

The Twenty Points of Criticism

Al-Ghazali identified twenty positions where philosophers contradicted Islamic belief. He declared that on seventeen of these, the philosophers committed heretical innovation (Bid'a), but on three they committed outright apostasy (Kufr):

The Three Points of Apostasy (Kufr)

  1. Eternity of the World: Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina held that the world is eternal — it has always existed. Al-Ghazali argued this directly contradicts Quranic teaching that God created the world from nothing (ex nihilo) — a direct denial of divine omnipotence and creation.
  2. God's Knowledge of Particulars: The philosophers argued that God, being pure intellect, knows only universal forms/essences — not individual particular events or persons. Al-Ghazali argued this contradicts divine omniscience (God knows all things, including every leaf that falls — Quran 6:59) and makes prayer meaningless.
  3. Bodily Resurrection: The philosophers held that resurrection is only spiritual (souls reunite with Active Intellect) — there is no bodily resurrection. Al-Ghazali insisted that the Quran clearly affirms physical bodily resurrection, reward, and punishment — and that denying this is apostasy.

Other Major Criticisms

On Causality — Occasionalism

Al-Ghazali developed his famous critique of necessary causation: philosophers assume that fire necessarily burns cotton — but this is mere habit of observation, not logical necessity. There is no rational necessity connecting cause and effect; God creates burning directly whenever He wills. This occasionalist position protects divine omnipotence and preserves the possibility of miracles.

On Emanation

The philosophers' Neo-Platonic emanation theory (the universe flowing necessarily from God like light from the sun) compromises divine freedom. A God who necessarily creates is not truly omnipotent or free — He is more like an impersonal force than the personal God of Islam.

On Prophecy

Al-Ghazali objected to the philosophers' reduction of prophecy to a natural intellectual phenomenon — the Prophet as merely a superior philosopher. This denies the special, supra-rational nature of divine revelation.

Ibn Rushd's Response (Tahafut al-Tahafut)

Ibn Rushd wrote a systematic rebuttal (Incoherence of the Incoherence) pointing out that Al-Ghazali often misrepresented the philosophers' positions, and that his occasionalist critique of causation actually undermines all rational science and natural knowledge.

Critical Evaluation of Al-Ghazali's Critique

Strengths:

  • Successfully defended the transcendence and freedom of God against mechanistic emanationist schemes.
  • His critique of necessary causation was philosophically sophisticated and remains interesting in philosophy of science.
  • Effectively protected key Islamic doctrines (creation, omniscience, resurrection) from philosophical erosion.

Weaknesses:

  • His occasionalism makes natural scientific explanation impossible — if fire does not naturally burn, how can medicine work?
  • He sometimes misrepresents or oversimplifies philosophical positions (as Ibn Rushd showed).
  • His critique contributed to a decline of the Falsafa tradition in the Islamic East — which some historians see as contributing to scientific stagnation.
Historical Impact: Al-Ghazali's critique was enormously influential — it effectively ended the flourishing of Aristotelian philosophy in the Islamic East. However, it also drove Islamic thought toward a richer integration of Sufism and orthodoxy (as in Ihya Ulum al-Din), which shaped Islamic civilization profoundly for centuries.

Introduction: Iqbal's Mission

Allama Muhammad Iqbal (1877–1938) addressed one of the most pressing problems facing Islam in the modern era: how should Islamic thought respond to modernity — to modern science, Western philosophy, and the intellectual challenges of the 20th century? His book The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam (1930) — originally delivered as six lectures — is his most systematic prose response to this challenge.

Background and Context

Iqbal observed that the Muslim world had reached a state of intellectual stagnation — bound by centuries of uncritical Taqlid (blind imitation) and unable to engage creatively with modern thought. He believed that the Quran itself calls for rational engagement with reality — and that the closing of the gates of Ijtihad (independent reasoning) had paralyzed Muslim civilization.

Key Themes of the Reconstruction

1. Ijtihad as the Principle of Movement

Iqbal insisted that Ijtihad — independent intellectual inquiry and legal reasoning — must be reopened and exercised boldly. Just as the Prophet's companions exercised Ijtihad in new situations, so must Muslims today. Ijtihad is "the principle of movement in the structure of Islam." Without it, Islam becomes a fossilized system rather than a living force.

2. The Quran and the Empirical Spirit

Iqbal argued that the Quran's repeated call to observe nature (the natural world as God's signs, Ayat) was actually the origin of the empirical scientific spirit. The Quran liberated humanity from the Greek tendency to devalue sensory observation in favor of pure abstract reasoning — making the Quran the founding document of empiricism in the broadest sense.

3. Ego (Khudi) and Reality

Reality, for Iqbal, is fundamentally egotic — it consists of selves (egos) arranged in a hierarchy culminating in the Supreme Ego (God). The universe is not dead matter governed by mechanical laws but a living community of selves engaged in creative activity. This personalistic metaphysics is opposed to pantheism and to mechanistic materialism.

4. Prayer and Religious Experience

In one lecture, Iqbal analyzes the nature of religious experience. Against those who dismiss mystical experience as subjective, Iqbal argues that it is a genuine form of knowledge — like sense perception and reason, but oriented toward the ultimate spiritual reality. The namaz (daily prayer) is not mere ritual but a training of the soul to attend to the highest reality.

5. The Concept of God

God in Iqbal's framework is the Ultimate Ego — creative, free, omniscient, and intimately connected to the world He creates. God is not the deistic watchmaker who creates and withdraws, nor the pantheistic absolute who IS the world, but a personal God continuously engaged in creative activity who respects human freedom.

6. Human Freedom and Destiny

Iqbal navigated the free will debate by emphasizing that genuine human freedom is both theologically necessary (moral responsibility) and philosophically defensible. His concept of Khudi (self) implies genuine human agency — humans are co-creators with God, not puppets of determinism.

7. Critique of Western Philosophy and Culture

Iqbal engaged seriously with Western thinkers — Hegel, Nietzsche, Bergson, Kant — both learning from them and critiquing them. He criticized Western materialism, nationalism (as a tribalism that fragments human brotherhood), and colonial exploitation while appreciating the West's scientific achievements.

Philosophical Influences on Iqbal

  • Rumi: The greatest single influence — spiritual master and guide
  • Bergson: Creative evolution and the élan vital influenced his concept of a dynamic universe
  • Nietzsche: Will to power — Islamized and redirected toward spiritual self-development (Khudi)
  • Whitehead: Process philosophy and dynamic reality
  • Fichte: Philosophy of the self (Ego)

Critical Evaluation

Strengths:

  • The most serious modern Muslim attempt to engage Western philosophy without surrendering Islamic identity.
  • The call to reopen Ijtihad remains urgently relevant.
  • His personalist metaphysics provides an alternative to both materialism and pantheism.

Limitations:

  • Some traditional scholars felt he went too far in his reinterpretations.
  • His philosophical synthesis is sometimes inconsistent.
  • His prescription for Ijtihad raises difficult questions: who is qualified? What are the criteria?
Conclusion: Iqbal's Reconstruction remains the most significant modern attempt at Muslim philosophical renaissance. His call to dynamism, creative self-affirmation, and engagement with modernity through Ijtihad continues to inspire Muslim thinkers worldwide. He represents the most successful bridge between the classical Muslim philosophical tradition and contemporary thought.

Origin of the Mutazilites

The Mutazilite school emerged in Basra (Iraq) in the early 8th century. The founding moment (according to tradition) occurred when Wasil ibn Ata (700–748 CE) separated (I'tazala — "withdrew") from the circle of the great scholar Hasan al-Basri over the question of the grave sinner. Hasan al-Basri held that the grave sinner is a hypocrite (munafiq); the Kharijites said he is a kafir. Wasil proposed a third position: the grave sinner occupies an intermediate position (al-Manzila bayn al-Manzilatayn) — neither fully believer nor fully unbeliever. This became one of the five core Mutazilite principles.

The school flourished under the Abbasid caliphs — especially reaching its zenith when Caliph al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833 CE) adopted Mutazilism as the official state theology and enforced it through the Mihna (Inquisition). Scholars who refused to accept the Mutazilite doctrine of the createdness of the Quran were persecuted — most famously Ahmad ibn Hanbal, who suffered flogging and imprisonment for his refusal.

The Five Principles (Usul al-Khamsa)

1. Al-Tawhid (Divine Unity)

God is absolutely one and unique — with no resemblance to creation whatsoever. This led the Mutazilites to deny real divine attributes separate from the essence (to avoid what they saw as Shirk — placing eternal beings alongside God) and to interpret anthropomorphic Quranic expressions allegorically.

2. Al-Adl (Divine Justice)

God is absolutely just — He cannot do evil and would not punish the innocent. This principle requires: (a) that humans have genuine free will (otherwise God punishing people for acts He compelled is unjust); (b) that God cannot be said to have willed evil; (c) that God's promises and threats in the Quran are necessarily carried out.

3. Al-Wa'd wa al-Wa'id (Promise and Threat)

God's promises of Paradise for the righteous and threats of Hell for the sinful are necessarily fulfilled — God cannot fail to keep His promises or empty His threats. This distinguishes them from the Murji'a (who said God may forgive anyone) and the Asharites (who said God's will is absolute and He may forgive or punish as He pleases).

4. Al-Manzila bayn al-Manzilatayn (The Intermediate Position)

The Muslim grave sinner occupies a position between belief (Iman) and unbelief (Kufr) — he is neither fully a believer nor an unbeliever, but a transgressor (Fasiq). He will be in Hell if he dies without repentance, but his Hell is not the same as the Hell of outright unbelievers.

5. Al-Amr bi al-Ma'ruf wa al-Nahy 'an al-Munkar (Commanding Right and Forbidding Wrong)

Every Muslim has a religious obligation to promote good and prevent evil — in the community, in society, and politically. This principle gave the Mutazilites a basis for political activism and justified their advocacy for the Mihna.

Major Mutazilite Scholars

  • Wasil ibn Ata — founder
  • Amr ibn Ubayd — co-founder
  • Al-Allaf (Abu al-Hudhayl) — greatest early Mutazilite thinker
  • Al-Nazzam — innovative on atomism and natural philosophy
  • Al-Jahiz — famous litterateur and Mutazilite thinker
  • Al-Jubbai — teacher of Al-Ashari (before his defection)
  • Qadi Abd al-Jabbar — the greatest systematic Mutazilite (10th century)

Decline of the Mutazilites

The Mutazilite Mihna ended in 848 CE under Caliph al-Mutawakkil, who reversed the policy and supported traditional scholarship. Al-Ashari's defection from Mutazilism and his founding of a new school that used Mutazilite methods against Mutazilite conclusions was a major blow. By the 10th–11th centuries, the Asharite school had largely displaced Mutazilism in Sunni orthodoxy.

Historical Significance

  • The Mutazilites pioneered the use of rational philosophical argument in Islamic theology — creating the tradition of Kalam.
  • Their engagement with Greek logic and philosophy made Islamic theology intellectually sophisticated.
  • The Mutazilite emphasis on divine justice and human free will influenced Shia theology, which adopted many Mutazilite positions.
  • Their critique of anthropomorphism protected Islamic monotheism from literalist distortions.
  • Their political activism (the Mihna) is a cautionary tale about the dangers of enforcing theological orthodoxy through state power.
The Mutazilites may have lost the historical battle for Sunni orthodoxy, but their intellectual legacy — the use of reason in theology — became permanently embedded in Islamic thought through the Asharite and Maturidi schools.
