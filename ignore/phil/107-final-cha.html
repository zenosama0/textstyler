1. Neuron, Central Nervous System, Nervous System & Mental Health

What is a neuron?A neuron is a specialized nerve cell that receives, processes, and transmits information through electrical signals within the cell and chemical signals between cells.

Structure of a neuron

  1. Dendrites: receive signals from other neurons or receptors.
  2. Cell body (soma): contains the nucleus and maintains the cell’s metabolic functions.
  3. Axon: carries an electrical impulse away from the cell body.
  4. Myelin sheath: fatty insulating layer around many axons; increases the speed of neural transmission.
  5. Nodes of Ranvier: gaps in the myelin where the action potential is regenerated.
  6. Axon terminals: release neurotransmitters into the synapse.
  7. Synapse: junction between neurons where chemical communication occurs.

Functions of the central nervous system (CNS)

The CNS consists of the brain and spinal cord. It is the main processing and coordinating system of the body.

  • Receives and integrates sensory information.
  • Produces and coordinates motor responses.
  • Supports thought, memory, language, emotion, and decision-making.
  • Coordinates reflexes with the spinal cord.
  • Maintains homeostasis through interaction with the autonomic and endocrine systems.

Nervous system: broad organization

DivisionMajor role
CNSBrain + spinal cord; central processing and coordination.
Peripheral nervous system (PNS)Nerves outside CNS; carries information to and from the CNS.
Somatic nervous systemVoluntary movement and sensory information from skin, muscles, joints.
Autonomic nervous systemInvoluntary regulation of internal organs.
SympatheticMobilizes the body during challenge or arousal (“fight or flight”).
ParasympatheticSupports rest, digestion, and recovery (“rest and digest”).

Nervous system and mental health

Mental health is influenced by interactions among brain processes, body systems, psychological factors, and the social environment. The nervous system matters because communication among neurons and neural circuits supports mood, attention, memory, stress responses, sleep, and behaviour.

Persistent changes in neural functioning can be associated with psychological symptoms. For example, altered stress-system activity can affect anxiety and sleep; changes in circuits involved in mood and reward can contribute to depressive symptoms. These relationships are not one-cause-one-disorder: genetics, learning, environment, lifestyle, and social conditions also matter.

Remember: Neuron = information unit. CNS = brain + spinal cord. PNS = communication network to and from CNS.

Brain function and behaviour

Behaviour emerges from coordinated brain activity rather than from one isolated “behaviour center.” The brain receives sensory input, interprets information, predicts outcomes, regulates emotion, and organizes action.

Examples: frontal systems support inhibition and decision-making; motor areas help generate movement; reward-related circuits influence approach behaviour; limbic structures contribute to emotion and memory; language networks help produce and understand speech.

Brain function and mental health

Many psychological disorders involve altered patterns of brain activity or connectivity, but biological findings do not mean that a disorder is purely “chemical.” Brain function changes with experience, stress, learning, sleep, social context, and treatment.

Exam-ready point: Mental health is best explained through interaction between biological systems and psychological/social conditions, not through biological reductionism alone.

3. Psychology, Behavioural Methods, Animal Behaviour & Biopsychosocial Science

What is psychology? Psychology is the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes. It investigates how people and other organisms perceive, learn, remember, feel, think, develop, interact, and behave.

Main methods of psychology

  1. Naturalistic observation: observe behaviour in a real setting without manipulating variables.
  2. Case study: detailed investigation of an individual, group, institution, or unusual case.
  3. Survey/questionnaire: gathers self-report information from many participants.
  4. Correlational method: measures the relationship between variables; correlation does not establish causation.
  5. Experimental method: manipulates an independent variable and measures a dependent variable, usually with control and comparison conditions.
  6. Interview: structured or semi-structured questioning to collect detailed information.
  7. Psychological testing: standardized measures of abilities, traits, attitudes, or symptoms.

Why does psychology study animal behaviour?

Animal research helps psychologists understand general principles of learning, motivation, perception, memory, development, and physiological regulation. Some animal studies allow controlled investigation of mechanisms that cannot be studied ethically or practically in humans.

However, findings must be generalized cautiously because species differ biologically and socially. Modern research also requires ethical treatment, justification of animal use, and minimization of harm.

Why is psychology called a biopsychosocial science?

Psychology is biopsychosocial because behaviour and mental processes are shaped by three interacting levels:

Bio

Genes, brain, hormones, neurotransmitters, physiology, sleep, health.

Psycho

Thoughts, emotions, learning, personality, coping, beliefs.

Social

Family, peers, culture, relationships, socioeconomic context, institutions.

Example: anxiety may involve biological vulnerability, learned threat expectations, and stressful social circumstances.

4. Mind and Freud's Structure of Mind & Personality

What is the mind?

The mind refers broadly to the processes through which an organism experiences, represents, interprets, remembers, feels, desires, and thinks about the world and itself. Psychology studies observable behaviour and uses scientific methods to investigate mental processes.

Freud: structure of mind (topographic model)

LevelMeaning
ConsciousThoughts and perceptions currently in awareness.
PreconsciousInformation not currently in awareness but potentially accessible.
UnconsciousThoughts, wishes, memories, and conflicts outside conscious awareness that Freud believed can influence behaviour.

Freud emphasized that behaviour is not always fully explained by conscious reasoning.

Freud: structure of personality (structural model)

PartPrincipleMain role
IdPleasure principleImmediate gratification of basic drives and wishes.
EgoReality principleBalances desires with reality; planning, problem solving, delay of gratification.
SuperegoMorality principleInternalized standards, ideals, rules, guilt.

Example: The id wants to spend all the money now; the superego says that is irresponsible; the ego considers the real budget and chooses what can realistically be afforded.

Evaluation of Freud

Strengths: highlighted unconscious influences, childhood experience, conflict, and the importance of personality dynamics; influenced psychotherapy and later personality theory.

Limitations: some concepts are difficult to operationalize and test scientifically; evidence for specific psychoanalytic claims is mixed; some ideas are culturally or historically limited and rely heavily on clinical interpretation.

5. Learning and Pavlov's Classical Conditioning

What is learning? Learning is a relatively enduring change in behaviour or knowledge resulting from experience or practice.

Characteristics of learning

  • It involves experience or practice.
  • It produces a relatively enduring change.
  • It may change behaviour, knowledge, skills, attitudes, or responses.
  • It is not the same as temporary changes caused only by fatigue, drugs, illness, or maturation.
  • Learning can be adaptive, but it can also produce maladaptive habits.

Elements of learning

Common elements include motivation, stimulus/input, attention, practice, response, reinforcement/feedback, retention, and generalization/discrimination. Different theories emphasize different elements.

Pavlov's classical conditioning theory

Ivan Pavlov showed that a previously neutral stimulus can come to elicit a response after being repeatedly paired with a stimulus that naturally produces that response.

StageExample
Unconditioned stimulus (UCS)Food naturally produces salivation.
Unconditioned response (UCR)Salivation to food.
Neutral stimulus (NS)Bell before conditioning; no salivation-specific response.
ConditioningBell + food are repeatedly paired.
Conditioned stimulus (CS)Bell after conditioning.
Conditioned response (CR)Salivation to the bell.

Key processes

  • Acquisition: learning the association.
  • Extinction: CR weakens when CS is repeatedly presented without UCS.
  • Spontaneous recovery: an extinguished CR may reappear after rest.
  • Stimulus generalization: similar stimuli can produce the CR.
  • Stimulus discrimination: learning to respond differently to distinct stimuli.

Critical discussion

Strengths: experimentally testable, clearly demonstrates associative learning, helped establish objective learning research, and has practical applications such as understanding phobias and some behaviour-change methods.

Limitations: classical conditioning is not a complete theory of human learning. Humans also learn through observation, reasoning, language, expectations, reinforcement, and cognitive processes. Not every learned behaviour is a simple stimulus–response association.

Exam conclusion: Pavlov explains an important form of associative learning, but learning is broader than classical conditioning alone.

6. Motivation, Motivational Cycle, Classification & Maslow

What is motivation? Motivation is the process that initiates, directs, and sustains goal-directed behaviour.

Motivational cycle

Need/Drive → Arousal/Tension → Goal-directed behaviour → Goal attainment → Reduction of tension / satisfaction

Example: hunger creates a physiological need → hunger motivates food-seeking → eating reduces hunger → temporary satisfaction.

Classification of motives

TypeMeaningExamples
Biological / primaryLinked to basic physiological survival or regulation.Hunger, thirst, sleep, temperature regulation.
Social / secondaryLearned through social and cultural experience.Achievement, affiliation, approval, power.
IntrinsicActivity is done for inherent interest or satisfaction.Studying because you enjoy learning.
ExtrinsicBehaviour is performed for an external reward or to avoid punishment.Studying for grades.

Maslow's hierarchy of needs

  1. Physiological needs: food, water, sleep, basic bodily needs.
  2. Safety needs: physical security, stability, protection.
  3. Love and belongingness: relationships, affection, group membership.
  4. Esteem: competence, achievement, respect, self-respect.
  5. Self-actualization: realization of potential, growth, meaning, creativity.

Maslow proposed that people have multiple needs and that lower needs can demand attention before higher growth needs become dominant. Modern evidence does not support a rigid universal step-by-step ladder for every person and culture, so the hierarchy is best treated as a broad motivational framework rather than a strict law.

7. Memory, Elements, Short-Term vs Long-Term, Forgetting

Definition of memory Memory is the capacity to encode, store, and later retrieve information.

Elements/processes of memory

  1. Encoding: transforming information into a usable form.
  2. Storage: retaining information over time.
  3. Retrieval: bringing stored information back into awareness or use.

Attention, rehearsal, organization, elaboration, emotion, context, sleep, and retrieval practice can all influence memory performance.

Short-term / working memory vs long-term memory

FeatureShort-term / working memoryLong-term memory
DurationBrief unless maintainedCan last from hours to a lifetime
CapacityLimited and easily overloadedVery large
FunctionTemporary holding/manipulation of informationLong-term storage of knowledge, skills, experiences
ExamplesRemembering a phone number while dialingRemembering childhood events or learned facts

What is forgetting?

Forgetting is the failure to retain or retrieve information that was previously learned. Forgetting may reflect problems in encoding, storage, or retrieval.

Causes of forgetting

  • Encoding failure: information was never adequately learned or attended to.
  • Decay: memory traces may become less accessible over time, especially without rehearsal.
  • Interference: other information disrupts memory. Proactive interference = old information interferes with new learning; retroactive interference = new information interferes with old memory.
  • Retrieval failure: information may be stored but inaccessible without the right cue.
  • Motivated forgetting: some psychoanalytic and later psychological approaches propose that motivation can influence accessibility, though specific mechanisms are debated.
  • Context/state mismatch: recall can be poorer when the retrieval context or internal state differs from the learning context/state.

8. Personality, Projective Tests & Cattell's Trait Theory

Definition of personality Personality is the relatively enduring pattern of thoughts, emotions, motivations, and behaviours that distinguishes one person from another and influences how the person interacts with the environment.

Characteristics of personality

  • Relatively stable but capable of change.
  • Unique pattern within each individual.
  • Has biological, psychological, developmental, and social influences.
  • Includes thoughts, feelings, motives, and behaviours.
  • Shows consistency across situations but also contains situation-dependent variation.
  • Helps explain characteristic ways of coping, relating, and responding.

Elements/components of personality

Depending on the theoretical approach, personality is discussed in terms of traits, motives, self-concept, temperament, emotional patterns, learned habits, values, and characteristic behaviour patterns. Freud's id–ego–superego model is another classic way of conceptualizing personality structure.

Rorschach Inkblot Test

The Rorschach is a projective personality assessment using ambiguous inkblot stimuli. The individual describes what each blot might represent. Historically, clinicians interpreted responses as reflecting aspects of personality and psychological functioning.

Strength: can elicit open-ended material that may be difficult to obtain through direct questions.

Limitation: interpretation and reliability/validity can vary; modern use generally requires standardized systems and trained professionals.

Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

The TAT presents ambiguous pictures, often involving people or social scenes. The person is asked to make up a story about what is happening, what led to it, and what may happen next. The stories are interpreted as potentially revealing themes of needs, conflicts, relationships, and perception.

Like other projective measures, interpretation depends strongly on the scoring and theoretical system used.

Cattell's trait theory

Raymond Cattell used factor analysis to identify basic personality dimensions. He distinguished between surface traits (clusters of observable tendencies) and source traits (underlying dimensions proposed to account for those patterns). His work led to the 16 Personality Factors (16PF) framework.

Exam point: Cattell viewed traits as relatively enduring tendencies that help explain and predict behaviour across situations.

9. Intelligence, Heredity, Environment & Binet-Simon

What is intelligence? Intelligence is a broad capacity involving learning from experience, reasoning, problem solving, adapting to new situations, and effectively using knowledge.

Elements of intelligence

  • Learning and reasoning
  • Problem solving
  • Abstract thinking
  • Adaptation to changing conditions
  • Understanding complex ideas
  • Using knowledge effectively

Different theories divide intelligence differently; there is no single universally accepted list of “elements.”

Heredity and environment

Intelligence reflects both genetic influences and environmental influences, and they interact. Heredity contributes to individual differences in cognitive potential, while environment affects whether and how that potential develops.

Heredity

Genes, biological development, prenatal conditions, and inherited differences can contribute to cognitive performance.

Environment

Nutrition, health, education, language exposure, family conditions, stress, resources, culture, and opportunities for learning affect cognitive development.

Important: heritability is a population statistic; it does not mean that an individual's intelligence is fixed or that environment is unimportant.

Binet-Simon scale

Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon developed an early intelligence test in the early 20th century to identify children who might need educational support. The test used age-graded tasks involving reasoning, memory, comprehension, and problem solving.

The original work helped establish the idea of mental age. Later intelligence testing traditions used standardized comparisons and, in the classic ratio formulation, IQ = (Mental Age ÷ Chronological Age) × 100.

Modern intelligence tests use more sophisticated standardization and typically report scores relative to an age-based normative distribution rather than relying on the old ratio formula for all ages.

10. Emotion: Elements, Classification & Physiological Changes

What is emotion? Emotion is a coordinated psychological and physiological response to significant situations or stimuli, involving subjective experience, bodily arousal, appraisal, and action tendencies.

Elements of emotion

  1. Physiological arousal: autonomic and bodily changes such as heart-rate changes.
  2. Cognitive appraisal: interpretation or evaluation of what an event means.
  3. Subjective feeling: conscious experience such as fear, joy, anger, sadness.
  4. Expressive behaviour: facial expression, posture, voice, gestures.
  5. Action tendency: readiness to approach, avoid, attack, withdraw, seek help, etc.

Classification of emotions

There is no single universal classification. A common approach identifies basic emotions such as happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust, and surprise, while other approaches emphasize dimensions such as valence (pleasant–unpleasant) and arousal (low–high).

Complex emotions such as guilt, shame, pride, jealousy, and gratitude may involve cognitive appraisal and social learning.

Physiological changes associated with emotion

  • Heart rate may increase or decrease depending on emotion and context.
  • Breathing can become faster or deeper during arousal.
  • Pupils may dilate.
  • Sweating may increase.
  • Digestive activity can change.
  • Muscle tension and posture change.
  • Hormonal and autonomic changes occur during stress and arousal.
  • Facial expressions and vocal characteristics may change.

11. Nerve Cell and Central Nervous System

What is a nerve cell or neuron? A neuron is a specialized cell that communicates information by generating electrical activity and releasing chemical neurotransmitters at synapses.

Central nervous system

The CNS consists of the brain and spinal cord. It interprets sensory information, coordinates movement, supports cognition and emotion, and regulates essential functions.

The spinal cord carries information between the brain and body and also coordinates many reflexes. The brain integrates sensory, cognitive, emotional, and motor processes at a highly complex level.

12. Sensation and the Sensory Organs

What is sensation? Sensation is the process by which sensory receptors detect physical energy or chemical signals from the environment or body and transmit that information to the nervous system.

Sensation through the sensory systems

SenseOrgan/receptorsMain information
VisionEyes; rods and cones in retinaLight, colour, form, movement, depth cues.
HearingEar; hair cells in cochleaSound frequency, intensity, timing.
Smell (olfaction)Olfactory receptors in nasal cavityAirborne chemical molecules.
Taste (gustation)Taste receptors in taste budsSweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami.
Touch / skin sensesSkin receptorsPressure, temperature, vibration, pain.
ProprioceptionMuscles, tendons, jointsBody position and movement.
Vestibular senseInner earBalance, head movement, spatial orientation.

Sensation vs perception

Sensation is detection of sensory information. Perception is the organization and interpretation of that information. Example: the eyes detect light patterns (sensation); the brain recognizes a familiar face (perception).

Previous-year questions — mark-friendly answers

4 marks

1. Why is psychology called a biopsychosocial science?

Psychology is called a biopsychosocial science because human behaviour and mental processes cannot be adequately explained by only one level of analysis. The biological level includes genes, brain activity, hormones, and nervous-system functioning. The psychological level includes thoughts, emotions, learning, motivation, personality, and coping. The social level includes family, peers, culture, relationships, and social conditions. These levels interact continuously. Therefore, psychology integrates biological, psychological, and social explanations to understand behaviour in a more complete way.

4 marks

2. What is nerve-cell?

A nerve cell or neuron is a specialized cell of the nervous system that receives, processes, and transmits information. Its main parts are dendrites, which receive signals; the cell body, which maintains the cell; the axon, which carries impulses; and axon terminals, which release neurotransmitters at synapses. Neurons communicate through electrical and chemical signals and are essential for sensation, movement, thought, memory, and emotion.

4. Causes of forgetting

The major causes are encoding failure, when information was not adequately learned; decay or loss of accessibility over time; interference, when other information disrupts recall; and retrieval failure, when stored information cannot be accessed without an effective cue. Forgetting can also be affected by context changes, stress, lack of attention, and poor consolidation.

12.5 marks

5. What is meant by psychology? Briefly discuss different branches of Psychology.

Definition: Psychology is the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes. It examines how organisms perceive, think, learn, remember, develop, feel, interact, and behave.

Major branches

  1. Biological / biopsychology: studies relationships between brain, nervous system, hormones, genes, and behaviour.
  2. Cognitive psychology: examines attention, perception, memory, language, reasoning, and problem solving.
  3. Developmental psychology: studies physical, cognitive, emotional, and social development across the lifespan.
  4. Social psychology: studies how people influence and are influenced by others.
  5. Personality psychology: studies enduring patterns of traits, motives, and behaviour.
  6. Abnormal / clinical psychology: studies psychological disorders, assessment, causes, and treatment-related processes.
  7. Educational / school psychology: applies psychological principles to learning, teaching, assessment, and educational settings.
  8. Industrial-organizational psychology: studies behaviour in workplaces, including selection, motivation, performance, leadership, and organizational systems.
  9. Health psychology: studies psychological influences on health, illness, treatment adherence, and health behaviour.
  10. Counseling psychology: focuses on adjustment, life concerns, relationships, and wellbeing, often across relatively broad populations.
  11. Forensic psychology: applies psychological knowledge to legal and justice settings.

Conclusion: Psychology is a broad scientific discipline because behaviour and mental life are influenced by biological, cognitive, developmental, individual, and social factors.

12.5 marks

6. Different methods of psychology. Advantages and disadvantages of experimental method.

Psychologists use several methods depending on the question.

  1. Observation — studies behaviour directly in natural or controlled settings.
  2. Case study — intensive study of an individual or small group.
  3. Survey/interview — gathers self-report data.
  4. Correlation — measures relationships between variables.
  5. Experiment — manipulates an independent variable and measures its effect on a dependent variable under controlled conditions.

Experimental method

In an experiment, researchers manipulate an independent variable, measure a dependent variable, and use control/comparison conditions and procedures intended to reduce confounding variables. Random assignment, when feasible, strengthens causal inference.

Advantages

  • Provides strong control over variables.
  • Can test cause-and-effect hypotheses.
  • Often allows replication.
  • Supports precise measurement and comparison.

Disadvantages

  • Laboratory settings can be artificial and reduce ecological validity.
  • Some questions cannot ethically be manipulated.
  • Participant expectations or researcher effects may influence results.
  • Not all psychological phenomena can be reduced to a simple controlled manipulation.

Conclusion: experiments are powerful for causal inference, but psychologists should combine them with other methods when studying complex real-world behaviour.

12.5 marks

7. Explain Freud's theory of the structure of mind and personality.

Structure of mind: Freud proposed conscious, preconscious, and unconscious levels. The conscious contains present awareness; the preconscious includes material that can be brought into awareness; and the unconscious contains mental contents outside awareness that may influence behaviour.

Structure of personality: Freud later proposed the id, ego, and superego. The id follows the pleasure principle and seeks immediate gratification. The ego follows the reality principle and tries to satisfy needs in realistic ways. The superego represents internalized values, standards, and moral demands.

Dynamic view: conflict among impulses, reality, and moral standards can create anxiety. The ego may use defence mechanisms to manage conflict.

Evaluation: Freud's ideas made the unconscious and early development central to personality theory and psychotherapy, but many claims are difficult to test and some have limited empirical support.

12.5 marks

8. Explain and evaluate the nature and function of the nervous system.

The nervous system is the body's rapid communication and coordination network. It receives information, processes it, and produces responses.

Structure

  • CNS: brain and spinal cord.
  • PNS: nerves outside the CNS.
  • Somatic division: voluntary movement and sensory pathways.
  • Autonomic division: involuntary regulation; includes sympathetic and parasympathetic branches.

Functions

  • Sensory reception and integration.
  • Motor control.
  • Thought, memory, emotion, and language.
  • Homeostasis and autonomic regulation.
  • Fast responses such as reflexes.

Evaluation

The nervous system is highly specialized yet integrated. Behaviour cannot be explained by one structure alone because neural systems operate in networks and interact with endocrine, immune, psychological, and social processes. Therefore, a systems perspective is more accurate than a simple “one part–one function” model.

12.5 marks

9. What is personality? How does it influence human behavior? Difference between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation.

Personality is the relatively enduring pattern of thoughts, emotions, motives, and behaviours that characterizes an individual.

Influence on behaviour

Personality influences typical ways of approaching challenges, interacting socially, managing emotions, making decisions, and pursuing goals. For example, a person high in conscientious tendencies may be more likely to plan and persist, while an outgoing person may seek social interaction more often. Personality does not determine behaviour mechanically; situation and learning also matter.

Intrinsic motivationExtrinsic motivation
Comes from interest, enjoyment, or internal satisfaction.Comes from external rewards, approval, grades, money, or avoidance of punishment.
Example: reading because you enjoy learning.Example: reading to earn a grade.

Conclusion: personality provides tendencies, while motivation helps explain why a person starts and persists in particular goal-directed behaviours.

12.5 marks

10. What is learning? How does observational learning help in acquiring skills? What role does reinforcement play?

Learning is a relatively enduring change in behaviour or knowledge resulting from experience or practice.

Observational learning

People can acquire behaviour by watching models, remembering what they do, being able to reproduce the action, and having motivation to perform it. A learner does not have to directly experience every consequence to acquire a new skill. For example, a beginner can learn a procedure by observing an experienced person demonstrate it.

Role of reinforcement

Reinforcement is any consequence that increases the future likelihood of a behaviour. Positive reinforcement adds a desirable consequence; negative reinforcement removes an aversive condition. Neither means punishment. Reinforcement helps strengthen behaviours that are followed by useful outcomes, while lack of reinforcement may reduce some behaviours.

Conclusion: learning can occur through direct experience, conditioning, and observation. Reinforcement influences which learned behaviours are maintained.

12.5 marks

11. Definition of intelligence and limitations of tests used to measure intelligence.

Intelligence refers broadly to the ability to learn from experience, reason, solve problems, adapt to new situations, and use knowledge effectively.

Limitations of intelligence tests

  • Culture and language: test performance can be influenced by cultural knowledge and language familiarity.
  • Narrow measurement: a test samples certain cognitive abilities rather than capturing every form of competence, creativity, wisdom, or practical skill.
  • Context effects: motivation, anxiety, sleep, health, and testing conditions can influence performance.
  • Socioeconomic opportunity: access to education and learning resources affects skills measured by many tests.
  • Misuse of scores: an IQ score should not be treated as a complete measure of a person's worth or future potential.
  • Measurement error: no test is perfectly reliable or free from error.

Conclusion: standardized intelligence tests can provide useful information under appropriate conditions, but scores must be interpreted carefully and in context.
