Lecture 1

Foundations of Macroeconomics

PDF pp. 1-9

What is economics?

Economics studies how society uses scarce resources to produce goods and services and distribute them among people.

Scarcity means resources are limited while wants are unlimited. That is why choices and trade-offs exist.

Very short history in the deck

  • 1776 - Adam Smith: The Wealth of Nations; free markets, competition, and self-interest can support prosperity with limited government intervention.
  • 1936 - J. M. Keynes: The General Theory; after the Great Depression, government spending and policy can help manage downturns.

Microeconomics vs macroeconomics

Micro: individual consumers, firms and specific markets; price determination, consumer behaviour, firm decisions; demand, supply, elasticity, costs and profits.

Macro: the economy as a whole; national income, inflation, unemployment and growth; GDP, inflation rate, unemployment rate and interest rates; fiscal and monetary policy.

The micro-macro linkage

A macro outcome is the combined result of millions of household and firm decisions. The relationship runs both ways: macro policies such as taxation and interest rates also change micro behaviour.

Memory cue: Micro = pieces. Macro = the whole picture.

Key macroeconomic variables

GDP
Total value of goods and services produced
National Income
Income earned by factors of production
Inflation Rate
Rate at which the general price level rises
Unemployment Rate
Share of the labour force without jobs
Interest Rate
Cost of borrowing / return on saving
Exchange Rate
Value of domestic currency vs foreign currency
Money Supply
Money circulating in the economy
Balance of Payments
Record of international transactions
Fiscal Deficit
Gap between government expenditure and revenue
Public Debt
Total government borrowing
Growth Rate
Annual increase in real GDP
Aggregate Demand
Total spending by households and firms
How the system connects

Macroeconomic relationships

Growth channel: higher consumption + investment can raise aggregate demand, GDP and employment.
Inflation channel: higher money supply / aggregate demand can raise inflation; the deck links easing inflation with central-bank interest-rate decisions and borrowing / growth.
Okun's Law link: as output rises, unemployment tends to fall.
Policy is connected: fiscal and monetary policy also affect exchange rates, capital flows and the balance of payments.

Macroeconomic objectives

  • Economic growth: increase in real GDP
  • Full employment / lower unemployment
  • Reducing inflation / stable prices
  • Reducing income inequality
  • Foreign exchange management
  • Debt management
  • Sustainable development

Why study macroeconomics?

  • Understand national income and whether a country is relatively rich or poor using GDP, GNP and NNP.
  • Understand why unemployment exists and how policy can reduce it.
  • Understand inflation and the monetary / fiscal responses used to control it.
Memory cue: growth, jobs, prices, inequality, external position, debt, sustainability.

Stock vs flow

Stock: measured at a point in time. Think “snapshot”. Examples: wealth on 31 March, population on 1 January, money supply today.

Flow: measured over a period. Think “video”. Examples: national income per year, salary per month, GDP per year.

Income is a flow; wealth is a stock.

Short run vs long run

Micro short run: at least one factor of production is fixed. Shoe factory example: machines fixed, labour adjustable.

Micro long run: all factors are variable; the firm can build a new plant, buy technology and expand production.

Macro short run: prices are sticky because contracts, wages or regulations slow adjustment; output and unemployment can fluctuate.

Macro long run: prices are fully flexible, wages and prices adjust, and output returns to its natural level; long-run output depends on real factors rather than the money supply.

Do not mix the two meanings of “short run”. In microeconomics, it is defined by a fixed factor. In macroeconomics, the lecture defines it by sticky prices.
Lecture 2

GDP Measurement and National Income

PDF pp. 10-18

GDP definition

GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a given period of time.

Market value
Market prices reflect willingness to pay.
All
All legal market production included; illegal and unreported activity is not measured.
Final
Intermediate goods are already counted in the final price.
Produced
Only current production; used-goods sales are excluded.
Within a country
Geographic location matters.
Given period
A month, quarter or year can be the measurement period.

Domestic vs national; gross vs net

Domestic = production within the country, regardless of who owns the productive resources.

National = production / income associated with the nation's residents, even when earned abroad.

Gross = before depreciation.

Net = after depreciation.

GDP identity

Y = C + I + G + NX
  • C: household consumption, excluding purchases of new housing.
  • I: purchases of capital goods used to produce more output in the future.
  • G: government purchases of goods and services, excluding transfers.
  • NX: exports minus imports.

What GDP excludes

  • Intermediate goods already counted in final prices
  • Sale of used goods
  • Financial asset purchases
  • Transfer payments
  • Home-produced goods without market transactions
  • Illegal / unreported activities
  • Leisure time, happiness, environmental quality and income distribution

Net exports

NX = X - M

Exports are spending by foreigners on domestically produced goods. Imports are spending by domestic residents on foreign goods.

Nominal vs real GDP

Nominal GDP = Σ(Current price × Current quantity)

Uses the prices of the same year as production. It can rise because prices rise, quantities rise, or both.

Real GDP = Σ(Base-year price × Current quantity)

“Freezes” prices at the base year, so changes in real GDP focus on production volume.

Nominal GDP worked example

Economy: rice and mobile phones.

GoodY1 priceY1 qtyY2 priceY2 qty
RiceTk. 501,000 kgTk. 601,100 kg

Y1 = 50×1,000 + 20,000×100 = Tk. 2,050,000

Real GDP worked example

Use Year 1 prices for Year 2 quantities.

Real GDP (Y2) = 50×1,100 + 20,000×120 = Tk. 2,455,000
  • In the base year, nominal GDP = real GDP.
  • Year 2 nominal GDP is higher than real GDP because prices increased.
  • Real GDP isolates the change in production, while nominal GDP reflects prices and quantities.

GDP deflator

A price index for all final goods and services produced domestically.

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100

For Year 2 in the deck: (2,706,000 / 2,455,000) × 100 = 110.22. With Year 1 as base, 100 = base-year price level, so 110.22 means the overall domestic price level is 10.22% above base.

Inflation from the deflator

Inflation Rate = [(Indexₜ - Indexₜ₋₁) / Indexₜ₋₁] × 100

From 100 to 110.22: 10.22%. Interpretation: the price level of domestically produced final goods and services rose 10.22% from Year 1 to Year 2.

CPI vs GDP deflator: the deck emphasizes that the CPI tracks a fixed consumer basket, while the GDP deflator reflects prices of all domestically produced final goods and services.

Expenditure approach

GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)

Worked example: C = 1.5m, I = 0.6m, G = 0.4m, NX = 0.2m.

GDP = Tk. 2.7 million

Income approach

Production creates income paid to workers, landowners, capital owners and entrepreneurs.

GDP = Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit + Indirect taxes + Depreciation

Worked example: 500 + 100 + 80 + 120 + 50 + 30 = 880.

Research check - why the three GDP methods should match

The BEA describes expenditure, income and production approaches as alternative ways of measuring the same production. In actual data, independently collected sources can create a statistical discrepancy. The deck's Value Added method in Lecture 3 is the production-side approach.

Lecture 3

Value Added, GNP, NNP and the Income Chain

PDF pp. 19-25

Value Added Method

Value added is the value of a firm's output minus the value of the intermediate goods used to produce that output.

Value Added = Value of Output - Value of Input

The purpose is to measure each firm's net contribution and prevent double counting.

Three methods of measuring national income / GDP

Expenditure method
GDP at market prices
Income method
GDP at factor cost plus relevant taxes / depreciation in the deck's formulation
Value added method
Output-side value added

Ideally they give the same figure. A statistical discrepancy can occur because of measurement errors and incomplete data.

Statistical discrepancy

Statistical Discrepancy = GDP (Expenditure) - GDP (Income)

Deck example: 5,200m - 5,150m = Tk. 50 million.

GNP and NNP

GNP = GDP + Factor Payments From Abroad - Factor Payments To Abroad
NNP = GNP - Depreciation

The deck notes depreciation is roughly 10% of GNP and should be subtracted to obtain the net result of economic activity.

National Income

National Income = NNP - Indirect Business Taxes

The worked example uses net indirect taxes:

NIT = Indirect Taxes - Subsidies

Personal Income

Personal income is income received by households and noncorporate businesses. The deck's general formula subtracts corporate profits, social insurance contributions and net interest, then adds dividends, government transfers and personal interest income. The worked example uses only the components actually supplied.

Lecture 4

CPI and Unemployment

PDF pp. 25-34

Consumer Price Index (CPI)

CPI measures the average change in prices of a fixed basket of goods and services over time. It compresses many prices into a single index and is used for cost-of-living changes, wage adjustments, pensions and policy decisions.

Select a base year

The base-year CPI is always 100.

Select a basket

Choose commonly consumed goods and services such as rice, fish, transport or rent.

Use the fixed quantities

The base-year quantities stay fixed for the index calculation.

Calculate CPI

Use the current-year cost of that fixed basket relative to the base-year cost.

CPI = [Σ(Current price × Base-year quantity) / Σ(Base-year price × Base-year quantity)] × 100

CPI worked example from the deck

GoodBase qtyBase priceCurrent price
Rice10 kg5060
Fish5 kg200250
Base basket cost = (10×50) + (5×200) = 1,500
Current basket cost = (10×60) + (5×250) = 1,850
CPI = (1,850 / 1,500) × 100 = 123.33
CPI 123.33 means the basket is 23.33% more expensive than in the base year.

From CPI to inflation

Inflation Rate = [(CPIₜ - CPIₜ₋₁) / CPIₜ₋₁] × 100

Using 123.33 and 100: inflation = 23.33%.

Unemployment: the official idea

The deck defines unemployment as people of working age who are without work, actively seeking employment and available to start a job. They are not in paid employment or self-employment and cannot find work at the prevailing wage.

Employed

Paid employees, own-business workers and unpaid workers in a family business. It also includes people temporarily absent from a job because of vacation, illness or bad weather.

Unemployed

Not employed, available for work and trying to find employment during the previous four weeks; also includes those waiting to be recalled to a job after being laid off.

Not in the labour force

People outside the first two categories, such as full-time students, homemakers or retirees.

Discouraged worker

A person who wants a job but has stopped looking because they believe no work is available. They are counted as not in the labour force, so they are excluded from the official unemployment rate.

Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR)

LFPR = (Labour Force / Adult or Working-age Population) × 100

Unemployment rate

Labour Force = Employed + Unemployed
Unemployment Rate = (Unemployed / Labour Force) × 100

Mathematical problem from the deck

Adult population = 203.1 million; employed = 129.6 million; unemployed = 6.7 million.

Frictional unemployment

Unemployment caused by the time it takes workers and jobs to find a suitable match.

  • Different worker preferences and abilities
  • Imperfect information about vacancies and candidates
  • Limited or slow geographic mobility
Think: “between jobs”.

Structural unemployment

Occurs when workers' skills do not match the skills required by available jobs because of structural changes in the economy.

Causes: technological advancement, automation, decline or shutdown of industries, globalization, regional economic imbalance.

Characteristics: long-term, more serious than frictional, skill mismatch, often requires retraining.

Example: automation replaces garment workers with machines, while some workers lack the technical skills needed to operate the machines.

Cyclical unemployment

Cyclical unemployment occurs because of fluctuations in the business cycle. In a recession, demand for goods and services falls, firms cut production and workers lose jobs. In an expansion, demand rises, firms hire and cyclical unemployment falls.

Causes:
  • Economic recession
  • Lower demand for goods and services
  • Business losses / reduced production
  • Layoffs
Characteristics:
  • Involuntary
  • Related to the business cycle
  • Rises during recession
  • Falls during expansion

Deck example: during the global recession after COVID-19, many businesses reduced employees because sales were low.

Cyclical unemployment + Phillips curve

Cyclical Unemployment, Costs and the Phillips Curve

PDF pp. 34-46

How cyclical unemployment is measured

Cyclical unemployment is measured by comparing the actual unemployment rate with the natural rate.

Natural rate in the deck = frictional + structural unemployment.

Can cyclical unemployment be negative?

Yes. It is negative when the actual unemployment rate is below the natural rate.

Cyclical unemployment = Actual - Natural

Example: actual 3%, natural 5% → -2%. The deck interprets this as an economy operating above potential / overheating.

In a strong boom, labour demand can rise very rapidly: normally unemployed people may quickly find jobs, while labour shortages may cause firms to hire less-qualified workers.

Why unemployment is a problem

Individuals / households

  • Loss of income: no wages or salaries, reducing the ability to purchase food, housing, education and health care.
  • Poverty and inequality: long-term unemployment can push households into poverty and increase vulnerability.

Economy as a whole

  • Waste of human resources: willing and able labour remains unused.
  • Lower national output (GDP): fewer goods and services are produced, so output can fall below potential.

Social and psychological effects

High unemployment can create social instability. The deck links long-term unemployment with depression, anxiety, crime, drug abuse, domestic violence, stress and loss of self-confidence.

Government burden and skill loss

More unemployment benefits and welfare spending, lower tax revenue from reduced income, and possible skill deterioration / human-capital depreciation.

Challenges in measuring unemployment

  • Large informal sector: workers may not have formal contracts or recorded employment.
  • Underemployment: people may have jobs but work fewer hours than desired or work below their skill level.
  • Discouraged workers: people who stop searching can be excluded from the official unemployment count.
  • Hidden unemployment: especially in agriculture, extra workers may contribute little additional output and appear employed in statistics.
  • Seasonal employment: jobs can exist only at certain times of year, complicating point-in-time measurement.

Hidden / disguised unemployment

In the deck's family-farm example, more workers are employed than are actually needed. Even though fewer workers could produce the same output, the extra workers appear employed while their marginal productivity is close to zero.

Key phrase: hidden unemployment is also called disguised unemployment.

The Phillips Curve

In the short run, the Phillips Curve shows an inverse relationship between inflation and unemployment.

Low unemployment → inflation tends to increase.
High unemployment → inflation tends to decrease.

The curve therefore illustrates a short-run trade-off that policymakers may face.

Policy trade-off

Expansionary policy

Raises aggregate demand through higher government purchases, lower taxes or a larger money supply.

  • Unemployment decreases
  • Inflation increases

Contractionary policy

Reduces aggregate demand through lower government purchases, higher taxes or a smaller money supply.

  • Unemployment increases
  • Inflation decreases

Thus governments may try to reduce unemployment at the cost of higher inflation, or lower inflation at the cost of higher unemployment.

Points A and B

The deck's illustration moves from A at 5% unemployment and 2% inflation to B at 3% unemployment and 6% inflation.

Unemployment: 5% → 3%
Inflation: 2% → 6%

Moving from A to B is an up-left movement on the short-run Phillips curve and represents lower unemployment with higher inflation. Expansionary policy is the deck's likely policy explanation.

Research nuance: what this deck does not cover

The deck teaches the short-run Phillips trade-off only. A Federal Reserve source notes that the long-run Phillips curve is treated as vertical in standard macroeconomic analysis, so there is not a permanent long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment. Treat this as extra context, not as a replacement for the slide's short-run framing.

International Business and Trade Barriers

PDF pp. 48-55

International Business

International business is the performance of business activities across national boundaries.

The deck notes that every country is involved in some form of international trade or business, and both large and small firms sell products globally.

Foreign company in Bangladesh: Unilever Bangladesh Ltd.; products include Lux, Sunsilk and Surf Excel.
Bangladeshi company abroad: BEXIMCO Group; exports pharmaceuticals, textiles and ceramics to Europe, the USA and Asia.

Why firms / countries conduct international business

Surplus of goods

A country can sell excess production abroad and use the earnings to buy goods it lacks.

Scarcity of resources

No country has every resource or the capacity to produce everything it needs.

Specialization

Countries may specialize in goods they can produce more efficiently. Deck example: Saudi Arabia specializes in crude oil.

Trade-balance task from the deck: think about why a country should balance exports and imports rather than asking only whether it should export.

Barriers to international business

1. Cultural and social barriers

Differences in values, concepts, family structures, education, religion and customs can affect product acceptance and create costly mistakes. The deck's Bangladesh example says a global fast-food chain may need halal options, local flavours and locally suitable pricing.

2. Political barriers

Political climate and stability create risk. Governments may change policies toward foreign firms, creating uncertainty.

3. Tariffs and trade restrictions

Tools in the deck include import tariffs, quotas, embargoes, local content requirements and dumping / anti-dumping measures.

Import tariff

A duty or tax charged on goods brought into a country.

Tariffs: specific vs ad valorem

Specific tariff

Fixed charge per unit, for example $10 per unit, regardless of the item's cost or value. The deck notes relatively predictable revenue.

Ad valorem tariff

Percentage of value. Higher-value goods pay more, so revenue varies with value.

Export tariffs in the deck

Pros shown

  • Revenue generation
  • Export moderation

Cons shown

  • Reduced competitiveness
  • Trade tensions
The deck also raises the prompt: “Export ban?”

Quotas, VER and quota rent

  • Import quota: direct quantitative restriction on imports; may use licensing-based control.
  • Tariff rate quota (TRQ): a hybrid system with one tariff rate inside the quota and a higher rate above it.
  • VER (Voluntary Export Restraint): exporting-side agreement to limit exports.
  • Quota rent: the economic value created by the scarcity / price difference associated with a quota.

Tariff-rate quota example in the deck

A lower tariff rate of 10% applies to the first 1 million tons of rice imports into South Korea; an 80% out-of-quota tariff applies above that amount. The example then shows 2 million tons imported, with 1 million at 10% and another 1 million at 80%.

Exam picture: x-axis = tons of rice imported; y-axis = tariff rate. The first block is “in quota” at 10%; the second is “out of quota” at 80%.

Balancing trade policy impacts

Tariffs

  • Benefit domestic producers
  • Raise import prices

Subsidies

  • Can help firms compete against foreign imports
  • Can support access to export markets
  • Can lower production costs

Local content requirements

Pros in the deck

  • Boost local manufacturing
  • Protect local jobs
  • Support domestic industry

Cons in the deck

  • Increase consumer costs
  • Limit foreign competition
  • May reduce product quality

Dumping

The final slide frames dumping as unloading excess production into another market.

Pros shown in the visual

  • Unloads excess production
  • Can drive out competitors
  • May be followed by higher prices later

Cons shown

  • Unfair competition
  • Market distortion
  • Harm to local industries
Research check - trade-barrier terminology

The WTO treats quotas, import/export licensing and similar quantitative restrictions as quantitative restrictions, and it explains tariff-rate quotas as lower tariffs within a quota with higher out-of-quota rates. WTO materials also define voluntary export restraints as export-side agreements to reduce exports. This confirms the terminology used in the deck while adding a little precision.

Exam Practice - with Worked Answers

PDF pp. 46-47
How to use this: cover the answer, solve on paper, then open the answer. The numerical questions are solved from the exact practice data shown in the PDF.
1. Define macroeconomics. Give one example of how a macro-level policy change in interest rates affects household or firm decisions.

Answer:

Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole. Example: a lower policy interest rate can reduce borrowing costs, encouraging households to borrow/spend and firms to invest.

2. Differentiate between microeconomics and macroeconomics, with one example of each.

Answer:

Micro studies individual consumers, firms and specific markets, e.g. how a rise in coffee-bean prices changes a coffee shop's decisions. Macro studies aggregate outcomes such as GDP, inflation and unemployment, e.g. how higher government spending during a recession can raise national activity and employment.

3. Classify stock or flow: population on 1 Jan 2026; salary earned during March; money supply today; national income earned during 2025.

Answer:

Population on 1 Jan = stock. Salary during March = flow. Money supply today = stock. National income during 2025 = flow.

4. Explain the difference between the micro short run and macro short run.

Answer:

Micro short run: at least one factor of production is fixed. Macro short run: prices are sticky. Example: a factory cannot immediately change its machines; a wage contract can prevent wages from falling immediately during a downturn.

5. Tea and bicycles GDP problem

2025: tea Tk.40, qty 2,000; bicycles Tk.15,000, qty 150. 2026: tea Tk.45, qty 2,200; bicycles Tk.16,000, qty 170. Base year = 2025.

Answer:

Nominal 2025 = 40×2,000 + 15,000×150 = Tk. 2,330,000
Nominal 2026 = 45×2,200 + 16,000×170 = Tk. 2,819,000
Real 2026 = 40×2,200 + 15,000×170 = Tk. 2,638,000
Deflator 2026 = (2,819,000 / 2,638,000)×100 ≈ 106.86
Inflation 2026 ≈ (106.86 - 100)/100 ×100 = 6.86%

Meaning: the domestic price level was about 6.86% higher in 2026 than in the 2025 base year.

6. National income data problem

Income data (billion dollars): wages 900; rent 200; interest 150; profits 180; indirect taxes 90; subsidies 40; depreciation 100. Expenditure data: C 1,200; I 500; G 350; X 300; M 250. Factor income received from abroad 60; paid abroad 40.

Answer:

Income GDP = 900+200+150+180+90-40+100 = 1,580 billion
Expenditure GDP = 1,200+500+350+(300-250) = 2,100 billion

Statistical discrepancy = 2,100 - 1,580 = 520 billion.

Using expenditure GDP: GNP = 2,100 + 60 - 40 = 2,120 billion
NNP = 2,120 - 100 = 2,020 billion

For the final “policy change” part: new C = 1,200×1.15 = 1,380; new G = 350×1.10 = 385. New expenditure GDP = 1,380 + 500 + 385 + 50 = 2,315 billion, an increase of 215 billion over the original expenditure GDP, about 10.24%.

Important: because the practice data produce a large statistical discrepancy, GNP/NNP can differ depending on which GDP estimate you carry forward. The answer above uses the expenditure GDP requested in part (c); using the income GDP instead would give GNP 1,600 and NNP 1,500.
7. Country Z labour-force changes

Initial employed 3,600k; unemployed 420k; not in labour force 1,500k. Then 55k unemployed find jobs; 70k employed lose jobs; 18k unemployed stop looking.

Answer:

After 55k find jobs: employed +55, unemployed -55. After 70k lose jobs: employed -70, unemployed +70. After 18k stop looking: unemployed -18, not-in-labour-force +18.

Final employed = 3,585k
Final unemployed = 417k
Final not in labour force = 1,518k
Labour force = 3,585 + 417 = 4,002k
UR ≈ 417/4,002×100 = 10.42%
LFPR = 4,002/5,520×100 = 72.50%
8. Working-age population 180m; employed 110m; unemployed 8m.

Answer:

Labour force = 110 + 8 = 118m
UR = 8/118×100 ≈ 6.78%
LFPR = 118/180×100 ≈ 65.56%
9. Natural unemployment 4.5%, actual unemployment 6.2%; then actual falls to 3% while natural stays 5%.

Answer:

Case 1: cyclical = 6.2 - 4.5 = +1.7%

Positive cyclical unemployment means the economy is below its normal employment level / has slack.

Case 2: cyclical = 3 - 5 = -2%

Negative cyclical unemployment means actual unemployment is below the natural rate and the economy is operating above potential / overheating.

10. Define unemployment. Distinguish frictional, structural and cyclical. Why is cyclical more closely tied to the business cycle?

Answer:

Unemployment: working-age people without work who are actively seeking and available for work. Frictional: time spent finding a suitable job. Structural: skills do not match jobs after structural change. Cyclical: joblessness caused by business-cycle downturns and low aggregate demand. Cyclical is directly linked to recessions and expansions, while frictional and structural can exist even when the economy is operating normally.

11. Fixed basket: 8 rice, 4 milk, 6 sugar. Prices: 2023 = 45, 60, 90; 2024 = 50, 68, 95; 2025 = 58, 75, 110. Base year 2023.

Answer:

2023 basket = 8×45 + 4×60 + 6×90 = 1,140
2024 basket = 8×50 + 4×68 + 6×95 = 1,242
2025 basket = 8×58 + 4×75 + 6×110 = 1,424
CPI 2024 = 1,242/1,140×100 ≈ 108.95
CPI 2025 = 1,424/1,140×100 ≈ 124.91
Inflation 2024→2025 = (1,424-1,242)/1,242×100 ≈ 14.65%
12. CPI in 2026 = 108. What does it imply? Compare CPI with GDP deflator. Is inflation always bad?

Answer:

CPI 108 means the fixed consumer basket costs 8% more than in the base year. CPI is built from a fixed consumer basket; the GDP deflator covers prices of domestically produced final goods and services. For consumer cost of living, CPI is more directly relevant; for the broad domestic-production price level, the GDP deflator is broader. Inflation is not automatically bad: moderate and stable inflation can coexist with economic growth, while high or volatile inflation can create significant costs.

13. Define inflation. State the two types. Explain demand-pull and cost-push inflation using AD-AS.

Answer:

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. The two types in the practice set are demand-pull and cost-push.

Demand-pull: aggregate demand rises faster than the economy's capacity, pushing the price level up. Causes can include higher government spending, easier monetary conditions and stronger overall demand.
Cost-push: input costs rise or supply is disrupted, shifting short-run aggregate supply left and raising the price level while reducing output. Causes can include higher wages, raw-material / energy costs, taxes or supply disruptions.
14. Point A: unemployment 6%, inflation 2%. Point B: unemployment 3%, inflation 7%.

Answer:

On a short-run Phillips Curve, the economy moves up and left: lower unemployment is associated with higher inflation. The policy most consistent with this move is expansionary policy, because it raises aggregate demand and, in the short run, lowers unemployment while increasing inflation.

One-Screen Formula Sheet

All formulas below appear in or directly follow the deck's worked examples

GDP and price indices

Y = C + I + G + (X - M)
NX = X - M
Nominal GDP = Σ(P_current × Q_current)
Real GDP = Σ(P_base × Q_current)
GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100
Inflation = [(Indexₜ - Indexₜ₋₁)/Indexₜ₋₁] × 100
CPI = [Σ(P_current × Q_base) / Σ(P_base × Q_base)] × 100

Income accounting

GDP = Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit + Indirect taxes - Subsidies + Depreciation
GNP = GDP + NFIA
NNP = GNP - Depreciation
NIT = Indirect taxes - Subsidies
NI = NNP - NIT
PDI = PI - Personal taxes

Labour market

Labour Force = Employed + Unemployed
UR = (Unemployed / Labour Force) × 100
LFPR = (Labour Force / Adult population) × 100
Cyclical unemployment = Actual UR - Natural UR
Natural rate = frictional + structural unemployment (deck framing).

Trade barriers

Specific tariff = fixed charge per unit
Ad valorem tariff = percentage of value

Quota: quantity restriction. TRQ: lower in-quota tariff + higher out-of-quota tariff. VER: voluntary export restraint. Dumping: deck's excess-production framing.

Outside-Source Checks

GDP accounting

The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis confirms the standard expenditure identity and explains that expenditure, income and production approaches are conceptually measuring the same output. The deck's statistical-discrepancy treatment fits this framework.

Labour statistics

The deck's Bangladesh figures are not all the same concept or period. Contemporary reporting based on BBS supports the 2024 annual unemployment figure of 3.66%, the 9.65% youth rate and roughly 84% informal employment. The 4.63% figure is associated with the October-December 2024 quarterly survey period published in 2025. For the exam, use the period and wording your lecturer's slide uses.

Phillips Curve

The Federal Reserve source used for the research check emphasizes that the lecture's short-run inverse relationship should not be interpreted as a permanent long-run trade-off.

Trade restrictions

WTO materials confirm the terminology around quantitative restrictions, tariff-rate quotas and voluntary export restraints, and explain that TRQs use a lower in-quota tariff and higher out-of-quota tariff.
