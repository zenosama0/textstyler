01

What is Economics? — Scarcity, Trade-offs & Opportunity Cost

Lecture 1
Economics is the study of how people and societies make choices when resources are limited but wants are unlimited.

"We cannot have everything we want, so we must choose." — Example: with 500 taka you must choose between buying a book or going out to eat.

Branches of Economics

Microeconomics

The study of how households and firms make decisions and how they interact in specific markets — supply, demand and price mechanisms that determine prices and resource distribution.

Macroeconomics

Studies economy-wide phenomena such as inflation, unemployment, and economic growth.

Trade-offs

"There ain't no such thing as a free lunch." To get one thing we like, we usually have to give up another. Making decisions requires trading off one goal against another.

Guns vs. Butter

Guns: more spent on national defense to protect the country from foreign aggressors.
Butter: less left for consumer goods that raise the standard of living at home.

Efficiency vs. Equality

Efficiency: society gets the maximum benefit from scarce resources — the size of the economic pie.
Equality: how uniformly those benefits are distributed — how the pie is sliced.

Opportunity Cost

The opportunity cost of an item is what you give up to get that item — the next best alternative forgone.
Example: A year spent listening to lectures, reading textbooks and writing papers means you cannot work at a job. For most students the earnings given up are the largest single cost of their education.

Opportunity cost is not all alternatives — only the next-best one.

Scarcity

Scarcity = limited resources vs. unlimited wants.
TimeMoneyLandLaborCapital
Example: Only 10 seats in a class, but 40 students want to enroll — that is scarcity.

Scarcity forces choice, and choice creates opportunity cost.

Marginal Analysis

Rational People

People who systematically and purposefully do the best they can to achieve their objectives.

Marginal Change

A small incremental adjustment to a plan of action.

"Rational people think at the margin." A rational decision-maker takes an action if and only if the marginal benefit exceeds the marginal cost.

Basic Economic Decisions

What to produce?

Food or cars? Books or weapons?
Solved by the Production Possibility Frontier

How to produce?

Labor-intensive or machine-intensive?
Solved by the Iso-Quant

For whom to produce?

Rich, poor, or an equal distribution?

Positive vs. Normative Analysis

Positive Analysis

Descriptive statements about how the world is; can be confirmed or refuted with evidence (e.g. evaluating minimum wage vs. unemployment data).

Normative Analysis

Prescriptive statements about how the world ought to be; cannot be judged by data alone — involves ethics, religion and political philosophy.

Graphing in Economics

X-axis: independent variable  •  Y-axis: dependent variable.
Common graphs: demand curve, supply curve, cost curves, Production Possibility Curve (PPC).

Short Run vs. Long Run

Short Run

At least one factor of production is fixed and cannot be changed immediately.
Example: a shoe factory's machines (capital) are fixed; labour is variable.

Long Run

All factors of production are variable — the firm can build new plants, buy new technology, or enter/exit the market.

Economic Systems & The 10 Principles of Economics

Lecture 2
An economic system is the way a society organizes production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services. It answers What?, How?, and For whom? to produce — necessary because of scarcity, unlimited wants, the need for efficient allocation, and the need for income distribution.

The Four Types of Economic Systems

1 · Traditional Economy

Decisions based on customs, traditions and long-established practices. The oldest economic system — common in agriculture, hunting, fishing and gathering societies. Still found in: Ethiopia, tribal Africa, rural Asia.

Advantages

Strong social cooperation; economic stability from known roles; less competition/stress.

Disadvantages

Low productivity; very slow growth; low living standards; limited occupational choice.

2 · Command (Planned) Economy

Government makes all major production/distribution decisions, owning most resources. Popularized in the 20th century by socialist revolutions influenced by Karl Marx. Examples: former Soviet Union, North Korea.

Advantages

Reduces income inequality; can mobilize resources quickly; affordable basic goods for all.

Disadvantages

Lacks incentives; shortages/surpluses from ignoring demand; inefficiency and bureaucracy.

3 · Market Economy

Individuals and private firms decide production/consumption via prices, profit and loss. A pure hands-off version is laissez-faire. Adam Smith's "invisible hand" (The Wealth of Nations) guides markets without direct government control. Examples: United States, Singapore.

Advantages

Promotes efficiency and innovation; freedom of choice; supports growth via competition.

Disadvantages

Income inequality; monopoly power; instability from business cycles.

4 · Mixed Economy

Combines market and command elements — private sector and government both play a role. Most countries follow this after the Great Depression / WWII expansion of government's regulatory role. Examples: Bangladesh, India, United Kingdom.

Advantages

Balances efficiency with welfare; reduces extreme inequality via taxes; protects consumers/workers.

Disadvantages

Intervention may create inefficiency; bureaucracy; conflict between market forces and policy.

Every modern economy blends elements of all four models in different proportions.

The 10 Principles of Economics

1. People Face Trade-offs — there ain't such thing as a free lunch.
2. The Cost of Something Is What You Give Up to Get It — opportunity cost.
3. Rational People Think at the Margin — small incremental adjustments to a plan.
4. People Respond to Incentives — an incentive induces action via the prospect of reward or punishment.
5. Trade Can Make Everyone Better Off — countries and families both gain by specializing and trading.
6. Markets Are Usually a Good Way to Organize Economic Activity — decentralized decisions of firms/households.
7. Governments Can Sometimes Improve Market Outcomes — property rights need enforcement (police/courts) for the invisible hand to work.
8. A Country's Standard of Living Depends on Its Ability to Produce Goods and Services — via productivity: output per unit of labor input.
9. Prices Rise When the Government Prints Too Much Money.
10. Society Faces a Short-Run Trade-off Between Inflation and Unemployment — more money → more spending/demand → firms hire more (lower unemployment) before prices rise.

The Three Basic Economic Problems, PPF & Iso-Quants

Lecture 3

Because wants are unlimited and resources scarce, society must make choices — every choice involves opportunity cost. This creates the Three Basic Economic Problems: What to produce? How to produce? For whom to produce? — true in every economic system (market, command, mixed).

Inputs and Outputs

Land

Natural resources — the gift of nature: farmland, energy resources, minerals like copper and iron ore.

Labor

Human time spent in production — factory work, writing software, teaching, baking.

Capital

Durable produced goods used to produce other goods — machines, roads, computers, buildings.

Output

The useful goods/services resulting from production, consumed or used in further production.
Example: To make a pizza — eggs, flour, heat, oven and the chef's labor are inputs. The pizza is the output.

Problem 1 — What to Produce? The Production-Possibility Frontier

The PPF is an economic model illustrating the maximum possible output combinations of two goods an economy can produce, using limited resources and current technology.

Assumptions: only two goods are produced; the same resources make both; quantity/quality of resources is constant; technology is constant; resources are fully employed; production methods are efficient.

Guns & Butter example: the economy can produce 15,000 guns if no butter is made, or 5 million lbs of butter if no guns are made.

Below the PPF
Attainable but inefficient — underutilization of resources (unemployment, business cycles).

Shifts and Rotation

The whole PPF can shift inward (a natural disaster or resource decline — e.g. the 2011 Japanese tsunami) or outward (economic growth from more/better resources or improved technology). A rotation occurs when productive capacity changes for only one of the two goods, pivoting the curve on one axis — unlike a shift, which changes capacity for both goods.

Problem 2 — How to Produce? The Iso-Quant

An isoquant curve shows the various combinations of two inputs (factors of production) that yield the same total output. "Iso" = equal, "quant" = quantity. Isoquants are downward-sloping and convex to the origin.

Problem 3 — For Whom to Produce?

Determines how limited output is distributed among the population — based on income, purchasing power, or need.

Income Distribution

Higher income → higher purchasing power → more consumption.

Distribution Type

Personal: which households get goods. Functional: how income is shared among land, labor, capital.

Economic Systems

Free market: driven by demand/profit. Planned economy: state determines distribution via quotas.

Markets & The Theory of Demand

Lecture 4
A market is a group of buyers and sellers of a good or service. Buyers determine demand; sellers determine supply.
Competition: a market with many buyers and sellers such that no single one can influence the market price. A perfectly competitive market requires (1) identical goods and (2) so many buyers/sellers that no one has price influence — all are price takers.

Quantity Demanded & the Law of Demand

Quantity demanded: the amount of a good buyers are willing and able to purchase.
Law of Demand: other things equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls — and vice-versa.

Shifts in the Demand Curve

The demand curve holds other things constant, so it can shift. A change that increases quantity demanded at every price shifts demand right (increase); a decrease shifts it left.

Variables That Shift Demand

1. Income

Normal good: ↑ income → ↑ demand (e.g. ice cream).
Inferior good: ↑ income → ↓ demand (e.g. bus rides, as people switch to cars/Uber).

2. Price of Related Goods

Substitutes: ↑ price of one → ↑ demand for the other (hot dogs & hamburgers, tea & coffee).
Complements: ↑ price of one → ↓ demand for the other (electricity & A/C, peanut butter & jelly).

3. Tastes

If you like pistachio ice cream, you buy more of it.

4. Expectations

Expecting higher income tomorrow may raise spending today; expecting a price drop may delay purchases.

5. Number of Buyers

Market demand depends on how many buyers exist — an extra consumer raises quantity demanded at every price.

Shifts in the Supply Curve

Input Prices

↑ input price → less profitable → firms supply less. Supply is negatively related to input prices.

Technology

Better technology reduces cost of production → raises supply (e.g. the mechanized ice-cream machine).

Expectations

Expecting a future price rise → firm stores output now → supplies less today.

Number of Sellers

Fewer sellers → market supply decreases.

A rise in quantity supplied at every price shifts supply right (increase); a fall shifts it left (decrease).

Supply and Demand Together — Equilibrium

Equilibrium: the market price at which quantity supplied equals quantity demanded — the equilibrium price (market-clearing price) and equilibrium quantity.
Fig. 8 — Equilibrium of Supply and Demand: P* = $2.00, Q* = 7 cones

Surplus & Shortage

Surplus (Excess Supply)

Price above equilibrium (e.g. $2.50): Qs (10) > Qd (4). Sellers cut prices → price falls toward equilibrium.

Shortage (Excess Demand)

Price below equilibrium (e.g. $1.50): Qd (10) > Qs (4). Sellers raise prices → price rises toward equilibrium.
Fig. 9 — Markets Not in Equilibrium: Surplus at $2.50, Shortage at $1.50

Comparative Statics

↑ Demand (e.g. hot summer)

Demand curve shifts right → equilibrium price rises ($2.00→$2.50) and equilibrium quantity rises (7→10).

↓ Supply (e.g. sugar price ↑)

Supply curve shifts left → equilibrium price rises ($2.00→$2.50) and equilibrium quantity falls (7→4).

When both curves shift simultaneously, price always moves in the direction of the shifts' combined pressure, but the quantity outcome depends on which shift is larger — quantity can rise or fall.

Elasticity — Price, Income & Cross-Price

Lecture 6
Elasticity measures the responsiveness of one economic variable to changes in another — how much consumers/producers react to changes in price, income, or the price of related goods.
The Pizza Dough Analogy: demand is like pizza dough. Warm dough stretches a lot with little effort (elastic); cold dough is stiff and doesn't pull apart easily (inelastic). Elasticity is about percentage change, not absolute change — it allows comparison across goods and markets.

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

PED measures how much quantity demanded responds to a price change = % change in Qd ÷ % change in price. PED is always reported as an absolute value.
\[ PED = \dfrac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P} \]
Example: a 10% price rise causes quantity demanded to fall 20%. \(PED = \dfrac{20\%}{10\%} = 2\) — the change in quantity is proportionately twice as large as the price change.

The Midpoint (Point) Method

\[ PED = \dfrac{\Delta Q_d}{\Delta P}\times\dfrac{P}{Q_d} \]
Worked Example: \(P_1=10,\ P_2=12\); \(Q_1=100,\ Q_2=80\).
Step 1: \(\Delta Q = 80-100=-20\); \(\Delta P = 12-10=2\)
Step 2: \(PED = \left(\dfrac{-20}{2}\right)\times\left(\dfrac{10}{100}\right) = -1\) → take absolute value → |PED| = 1
Step 3: Interpretation → |PED| = 1 → Elastic demand (source's stated interpretation)
ResultInterpretation
|PED| > 1Elastic
|PED| = 1Unit elastic
|PED| < 1Inelastic

Determinants of Price Elasticity of Demand

Availability of Close Substitutes

More substitutes → more elastic (e.g. tea and coffee).

Necessities vs. Luxuries

Necessities (rice) → inelastic. Luxuries (a lavish vacation) → elastic.

Time Horizon

Demand is more elastic in the long run — people have time to switch to substitutes.

Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

Measures how demand responds to changes in income. Positive YED = normal good; negative YED = inferior good.
\[ YED = \dfrac{\Delta Q_d}{\Delta Y}\times\dfrac{Y}{Q_d} \]
Worked Example: \(Y_1=1000,\ Y_2=1100\); \(Q_1=50,\ Q_2=55\).
\(YED = \dfrac{5}{100}\times\dfrac{1000}{50} = 0.05\times20 = 1.0\)
Interpretation: YED = 1 → demand is unit income elastic — quantity demanded rises proportionately with income.
ResultInterpretation
YED > 1Luxury / Normal good
0 < YED < 1Necessity / Normal good
YED = 0Neutral
YED < 0Inferior good

Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (XED)

Measures how the quantity demanded of good X changes when the price of good Y changes.
\[ XED = \dfrac{\Delta Q_x}{\Delta P_y}\times\dfrac{P_y}{Q_x} \]
Worked Example: Price of tea: \(P_1=10\to P_2=12\) (\(\Delta P_t=2\)). Quantity of coffee: \(Q_1=40\to Q_2=50\) (\(\Delta Q_c=10\)).
\(XED = \dfrac{10}{2}\times\dfrac{10}{40} = 5\times0.25 = 1.25\)
Interpretation: XED = +1.25 → tea and coffee are substitutes — demand for coffee rises when tea becomes more expensive.
ResultInterpretation
XED > 0Substitute goods
XED < 0Complements
XED = 0Unrelated goods

Consumer Theory — Utility, Indifference Curves & Equilibrium

Lecture 7 & 8
Consumer Theory explores how individuals make consumption decisions to maximize satisfaction (utility) subject to limited income and given prices.

Utility

Utility is the level of satisfaction or pleasure a consumer derives from consuming goods/services — subjective, but assumed measurable in hypothetical units called "utils" for analysis.

Cardinal Utility

Assumes satisfaction can be measured numerically (e.g. 10 or 20 utils). Developed by Marshall — but precise measurement is unrealistic.

Ordinal Utility

Assumes consumers cannot measure utility but can rank preferences (prefer tea over coffee, without saying by how much). Developed by Hicks & Allen — basis of modern consumer theory.

Properties of the Indifference Curve

1. Downward Sloping

More of one good requires giving up some of the other to hold satisfaction constant.

2. Convex to the Origin

Due to a diminishing marginal rate of substitution (MRS) — willingness to substitute decreases.

3. Higher IC = Higher Satisfaction

Curves farther from the origin represent greater utility.

4. ICs Never Intersect

Crossing would violate consistency of preferences.

The Budget Line

The budget line shows all combinations of two goods a consumer can afford given income and prices — a straight, negatively sloped line whose intercepts are the maximum quantity of each good purchasable alone.
Example: Income (M) = 100, \(P_x = 2\) → X-intercept = 50. \(P_y = 1\) → Y-intercept = 100.

Consumer Equilibrium

Consumer equilibrium is reached where the highest attainable indifference curve is tangent to the budget line — satisfaction is maximized and no reallocation of income can improve utility further.

Producer Theory — Production, Productivity & Diminishing Returns

Lecture 9–11
Production is the process of transforming inputs (labor, capital, land, technology) into output (goods and services). The goal is to maximize output or profit using limited resources efficiently.

Factors of Production

Land

Natural resources — soil, water, minerals.

Labor

Human effort — physical and mental.

Capital

Machinery, tools, infrastructure.

Entrepreneurship

Decision-making, risk-taking, innovation.

The Production Function

A mathematical relationship expressing how quantities of inputs combine to produce output.
\[ Q = f(L,K) \]

Q = Output, L = Labor, K = Capital. More/better use of inputs generally increases output, depending on efficiency and technology.

Fixed vs. Variable Input

Fixed Input

Cannot be changed in the short run; quantity stays constant regardless of output. e.g. machinery, factory building, land.

Variable Input

Can be changed in the short run; varies with production level. e.g. labor, raw materials, electricity.

Short-Run vs. Long-Run Production

Short Run

At least one factor is fixed (usually capital). \(Q = f(L,\bar K)\) — only labor changes, giving rise to the law of diminishing returns.

Long Run

All factors are variable. \(Q = f(L,K)\) — the firm can change the scale of production, leading to returns to scale (increasing, constant, or decreasing).

Types of Productivity

Total Product (TP): total output produced with given inputs.
Marginal Product (MP): extra output from one more unit of a variable input, other inputs held constant. \( MP=\dfrac{\Delta TP}{\Delta L} \)
Average Product (AP): output per unit of variable input. \( AP=\dfrac{TP}{L} \)

The Three Stages of Production

Stage 1 — "Growth" (Increasing Returns)

0 → where AP is maximum. TP rises at an increasing rate; MP rising, reaches its max; AP rising (MP > AP). Not rational — fixed factors are under-utilized.

Stage 2 — "Efficiency" (Diminishing Returns)

AP max → MP = 0. TP rises at a decreasing rate; MP positive but falling (MP < AP); AP falling. Rational stage — firms operate here.

Stage 3 — "Decline" (Negative Returns)

MP < 0. TP starts falling; AP keeps falling. Irrational — production should stop before this stage.

Law of Diminishing Returns

As more units of a variable input (labor) are added to fixed inputs (capital), the marginal product of the variable input eventually decreases, holding technology constant.
Key Condition 1
At least one input is fixed (short run).
Key Condition 2
Technology remains unchanged.
Key Condition 3
Units of the variable input are homogeneous.

Question 2

If the price of a good rises from Tk 40 to Tk 60, and as a result the quantity demanded falls from 90 units to 70 units.
a) Which good is likely to have the more elastic demand? Explain using the determinants of elasticity.
b) Calculate the PED and comment about the nature of the good.

Answer

b) Calculating PED (using the point/midpoint method from Ch.6, applying the initial values \(P_1,Q_1\) as in the worked example):

\(P_1=40,\ P_2=60 \Rightarrow \Delta P = 20\)  |  \(Q_1=90,\ Q_2=70 \Rightarrow \Delta Q = -20\)

\[ PED=\left(\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P}\right)\times\left(\dfrac{P_1}{Q_1}\right)=\left(\dfrac{-20}{20}\right)\times\left(\dfrac{40}{90}\right)= -0.444 \]

|PED| ≈ 0.44, which is less than 1 → Demand is Inelastic. A 50% rise in price produced only a proportionately smaller (≈22%) fall in quantity demanded.

a) Why is this good relatively inelastic? Using the determinants of elasticity from Ch.6:

  • Availability of substitutes — a good with few close substitutes tends to be inelastic, because consumers cannot easily switch away when price rises. The small drop in quantity here suggests limited substitutes.
  • Necessity vs. luxury — necessities have inelastic demand (like rice), while luxuries are elastic. The muted response to a large price increase suggests this good behaves like a necessity.
  • Time horizon — demand is more elastic in the long run; this measured, short-run response is naturally closer to inelastic.

A good with more elastic demand would instead be a luxury with many close substitutes, so a comparable price rise would trigger a proportionately larger fall in quantity demanded (|PED| > 1).

Question 5

A small business owner uses her own building (which she could otherwise rent out for Tk 15,000/month) to run her shop, and pays herself no salary even though she works full-time. a) Identify explicit vs. implicit costs. b) Explain why economists include implicit costs.

Answer

a) Explicit vs. Implicit Costs:

Explicit Costs

Any cash payments she actually makes to run the shop — e.g. cost of inventory/stock, utility bills, supplies, and wages paid to any hired staff.

Implicit Costs

Forgone rent of Tk 15,000/month — the rental income she gives up by using her own building instead of renting it out.
Forgone salary — the wage/salary she could have earned working elsewhere, since she pays herself nothing despite working full-time.

b) Economists include implicit costs because total economic cost = explicit cost + implicit cost, and implicit costs represent the true opportunity cost of resources the owner already possesses (Ch.1). Even though no cash changes hands, her building and her labor time both have valuable alternative uses. Ignoring implicit costs would overstate her true profit (accounting profit) and give a misleading picture of whether the business is really worth running compared to her next-best alternative — i.e. whether economic profit is positive.

Ref: Ch.1 — Opportunity Cost; Q1 definitions — Explicit/Implicit Cost

c) Marginal Cost is lowest at Q = 3 (MC = 40). It falls from Q=1 to Q=3 (60→50→40) and then rises from Q=4 onward (50→70).
