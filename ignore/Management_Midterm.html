MGT281 — Chapter 1

Introduction to Business

Exam-Ready Notes — Likely Questions & Answers

Q1. What is Business? How is a Business Described?
Answer:

Business is an economic activity that involves the exchange, purchase, sale, or production of goods and services with a motive to earn profits and satisfy the needs of customers.

Each business can be defined or described by:

  • Its type of ownership (sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation).
  • The goods produced or services offered.
  • The types of jobs it provides.
  • The functions it performs in a community.
Q2. What do Businesses do? Explain the Input–Output Cycle.
Answer:

Businesses are started by entrepreneurs who see a need, recognize the opportunity, and go into business to meet that need. Business and people need each other — there are many stakeholders, and society depends on businesses for products and services, but also for employment.

Every business, at its core:

  • Buys inputs – raw materials, labour, machinery and equipment, and land.
  • Produces outputs – goods and services.
  • Focuses on efficient use of resources.
  • Generates profit / surplus.
Q3. Who are the Stakeholders of a Business? Explain with a Case Example.CASE-BASED
Answer:

A stakeholder is anyone who has an interest in the success of a business. Stakeholders are usually grouped into internal stakeholders (part of the organisation) and external stakeholders (outside the organisation, but still affected by or able to affect it).

TypeStakeholderMain Interest / Expectation
InternalOwnersReturn on investment, profit, growth in value of the business
ManagersSmooth operations, achieving targets, career growth, authority to make decisions
EmployeesFair wages, job security, safe working conditions, career development
ExternalCustomersFair price, good quality, reliable products/services
SuppliersTimely and full payment, long-term/repeat orders
GovernmentTax revenue, legal compliance, job creation
Local Community / EnvironmentEmployment opportunities, minimal pollution, community support
Creditors (Banks/Lenders)Timely repayment of loans with interest, low credit risk
Internal stakeholders (blue) sit inside the firm; external stakeholders (orange) sit outside but still hold a stake in it.
Case: Green Leaf Foods Ltd. — opening a new packaged-food factory near a residential area in Savar

Green Leaf Foods Ltd. is planning to build a new snack-food factory. Applying stakeholder analysis:

  • Owners want the factory to be profitable and increase the value of their shares.
  • Managers want the factory to run efficiently and hit production targets set by the owners.
  • Employees want safe working conditions and fair pay for the new factory jobs.
  • Customers want the new product line to stay affordable and maintain food-safety quality.
  • Suppliers (of flour, packaging, etc.) want a long-term, reliable buying contract from the new factory.
  • Government wants the factory to pay taxes, follow labour and environmental law, and create local jobs.
  • Local Community wants job opportunities but is also concerned about noise, traffic and pollution from the factory.
  • Creditors (the bank financing the factory) want assurance that loan instalments will be repaid on schedule.

Conflict of interest: Owners/Managers may want to cut costs (e.g. lower wages, cheaper waste disposal) to raise profit, while Employees want higher wages and the Community wants stricter pollution control — this is a classic example of stakeholder conflict that a manager must balance.

Q4. What is meant by "Industry"? Give an Example.
Answer:

A group of businesses that all produce similar products is called an industry.

Example: Automobile manufacturers together with automobile-parts producers form the Automobile Industry.

Q6. Who Forms the Core of Business? Explain Owners, Managers, Employees and Consumers.
Answer:

The human element is the core of business. Business needs people as owners, managers, employees and consumers, while people need business for the production of goods/services and the creation of jobs.

  • Owners: People who own a business, or invest money in one, expect to earn a profit. Large corporations may have huge numbers of owners – e.g., General Motors has over 1.2 million shareholders (owners) and 820,000 employees.
  • Managers: The person operating the business may be the owner (an owner-manager, also called an entrepreneur) or a professional manager employed by the owner. Both seek profit, growth, survival, and social responsibility.
  • Employees: Supply the skills and abilities needed to provide a product or service and to earn a profit; expect an equitable wage or salary.
  • Consumers: Purchase a good or service for personal or organisational use; want a fair price and reliable goods/services.
Q7. What are the Objectives of a Business? Explain Profit: Two Views.
Answer:

Business objectives generally include profit, survival, growth, and social responsibility.

  • Survival – the most basic objective; other objectives can only be met if the business survives.
  • Growth – because a business never stands still; measured through increased market share, individual development, and increased productivity.
  • Social Responsibility – accepting responsibility in areas such as pollution control, eliminating discriminatory practices, and energy conservation.
  • Profit – the major driving objective, viewed in two ways:
ViewMeaning
Business ProfitRevenue minus all costs, including taxes.
Economic ProfitRevenue minus both actual expenses and opportunity cost (the value of the next-best alternative use of resources).
Q8. What are the Different Types of Economic Systems?
Answer:

An economic system is an accepted way of organising production, establishing rights and freedom of ownership, using productive resources, and governing business transactions in a society.

SystemExplanationExample
Planned / Command EconomyGovernment owns most factors of production and regulates their allocation; personal property is privately owned but housing and means of production are government-owned.North Korea
SocialismPublic ownership & operation of key industries (e.g., gas, electricity) combined with private ownership of less-vital industries.Cuba
Free-Market EconomyDecisions on what to produce and in what quantities are decided by buyers and sellers in the market (decision-making focused).USA, Japan, UK
CapitalismPrivate ownership of capital, competition among businesses for profit; consumers have freedom of choice (ownership focused).USA, New Zealand
Mixed EconomyBoth government and private business produce/distribute goods and services; markets are generally free and competitive.Bangladesh (BPDB, DESCO government; Summit Power private)

Most capitalist economies are also free-market economies. The most appropriate economic system for a nation depends on cultural factors and the availability of economic resources.

Q9. Define Inflation, Deflation, and Recession.
Answer:
TermMeaning

DeflationAn economic condition in which prices fall steadily throughout the economy – i.e., the increase of purchasing power over time.

MGT281 — Chapter 2

Forms of Business Ownership & Business Combinations

Exam-Ready Notes — Likely Questions & Answers

Q1. What are the Basic Forms of Business Ownership?
Answer:

One of the major decisions an entrepreneur must make is which legal form of business ownership to use in creating a business venture. There are three basic forms:

  1. Sole Proprietorship
  2. Partnership
  3. Corporation

There is no single "best" form – the type and scope of operations, the nature of the product, and the goals of the entrepreneur influence the decision.

Q2. What is a Sole Proprietorship? Explain its Advantages and Disadvantages.
Answer:

A sole proprietorship is an unincorporated business owned and managed by one person. As the business and owner are not legally separate, it is the simplest form of business structure (also called individual entrepreneurship or sole trading). Sole proprietors report income/expenses on their personal tax returns.

Advantages
  • Ease of formation – minimal legal requirements
  • Retains all profit
  • Freedom in decision making
  • Ease of dissolution
  • Direct relations with customers
  • Creates employment facilities
Disadvantages
  • Unlimited liability (personal assets at risk)
  • Limited funds for expansion
  • Small-scale economies (no bulk-buying power)
  • Lack of broad business/management skills
  • Limited life (dissolves if owner dies/is disabled)
Q3. What is a Partnership? Explain its Types and Advantages/Disadvantages.
Answer:

A partnership is a form of business where two or more people share ownership, management responsibility, and the income or losses generated. Partnerships pool talent and financial resources. The partnership deed (partnership agreement) is the written legal document setting out how profits and losses will be shared.

TypeExplanation
General Partnership (GP)All partners have unlimited liability – each can be held responsible for the other partner's business debts.
Limited Partnership (LP)Partners have limited liability – only responsible up to their own share.
Joint VentureA special type of partnership formed to carry out a specific project or to operate for a specific time period.
Advantages
  • Ease of formation
  • Pooling of knowledge & skills
  • Risk sharing
  • More funds available
  • Shared responsibility
  • Tax advantage (profit taxed as personal income)
Disadvantages
  • Unlimited liability (General Partnership)
  • Loss of autonomy – shared control
  • Limited life – ends when a partner changes
  • Potential conflict between partners
  • Difficulty dissolving the business
Q4. What is a Corporation? Explain its Advantages and Disadvantages.
Answer:

A corporation is a large business entity, recognised in law as a single entity, owned by its shareholders, who elect a board of directors to oversee the organisation's activities and hire professional managers.

Advantages
  • Limited liability (equal to funds invested)
  • Skilled, professional management team
  • Easy transfer of ownership (sale of shares)
  • Greater capital base (can attract many investors)
  • Perpetual life
  • Legal-entity status (can sue/be sued, own property)
Disadvantages
  • Difficult & expensive to start (state charter)
  • Lack of control for individual shareholders
  • Multiple taxation (corporate tax + dividend tax)
  • Government involvement / regulation
  • Lack of secrecy (annual reports)
  • Credit limitations (creditors rely only on business assets)

Q2. Why do Firms Conduct International Business?
Answer:
  • Surplus of Goods: If a country has a surplus of a product, it can sell it to other nations and use the earnings to buy goods it lacks.
  • Scarcity of Resources: Not every country has every resource or the capacity to produce all it needs – this scarcity encourages trade between nations.
  • Specialization: Countries often specialize in goods they can produce more efficiently (absolute advantage). Example: Saudi Arabia specializes in crude oil.
Q3. Why should a Country Balance between Exports and Imports?MAIN
Answer:

A country's trade balance is the difference between the value of its exports and imports. Keeping the two roughly in balance matters for several reasons:

  • Protects foreign currency reserves: Imports must be paid for in foreign currency. If imports far exceed exports for a long period, the country drains its foreign-exchange reserves and struggles to pay for essential imports (fuel, machinery, medicine).
  • Prevents currency depreciation: Persistent trade deficits increase demand for foreign currency, which can weaken the local currency and make future imports even more expensive.
  • Avoids over-reliance on foreign markets: Exporting too much of a limited resource (or too narrow a range of products) without importing enough in return can leave a country short of goods its own people need, and vulnerable to global price shocks in that one product.
  • Supports both local and export-facing industries: A balance allows a country to use export earnings to fund productive imports (technology, raw materials) instead of consumption imports alone, supporting long-run growth.
  • Reduces external debt: Persistent deficits often have to be financed by foreign borrowing, increasing a nation's external debt burden.
Example: A country that imports large volumes of fuel and machinery needs strong export earnings (e.g. from garments, agriculture, or remittances) to pay for them; if imports consistently outpace exports, its foreign reserves fall and its currency comes under pressure.

Q5. Explain the Types of Tariffs.
Answer:

A tariff is a duty or tax charged against goods brought into (or, less commonly, sent out of) a country.

ConceptExplanation
Import TariffA duty/tax charged on goods brought into a country. Benefits domestic producers, raises import prices for consumers.
Export TariffA tax on goods leaving the country. Pros: revenue generation, moderates excessive exports. Cons: reduces exporters' competitiveness, can create trade tensions. An export ban is the most extreme version.
Q6. Explain Quotas, Tariff-Rate Quotas, and Embargoes with an Example.MAIN
Answer:
ToolExplanation
QuotaA limit on the amount of a product that can leave or enter a country (a direct quantity restriction, often enforced through licenses).
Tariff-Rate Quota (TRQ)A hybrid of a quota and a tariff. A lower ("in-quota") tariff rate applies to imports within the quota; a higher ("out-of-quota") rate applies to imports beyond it.
EmbargoA total ban on certain imports and/or exports, often imposed for political reasons.
VER (Voluntary Export Restraint)The exporting country voluntarily agrees to limit its exports (often under pressure from the importing country), which can create a quota rent – extra profit captured by license-holders/exporters rather than tariff revenue for the importing government.
Worked Example – Tariff-Rate Quota on Rice Imports (South Korea): An ad valorem tariff rate of 10% is levied on the first 1 million tons of rice imports; after that, an out-of-quota rate of 80% applies. So South Korea might import 2 million tons in total – 1 million tons at the 10% rate and another 1 million tons at the 80% rate.
Hypothetical tariff-rate quota: 10% tariff up to 1 million tons of rice, 80% tariff beyond it.
Q7. How Effective are Trade Tools (Tariffs vs. Subsidies) in Protecting Local Business?MAIN
Answer:

Governments use both tariffs and subsidies to help local businesses compete against foreign imports, but the two tools work differently and involve different trade-offs.

Trade ToolHow it Helps Local BusinessMain Drawback
TariffRaises the price of imports, making local goods relatively cheaper – benefits domestic producers directly.Consumers pay higher prices; can trigger retaliation/trade tension.
SubsidyLowers domestic producers' production costs, helping them compete against imports at home and gain export markets abroad.Costly to government budget; may distort the market or invite trade disputes.
QuotaDirectly limits the quantity of competing imports allowed in.Can create shortages/higher prices; less flexible than a tariff.
EmbargoFully blocks competing imports/exports.Most extreme – often politically motivated, disrupts trade relationships entirely.
Local Content RequirementForces a minimum share of local materials/labour into production.Raises costs, may reduce product quality (see Q8).

In practice, tariffs are the most direct but most consumer-costly tool; subsidies support competitiveness without directly taxing consumers but strain government budgets; quotas/embargoes are blunter, quantity-based restrictions used when governments want certainty over volume rather than price.

Q8. What is a Local Content Requirement? Explain with Pros and Cons.MAIN
Answer:

A local content requirement is a rule requiring a company to use a minimum percentage of local materials, labour, or components in its production or assembly – for example, a government may require that at least 60% of a product's content be locally sourced before the finished good qualifies for lower tariffs, subsidies, or unrestricted market access.

Pros
  • Boosts local manufacturing
  • Protects local jobs
  • Supports domestic industry
Cons
  • Increases consumer costs
  • Limits foreign competition
  • May reduce product quality
Example: A country may require automakers to source 60% of a vehicle's components locally before it can be sold duty-free domestically – encouraging the growth of a local auto-parts industry, though this can raise the final price and slow adoption of the newest imported technology.
Q9. What is Dumping? Explain the Strategy with Pros and Cons.MAIN
Answer:

Dumping is when a company sells a product in a foreign market at a price lower than its production cost or its home-market price – often to unload excess production or drive out competitors in the target market.

Pros (for the dumping firm)
  • Unloads excess production
  • Drives out competitors
  • Can raise prices later once rivals exit
Cons (for the target market)
  • Unfair competition
  • Market distortion
  • Harm to local industries

To counter dumping, importing countries commonly impose anti-dumping duties – a special type of tariff designed to raise the dumped product's price back up to a fair market level and protect local producers.
