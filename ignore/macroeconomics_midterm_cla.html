Lecture 1 The Economy as a Whole

What is Economics?

Economics

The study of how society uses scarce resources to produce goods and services and distribute them among people.

Scarcity - resources are limited but wants are unlimited. This is the central problem all economies must solve.

A Brief History

YearEconomistContribution
1776 Adam Smith The Wealth of Nations - free markets, competition, and self-interest can drive prosperity with limited government intervention.
1936 J. M. Keynes The General Theory - after the Great Depression, argued that government spending and policy are needed to manage downturns.

Branches of Economics

Microeconomics

How firms and individuals make decisions, and how decision-makers interact. Prices are determined by supply and demand; incentives shape behavior.

Example: A rise in coffee bean prices changes how much shops buy and how much customers pay per cup.

Macroeconomics

Examines the economy as a whole - aggregate outcomes such as inflation, unemployment, and growth. Studies how governments and central banks influence national income and business cycles.

Example: Increased government spending during a recession can boost overall activity and reduce unemployment.

Macro outcomes result from millions of micro-level decisions. In turn, macro policies (taxation, interest rates) shape micro-level behavior - the two are closely interconnected.

Relationship Among Macroeconomic Variables

Macroeconomic variables are closely interrelated - a change in one often ripples through many others.

Growth Channel
Consumption + Investment rises → Aggregate Demand rises → GDP rises → Employment rises
Inflation Channel
Money Supply / Aggregate Demand rises → Inflation rises → Central Bank raises Interest Rates → Borrowing and Growth fall → Inflation eases
Unemployment - Okun's Law
Output rises → Unemployment falls

Fiscal and monetary policy also shape exchange rates, capital flows, and the balance of payments - the system operates together, not in isolation.

Macroeconomic Objectives

The key goals that governments and policymakers pursue:

  • Economic growth - increase in Real GDP
  • Full employment / reducing unemployment
  • Reducing inflation / stable price level
  • Reducing inequality of income
  • Foreign exchange management
  • Debt management
  • Sustainable development

Why Study Macroeconomics?

  • Understanding National Income - measures how rich or poor a country is using indicators like GDP, GNP, and NNP.
  • Solving Unemployment - explains why unemployment exists and how government policy can reduce it.
  • Controlling Inflation - a continuous rise in general prices; macroeconomics suggests monetary and fiscal policy responses.

Stock vs Flow Variables

Stock Variable

Measured at a particular point in time - no time period is involved. Like the water level in a bathtub at a given moment.

Examples: Wealth on 31 March, Population on 1 January, Money supply today.

A stock is a snapshot - like a photo taken at a single instant.

Flow Variable

Measured over a period of time - a time period is always involved (e.g. per year or per month). Like water flowing into the bathtub.

Examples: National income per year, Salary per month, GDP per year.

A flow is like a video - recorded over a duration.

Key distinction: Income is a flow; wealth is a stock.

Short Run vs Long Run

In Microeconomics

Short Run (Micro)

A period in which at least one factor of production (FOP) is fixed. The firm cannot change it immediately.

Shoe factory example: Machines are the fixed FOP; labour is the variable FOP.

Capital (machines) = fixed FOP  |  Labour = variable FOP

Long Run (Micro)

A period in which all factors of production are variable. The firm can change factory size, buy machines, enter or exit the market.

The shoe factory can: build a new plant, buy new technology, expand production.

In the long run, there are no fixed factors of production.

In Macroeconomics

Short Run (Macro)

A period in which prices are sticky - they do not adjust quickly due to contracts, wages, or regulations.

Example: Workers sign a 1-year salary contract. If the economy slows, wages cannot immediately decrease.

Key features: Output fluctuates, unemployment changes, demand affects production, business cycles occur.

Long Run (Macro)

Prices are fully flexible. Wages and prices adjust, and the economy returns to its natural level of output.

Output depends on real factors - technology, capital, labour, institutions - rather than the money supply.

Lecture 2 Measuring GDP

What is GDP?

Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

The market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period of time.

Word / PhraseMeaning
Market Value Market prices reflect what people are willing to pay. If an apple costs twice as much as an orange, it contributes twice as much to GDP.
of All All items produced and sold legally in markets. GDP excludes illegal goods (e.g. illicit drugs) and items produced and consumed at home that never enter the marketplace.
Final Only final goods count - intermediate goods are already included in the final good's price. Exception: a firm's inventory held for later sale counts as final.
Goods & Services Includes tangible goods (food, clothing, cars) and intangible services (haircuts, housecleaning, doctor visits).
Produced Only currently produced goods and services count - transactions of items produced in the past are excluded.
Within a Country Measures the value of production within the geographic confines of a country.
In a Given Period Measures the value of production within a specific interval of time (usually a year or a quarter).

Why "Domestic" and Why "Gross"?

Domestic

Production within a country's borders. Contrasts with "national product" - the value of goods and services produced anywhere in the world by the residents of a nation.

Gross

Before subtracting the depreciation of capital. The opposite term, "net," means after subtracting the depreciation of capital.

Components of GDP

GDP (denoted Y) is divided into four components:

Y = C + I + G + NX

This is an identity - it must be true because of how the variables are defined.

SymbolComponentDefinition
C Consumption Spending by households on goods and services, except purchases of new housing.
I Investment Purchase of capital goods used in the future to produce more goods and services. Includes new housing construction.
G Government Purchases Spending on goods and services by local, state, and federal governments. Excludes transfer payments (e.g. social security, welfare).
NX Net Exports Exports minus imports. Spending on domestically produced goods by foreigners, minus spending on foreign goods by domestic residents.

What Does GDP Exclude?

  • Intermediate goods - already counted in the final good's price.
  • Used goods - counted when first produced; resale does not count again.
  • Financial asset purchases - stocks and bonds are not current production.
  • Transfer payments - government payments with no current production (e.g. pensions, benefits).
  • Home-produced goods - no market transaction occurs.
  • Illegal / unreported activities - not measured.

Also excluded: leisure time, happiness, environmental quality, and income distribution.

Nominal vs Real GDP

Nominal GDP

Production valued at current prices. Does not account for inflation. Rises when either prices or quantities increase.

Real GDP

Production valued at constant base-year prices. Adjusts for inflation. Changes only when actual quantities (output) change - a better measure of economic growth.

Nominal GDP does not distinguish whether an increase is due to higher production, higher prices, or both. This is why economists use Real GDP to measure changes in actual output over time.

Calculating Nominal GDP

Nominal GDP = Σ(Current Year Price × Current Year Quantity) = (P₁×Q₁) + (P₂×Q₂) + ... + (Pₙ×Qₙ)
Worked Example

An economy produces only Rice and Mobile Phones:

GoodYear 1 PriceYear 1 QtyYear 2 PriceYear 2 Qty
RiceTk. 501,000 kgTk. 601,100 kg
Mobile PhonesTk. 20,000100 unitsTk. 22,000120 units

Step 1 - Nominal GDP for Year 1

= (50 × 1,000) + (20,000 × 100) = 50,000 + 2,000,000 = Tk. 2,050,000

Step 2 - Nominal GDP for Year 2

= (60 × 1,100) + (22,000 × 120) = 66,000 + 2,640,000 = Tk. 2,706,000

Interpretation: Nominal GDP rose from Tk. 2,050,000 to Tk. 2,706,000 because both prices and quantities increased. Nominal GDP cannot tell us how much of this rise came from more production vs higher prices.

Calculating Real GDP

To isolate real growth, we use base-year prices for every year. This "freezes" prices so we can observe changes in production volume only.

Real GDP = Σ(Base Year Price × Current Year Quantity)
Worked Example - Year 1 as base year

Real GDP for Year 1 (base year)

= (50 × 1,000) + (20,000 × 100) = Tk. 2,050,000 (equals Nominal GDP in the base year - always)

Real GDP for Year 2 (Year 1 prices × Year 2 quantities)

= (50 × 1,100) + (20,000 × 120) = 55,000 + 2,400,000 = Tk. 2,455,000

Interpretation:

  • In the base year, Nominal GDP = Real GDP (always true).
  • In Year 2, Nominal GDP (Tk. 2,706,000) > Real GDP (Tk. 2,455,000) because prices rose.
  • Real GDP only captures changes in output; Nominal GDP captures both price and quantity changes.

GDP Deflator

GDP Deflator

A price index measuring the change in prices of all final goods and services produced within a country. Used to gauge overall inflation. It is the ratio of Nominal GDP to Real GDP, multiplied by 100.

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100
Worked Example
YearNominal GDP (Tk.)Real GDP (Tk.)GDP Deflator
Year 1 (base)2,050,0002,050,000100
Year 22,706,0002,455,000110.22

GDP Deflator for Year 2

= (2,706,000 / 2,455,000) × 100 = 1.1022 × 100 = 110.22

Interpretation:

  • Year 1 is the base year, so the GDP Deflator = 100.
  • The deflator of 110.22 means the overall price level is 10.22% higher than in the base year.
  • Unlike the CPI (which tracks a fixed basket of consumer goods), the GDP Deflator covers all domestically produced final goods and services.

Calculating the Inflation Rate

Use the GDP Deflator to find the inflation rate between two years:

Inflation Rate = [(Deflatorₓ - Deflatorₓ₋₁) / Deflatorₓ₋₁] × 100
Worked Example
= [(110.22 - 100) / 100] × 100 = (10.22 / 100) × 100 = 10.22%

The overall price level of domestically produced final goods and services increased by 10.22% from Year 1 to Year 2, as measured by the GDP Deflator.

Expenditure Approach

Measures GDP as the sum of all spending in the economy:

GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)
SymbolMeaning
CConsumption expenditure by households
IInvestment expenditure by firms
GGovernment expenditure on goods and services
XExports
MImports
X - MNet exports
Worked Example

Given: C = 1,500,000  |  I = 600,000  |  G = 400,000  |  X = 350,000  |  M = 150,000

Step 1 - Net Exports

X - M = 350,000 - 150,000 = 200,000

Step 2 - Apply GDP Formula

GDP = 1,500,000 + 600,000 + 400,000 + 200,000 GDP = Tk. 2,700,000

Households spent Tk. 1.5 million; firms invested Tk. 0.6 million; government spent Tk. 0.4 million; exports exceeded imports by Tk. 0.2 million. Total GDP = Tk. 2.7 million.

Income Approach

GDP = Total Income Earned by Factors of Production

When firms produce goods and services, all revenue is paid out as income to the factors of production.

GDP = Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit

GDP = Factor Income + Indirect Taxes + Depreciation

  • Depreciation - consumption (wear and tear) of fixed capital.
