What is management?

Management = getting work done through people and resources to achieve organizational goals.

Efficiency: "doing things right"

Minimum resources, time, cost. Ex: finish a project without wasting money.

Effectiveness: "doing the right things"

Achieving the correct goal. Ex: the project solves the customer's problem.

Good management needs both. Cafe: serving coffee fast = efficient, but effective only if customers are satisfied and sales targets are met.

Learning outcomes: define management; explain the process (plan, organize, lead, control); differentiate manager levels; apply planning types, decision steps and control types; identify skills and Mintzberg's ten roles.

Management process and levels

Inputs (human, financial, physical, information resources) → Planning and decision making → Organizing → Leading → Controlling → Outputs (goals attained, effectiveness, efficiency).

It is a continuous cycle: set direction, build structure, motivate people, check results.

Top level

Sets mission, vision, long-term strategy. CEO, Managing Director, Vice Chancellor.

Middle level

Translates strategy into departmental plans. HR manager, Marketing manager, Dean.

Lower level

Supervises daily work and frontline staff. Shift supervisor, team leader.

University program: club president sets the event goal, coordinators plan teams, team leaders supervise volunteers.

Planning and decision making

Planning = setting goals and deciding the best way to achieve them. Ask: What goal? Why important? Who does each task? When does each step happen? Where? How are resources used?

Student business fair: goal Tk 50,000; plan = select products, assign teams, fix date, arrange venue, set budget and promotion method.

Types of planning (Unilever example)

Strategic / long-term

Top managers, several years. Overall direction.

Grow sustainable personal-care products in South Asia.

Tactical

Middle managers, months to 1 year. Strategy into department plans.

Marketing plans a campaign, sales sets retail targets, supply chain plans production and distribution.

Operational

Lower managers, daily/weekly/monthly. Routine tasks.

Supervisors prepare shift schedules, check stock delivery, monitor shelf display, review daily sales.

Strategic = where to go. Tactical = how departments support it. Operational = what workers do today.

Decision-making cycle (7 steps)

Logical flow prevents guessing.

  1. Identify the problem - recognize and define it clearly.
  2. Gather information - facts and data.
  3. Identify alternatives - possible solutions.
  4. Evaluate alternatives - feasibility, cost, risk.
  5. Select the best alternative.
  6. Implement the decision.
  7. Monitor and evaluate - review results, adjust.

Unilever case: sales of a personal-care product fall in urban supermarkets.

  1. Define it: which product, city, customers, how large the decline.
  2. Sales reports, competitor prices, customer reviews, shelf position, distributor stock, advertising reach.
  3. Short promotion, smaller pack, better shelf display, digital ads, product improvement.
  4. Compare by cost, speed, expected sales impact, brand-image risk, supply-chain capacity.
  5. Test a 4-week "small pack + shelf display + digital ad" pilot in selected stores before a national rollout.
  6. Assign budget and duties (marketing, sales, supply chain), inform distributors, train promoters, place stock on time.
  7. Track weekly sales, market share, customer response, stock-outs, campaign cost. If the pilot works, scale up; if not, adjust.
Do not jump straight to a solution. Support the decision with data, compare options, consider risk, check results.

Organizing

Organizing = deciding how to group activities and resources so the plan can be done.

Goal (what to achieve) → Tasks (what work) → Roles (who) → Authority (who decides) → Coordination (how teams work together).

Group presentation: divide research, slide design, speaking parts, deadlines.

A plan says "what we want"; organizing says "who does what and how".

Key organizing elements

Job design

Structuring roles. Cashier takes payment, barista makes coffee.

Departmentalization

Grouping similar tasks: marketing, finance, operations, HR.

Authority relationships

Hierarchy and communication lines. Team members report to team leader.

Span of control

Number of subordinates one manager supervises. 1 supervisor, 8 sales staff.

Line and staff roles

Line = core work; staff = support. Sales is line; HR/legal is staff.

Exam tip: definition + one example per element.

Leading

Leading = influencing, motivating and supporting people toward organizational goals.

Directive / Autocratic

Clear instructions, centralized control. Use when tasks are new, urgent, risky. Ex: lab safety procedure.

Supportive

Support and positive relationships. Use when work is stressful. Ex: tired service team.

Participative

Shared decisions, employee input. Use when ideas improve quality. Ex: ask staff how to improve customer service.

Achievement-oriented

High expectations, performance goals. Use when a skilled team needs challenge. Ex: sales target for a product launch.

No single style is always best. Match style to situation.

Controlling

Makes sure actual performance matches planned standards.

Steps: 1 Set standards → 2 Measure actual performance → 3 Compare actual with standards → 4 Take corrective action.

Feedforward (before)

Prevent problems. Train staff before service begins.

Concurrent (during)

Monitor in real time. Supervisor checks service quality in busy hours.

Feedback (after)

Learn from results. Review customer complaints after the event.

Before → During → After.

Administrative management: Fayol's 14 principles

Henri Fayol = father of administrative management. Management is a universal process of planning, organizing, leading, controlling. He gave 14 principles.

Slide order: Division of labor, Authority and responsibility, Unity of command, Unity of direction, Equity, Initiative, Order, Discipline, Fair remuneration, Stability of tenure, Scalar chain, Subordination of individual interest, Esprit de corps, Centralization and decentralization.

1. Division of labor

Specialization: work divided and allotted by expertise. Simpler work, efficiency, speed and accuracy.

Question on slide: it can also de-skill - repetitive narrow tasks, boredom, workers cannot do other jobs.

2. Authority and responsibility

Authority = right to give orders. Responsibility = obligation for the assigned job. Keep them balanced: more responsibility than authority frustrates the manager; more authority than responsibility frustrates employees. Authority can be delegated, responsibility cannot (the manager stays accountable).

3. Unity of command

One and only one boss per person. Avoid dual subordination, which causes confusion and conflict. Gives a disciplined, stable, orderly existence.

Designer gets conflicting tasks from marketing and sales managers; should report only to marketing manager.

4. Unity of direction

One head, one plan for activities with the same objective. All efforts toward a common goal.

R&D, marketing, production follow one product manager's plan for the launch.

5. Equity

Fairness + kindness + justice. Impartial, similar treatment for similar positions, no discrimination (age, caste, gender, religion, relation). Needed if devotion is expected.

A beats sales target by 20% gets 10% bonus; B meets target gets 5%.

6. Initiative

Workers start actions without being asked (voluntary participation). Management invites ideas and rewards them (financial or non-financial). Builds trust, creativity, innovation, future leaders.

7. Order

Right people, right time, right place, right materials. Material order: a safe, specific place for every item. Human order: most suitable person on the suitable job.

8. Discipline

Sincerity, obedience, respect for authority, following rules. Applies to subordinates and management for harmony.

9. Fair remuneration

Fair, reasonable, satisfactory, rewarding. Based on cost of living, work assigned, firm's finances, wage rate. Transparent performance evaluation.

10. Stability of tenure

Do not move staff frequently. People need time to settle; otherwise training time, effort and money are wasted. Stability builds team spirit, belonging, better quality and quantity.

11. Scalar chain

Clear, direct line of authority from highest to lowest level. Every order and message passes through it. Everyone knows their role.

12. Subordination of individual interest to the general interest

The organization is bigger than any individual; personal goals must not conflict with organizational objectives.

Worker takes frequent breaks and delays the assembly line; the firm's interest (smooth flow, deadlines) prevails.

13. Esprit de corps

Unity, mutual trust, team spirit; open communication avoids misunderstanding. Inspires harder work. Achieved through unity of command.

14. Centralization and decentralization

Centralization = decision authority kept at the top. Decentralization = authority spread to all levels (delegation). Depends on business size, superiors' experience, subordinates' dependability and ability. Fayol: absolute either way is not feasible; strike a balance.

Slide task: which principles are still relevant? Think of each one's limitations.

Bureaucratic management (Max Weber)

  • Focus: the ideal form of organization.
  • Weber found early organizations inefficient: decisions based on personal relationships and loyalty.
  • Bureaucracy = division of labor, hierarchy, formal rules, responsibility, selection and promotion by ability.
  • Authority comes from the position in the hierarchy, not tradition or charisma.

Motivation basics

Motivation = direction (which task), intensity (how much effort), persistence (how long) of effort toward a goal.

  • High motivation cannot replace missing ability or resources.
  • Performance = motivation + ability + role clarity + work situation.
  • Managers influence it via job design, rewards, fairness, feedback, meaningful goals.
Key question: what makes the desired effort worthwhile and achievable for this employee?

Motivation cycle: 1 Need/gap → 2 Search for ways → 3 Choice and effort → 4 Outcome → 5 Evaluation (satisfaction or frustration shapes the next cycle). Feedback changes future expectations; the cycle may repeat, change direction or stop.

An unmet lower need tends to demand attention before a higher one dominates. Application: diagnose the employee's priority; the same reward does not motivate everyone.

Limits: needs rarely follow a fixed staircase. People pursue several at once; culture and life stage matter; people may sacrifice security for purpose or family; satisfied needs can return. Good for diagnosis, not a universal sequence.

ERG theory

Existence

Material and safety needs: pay, benefits, physical safety, job security.

Relatedness

Relationships and social acceptance: trust, belonging, respect, communication.

Growth

Development and meaningful achievement: learning, autonomy, mastery, contribution.

Frustration-regression: if growth is blocked, a person focuses more on relatedness or existence rewards. ERG lets several needs work together and allows movement in more than one direction (unlike Maslow).

Case, Rafi: fair salary and good colleagues, wants promotion and development, but told no promotions for 3 years. Growth is frustrated, so he shifts to existence needs (higher salary, bonuses, allowances, benefits).

Herzberg's two-factor theory

Hygiene factors: prevent dissatisfaction

Pay and company policies; supervision and working conditions; security and relationships.

Motivators: create satisfaction

Achievement and recognition; responsibility and advancement; interesting work and growth.

Fixing poor conditions removes complaints, but meaningful work creates genuine satisfaction.

Case: pay raised, yet work is still repetitive and invisible. Step 1 remove dissatisfaction: competitive pay and clear policies, fix unsafe conditions or inconsistent supervision, explain decisions on schedules and security. Step 2 enrich work: ownership of a whole piece of work, direct feedback from task or customer, meaningful responsibility and learning. Diagnosis: the raise fixed hygiene, but the job lacks motivators.

Expectancy theory

Motivation is strongest when all three beliefs are strong.

Expectancy

If I exert effort, can I perform? (ability, resources, clear expectations)

Instrumentality

If I perform, will I get the outcome? (reliable measurement, credible rewards)

Valence

Do I value that outcome? (personal preferences, current needs)

Motivational force = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence. A near-zero belief weakens the whole chain.

Equity theory

Employees compare their outcome-to-input ratio with a relevant other person or group.

Inputs

Effort and time; skill and experience; responsibility and flexibility.

Outcomes

Pay and benefits; recognition and status; development opportunities.

Comparison other

A coworker; a previous job; an employee in another organization.

Perceived equity = ratios reasonably comparable, not necessarily identical. Fairness depends on the comparison chosen and how the employee interprets contributions and rewards.

Responses to perceived inequity

  1. Change inputs: reduce effort, hours, extra help.
  2. Seek different outcomes: ask for pay, recognition, flexibility.
  3. Change the comparison: another coworker or the market.
  4. Reinterpret the situation: reassess value of inputs or outcomes.
  5. Leave: transfer, withdraw psychologically, resign.

Managers should check distributive fairness, decision procedures and respectful explanation.

A practical motivation checklist

  1. What need or outcome matters to this employee now? Maslow/ERG
  2. Can the employee see how effort leads to performance? Expectancy
  3. Will performance reliably lead to a valued reward? Instrumentality, valence
  4. Does the exchange feel fair compared with relevant others? Equity
  5. Does the job give meaning, autonomy, useful feedback? JCM, Herzberg
  6. Which behaviors receive attention and reinforcement? Reinforcement
Strong motivation systems align employee needs, believable performance links, fair treatment, meaningful jobs and consistent consequences.

Lecture 4: International business

International business = business activities performed across national boundaries. Every country takes part in some form of international trade; both large (Coca-Cola, IBM) and small firms sell globally.

Foreign firms investing in Bangladesh

Unilever Bangladesh Ltd. (British-Dutch multinational Unilever). Products: Lux, Sunsilk, Surf Excel.

Bangladeshi firms going international

BEXIMCO Group exports pharmaceuticals, textiles, ceramics to Europe, USA, Asia.

Why firms trade internationally

  • Surplus of goods: sell the surplus abroad and use the earnings to buy goods you lack. Slide question: only export, never import? No, imports supply what is missing.
  • Scarcity of resources: no country has every resource or capacity, so nations trade.
  • Specialization: countries produce what they make more efficiently (absolute advantage). Ex: Saudi Arabia - crude oil.

extra Slide task "why balance exports and imports?": constant import excess drains foreign currency and builds debt; constant export excess invites retaliation and leaves needs unmet.

Barriers to international business

1. Cultural and social

Differences in culture (values, concepts, tangible items) and social forces (family, education, religion, customs) hurt product acceptance and cause costly mistakes.

Fast-food chain in Bangladesh needs halal options, local flavors, suitable prices.

2. Political

Risks from a country's political climate and stability. Governments may suddenly change policy toward foreign firms, creating uncertainty.

3. Tariffs and trade restrictions

Import tariffs, quotas, embargoes, local content rules, dumping and anti-dumping (below).

Tariffs and quotas

Import tariff = duty (tax) charged on goods brought into a country.

Specific tariffAd valorem tariff
ChargeFixed per unit (e.g. $10 per unit, whatever cost or value)Percentage of value (higher value pays more)

  • Embargo: total ban on certain imports and exports, often political.

Tariff rate quota

A hybrid of quota and tariff: a lower tariff inside the quota, a higher one above it. South Korea, rice: 10% ad valorem on the first 1 million tons, 80% beyond. Importing 2 million tons = 1 million at 10% + 1 million at 80%. Common in agriculture; goal is to limit imports over the quota.

Other trade tools

Export tariffs

Pros: revenue generation, export moderation.
Cons: reduced competitiveness, trade tensions.

Slide also asks: "Export ban?"

Local content requirement

Pros: boosts local manufacturing, protects local jobs, supports domestic industry.
Cons: raises consumer costs, limits foreign competition, may reduce quality.

Dumping strategy

Pros (for the dumper): unloads excess production, drives out competitors, raises prices later.
Cons: unfair competition, market distortion, harm to local industries.

extra Dumping = selling abroad below home price or cost; anti-dumping duties counter it.

VER (Voluntary Export Restraints) and Quota rent appear as titles only. extra VER: exporter agrees to cap its own exports. Quota rent: extra profit earned by whoever holds import licenses because the quota keeps prices high.

Balancing trade policy impacts

Tariffs: benefit domestic producers, raise import prices. Subsidies: lower production costs, help firms compete against imports and gain export markets.

Sample questions with model answers

Each answer is written at about 5-mark length: definition, explanation, example, short analysis. Tap a question to open it.

1. Managerial functions

Management is the process of getting work done through people and resources to achieve organizational goals efficiently and effectively. This work is carried out through four connected functions: planning, organizing, leading and controlling.

Planning means setting goals and deciding the best way to reach them, by asking what, why, who, when, where and how. Organizing means grouping activities and resources into a structure: deciding tasks, roles, authority and coordination so that the plan can actually be carried out. Leading means influencing, motivating and supporting people so that they work willingly toward the goals. Controlling means setting standards, measuring actual performance, comparing it with the standards and taking corrective action.

Example: for a university cultural program, the manager plans the goal, budget, date and venue; organizes finance, decoration, promotion and logistics teams; leads by motivating volunteers and solving conflicts; and controls by checking attendance, cost and schedule and correcting problems.

Analysis: the functions are not a one-time sequence. They form a continuous cycle, because the results of controlling feed back into the next round of planning. Without planning there is no direction, without organizing no structure, without leading no effort, and without controlling no way to know whether goals were met.

2. Levels of management

Organizations divide managers into three levels based on the scope of their decisions: top-level, middle-level and lower-level (first-line) managers.

Top-level managers, such as the CEO, Managing Director or Vice Chancellor, set the mission, vision and long-term strategy of the whole organization. They spend most of their time on strategic planning and external relations, and need the most conceptual skill. Middle-level managers, such as an HR manager, marketing manager or dean, translate the strategy into departmental plans, coordinate between top and lower levels, and allocate resources to their departments. They need strong human and communication skills. Lower-level managers, such as shift supervisors and team leaders, supervise daily work and frontline employees, solve everyday problems and check quality. They need more technical and interpersonal skills.

Example: in a university program, the club president sets the event goal, coordinators plan teams, and team leaders supervise volunteers on the day.

Analysis: the levels work as a chain. Strategy becomes tactical plans in the middle and operational action at the bottom, so weakness at any level breaks the link between organizational goals and daily work.

3. Managerial skills

Managerial skills are the abilities a manager needs to perform well, because managerial work involves tasks, people, ideas and decisions. The main skills are technical, interpersonal (human) and conceptual, supported by diagnostic and decision skills and communication and time-management skills.

Technical skill is the ability to use job-specific knowledge, tools and methods, for example using accounting software or production equipment. Interpersonal skill is the ability to communicate, motivate and work with people, for example resolving a conflict in a team. Conceptual skill is the ability to see the organization as a whole and understand complex ideas, for example planning a new market that affects finance, HR and operations. Diagnostic and decision skill is identifying why a problem exists and choosing a fix, for example finding why sales are falling. Communication and time skill is conveying information clearly and prioritizing work, for example explaining deadlines and keeping schedules.

Analysis: the mix changes with the level. Top managers need the most conceptual skill, middle managers need strong human and communication skills, and lower managers need more technical skill. Interpersonal skill matters at every level because managers always work through people. In an exam, explain why a skill matters at the level you mention.

4-5. Henry Mintzberg's managerial roles (with situational questions)

Henry Mintzberg studied what managers actually do and concluded that they perform ten roles, grouped into three categories: interpersonal, informational and decisional. This shows that managers do not only plan; they represent, communicate, solve problems and negotiate every day.

Interpersonal roles deal with people: the figurehead performs ceremonial duties, the leader guides and motivates employees, and the liaison builds networks inside and outside the organization. Informational roles deal with information: the monitor collects information, the disseminator passes it to employees, and the spokesperson represents the organization to outsiders. Decisional roles deal with choices: the entrepreneur starts improvements, the disturbance handler solves unexpected problems, the resource allocator distributes money, people and time, and the negotiator bargains with others.

How to answer a situational question: (1) ask whether the manager is dealing with people, information or a decision; (2) name the specific role; (3) quote the evidence from the situation; (4) briefly explain why the role matters. Example: "A manager studies sales data, informs staff and reports results to stakeholders" uses the monitor, disseminator and spokesperson roles, all informational.

6. Controlling

Controlling is the managerial function that makes sure actual performance matches planned standards. It closes the management cycle by checking whether the goals set during planning are really being achieved, and by correcting problems when they are not.

The process has four steps. First, set standards, such as sales targets or quality levels. Second, measure actual performance through reports, observation or data. Third, compare actual performance with the standards to find any deviation. Fourth, take corrective action, which may mean fixing the process, retraining staff or, if the standard was unrealistic, revising the standard.

Control is also classified by timing. Feedforward control works before the work starts and prevents problems, for example training staff before service begins. Concurrent control works during the work by monitoring in real time, for example a supervisor checking service quality during busy hours. Feedback control works after the work ends and learns from results, for example reviewing customer complaints after an event.

Analysis: the three types are best used together (before, during, after). Feedforward is cheapest because it prevents errors, concurrent control fixes errors while they can still be fixed, and feedback improves future planning.

7. Decision-making cycle

Decision making is the process of choosing the best course of action from alternatives. A decision should follow a logical cycle from problem to follow-up, which prevents guessing and reduces risk.

The steps are: (1) identify the problem by recognizing and clearly defining the issue; (2) gather information, meaning relevant facts and data; (3) identify alternatives, generating possible solutions; (4) evaluate alternatives for feasibility, cost and risk; (5) select the best alternative; (6) implement the decision by putting the solution into action with resources and responsibilities; and (7) monitor and evaluate by reviewing results and adjusting if needed.

Example (Unilever): sales of a personal-care product are falling in urban supermarkets. The manager defines which product, city and customer group are affected, then gathers sales reports, competitor prices, customer reviews and shelf-position data. Alternatives include a short promotion, a smaller pack, a better shelf display, digital advertising or product improvement. These are compared on cost, speed, expected sales impact, brand-image risk and supply capacity. A four-week pilot of a small pack with a shelf display and a digital ad is chosen, budgets and duties are assigned, and weekly sales, market share and stock-outs are tracked. If it works it is scaled up; if not, it is adjusted.

Analysis: a manager should not jump directly to a solution. Decisions backed by data, compared options and a follow-up check are more likely to succeed.

8. Types of planning

Planning means setting goals and deciding how to achieve them. A business uses different levels of planning for the same broad goal: the higher level gives direction, and the lower level turns it into daily action. The three types are strategic, tactical and operational planning.

Strategic planning is done by top managers over several years. Its purpose is to decide the overall direction of the company, for example Unilever deciding to grow sustainable personal-care products in South Asian markets and build stronger brands over the next few years. Tactical planning is done by middle managers, usually over several months up to one year. Its purpose is to convert the strategy into departmental plans, for example the marketing team planning a campaign, sales setting retail targets, and the supply chain planning production and distribution. Operational planning is done by lower-level managers on a daily, weekly or monthly basis and covers routine tasks, for example supervisors preparing shift schedules, checking stock delivery, monitoring shelf displays and reviewing daily sales reports.

Analysis: remember it as strategic = where to go, tactical = how departments support it, operational = what workers do today. All three must support the same goal, otherwise day-to-day effort will not add up to the strategy.

Leadership types

Leading means influencing, motivating and supporting people so they work toward organizational goals. Leadership style is the way a leader uses authority and involves followers. The slides describe four styles, and no single one is always best; a good leader matches the style to the situation.

Directive (autocratic) leadership gives clear instructions and keeps control centralized. It suits new, urgent or risky tasks, for example a safety procedure in a laboratory. Supportive leadership concentrates on employee support and positive relationships, and suits stressful work, for example a tired service team. Participative leadership shares decisions and takes employee input, and suits situations where ideas improve quality, for example asking staff how to improve customer service. Achievement-oriented leadership sets high expectations and performance goals, and suits a skilled team that needs a challenge, for example a sales team given a target for a product launch.

Analysis: directive style is fast but may reduce motivation, participative style builds commitment but is slower, and supportive style helps morale but may not push performance. Effective leaders therefore diagnose the task, the team's skill and the pressure level before choosing a style. In the classic three-style view, autocratic means the leader decides, democratic means the group is involved, and laissez-faire means the leader is hands-off.

Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Abraham Maslow's theory is a content theory of motivation. It says people are motivated by unmet needs arranged in five levels, and that an unmet lower-level need tends to demand attention before a higher-level need becomes dominant.

From lowest to highest, the needs are: physiological (food, rest, basic comfort); safety (security, stability, protection); belongingness (acceptance, relationships, teamwork); esteem (recognition, competence, status); and self-actualization (growth, creativity, purpose). At work these translate to adequate pay, breaks and manageable hours; stable policies, safe conditions and job security; supportive teams and trusted supervision; recognition, responsibility and visible achievement; and challenging work with learning and creative contribution.

Managerial application: a manager should diagnose the employee's current priority instead of assuming the same reward motivates everyone. Offering recognition to someone worried about job security will not work.

Limitations: human needs rarely follow a fixed staircase. People may pursue several needs at once, culture and life stage change the importance of each need, a person may sacrifice security for purpose, achievement or family, and satisfied needs can become important again. The theory is therefore useful for conversation and diagnosis, but it does not predict one universal sequence.

ERG theory

ERG theory, developed by Clayton Alderfer, is a content theory that simplifies Maslow's five levels into three groups of needs: Existence, Relatedness and Growth.

Existence needs are material and safety needs, such as pay, benefits, physical safety and job security. Relatedness needs are relationships and social acceptance, such as trust, belonging, respect and communication. Growth needs are development and meaningful achievement, such as learning, autonomy, mastery and contribution.

ERG differs from Maslow in two ways. First, several needs can operate at the same time. Second, movement is possible in more than one direction. The key idea is frustration-regression: when the opportunities to satisfy a higher need remain blocked, the person focuses more strongly on a lower need, such as relatedness or existence rewards.

Example (Rafi): Rafi, a university employee, has a reasonable salary and good relationships with colleagues, but strongly desires promotion and professional development. The organization tells him there will be no promotion for three years. His growth need is frustrated, so he shifts his focus toward existence needs, such as seeking a higher salary, bonuses, allowances or better benefits. This move from a frustrated higher need to a lower one is frustration-regression.

Analysis: managers should not be surprised when a blocked growth opportunity leads to demands for more pay. Providing alternative development opportunities can prevent this shift.

Expectancy theory

Expectancy theory, associated with Victor Vroom, is a process theory of motivation. It explains how people choose how much effort to put in by judging whether effort will lead to performance, whether performance will lead to a reward, and whether the reward is worth having. Motivation is strongest when all three beliefs are strong.

Expectancy asks "If I exert effort, can I perform?" and depends on ability, resources and clear expectations. Instrumentality asks "If I perform, will I receive the outcome?" and depends on reliable measurement and credible rewards. Valence asks "Do I value that outcome?" and depends on personal preferences and current needs. The relationship is: Motivational force = Expectancy x Instrumentality x Valence.

Example: a salesperson is promised a bonus for hitting a target. If the target is impossible (low expectancy), if bonuses are often not paid (low instrumentality), or if the person would rather have time off than money (low valence), motivation will be weak.

Managerial application and analysis: because the factors are multiplied, a near-zero belief weakens the entire chain even when the other two are strong. Managers should therefore provide training and resources, set achievable targets, measure performance fairly, deliver promised rewards on time and find out what each employee actually values.

9. The 14 principles: identify them

Quick clues: specialized tasks = Division of labor; power vs obligation = Authority/responsibility; two bosses = Unity of command; one plan for related activities = Unity of direction; fair, kind, no discrimination = Equity; staff suggest ideas = Initiative; right person/material in right place = Order; rules obeyed by all = Discipline; fair pay = Remuneration; frequent transfers = Stability of tenure; chain of reporting = Scalar chain; personal vs company interest = Subordination; team spirit = Esprit de corps; approval from head office = Centralization.

Case 1: Maya's bakery (everyone did everything, then tasks were split)

Principle applied: Division of labor. Maya divided the work (mixing, baking, decorating, serving, payments) and assigned employees according to their skills, which is specialization.

How it improved performance: each employee repeated one kind of task and became faster and more accurate, so mistakes dropped, orders were completed sooner and customers waited less. Subdividing work also makes each job simpler and lets people use their particular expertise.

Possible disadvantage: after several months some employees could perform only one narrow task. Over-specialization de-skills workers: the job becomes repetitive and boring, motivation falls, and the bakery struggles when someone is absent because nobody else can cover the task. Maya could use job rotation and cross-training to keep the benefits while reducing the drawback.

Case 2: Imran, lab manager, blamed for delays but cannot approve repairs

Principle applied: Authority and responsibility.

Imbalance: Imran has full responsibility for keeping the computers operational, but he lacks the authority to approve repairs, purchase equipment or assign work to technicians. He is accountable for results without having the power to produce them.

Why responsibility is ineffective without authority: responsibility is the obligation to perform the job, and authority is the right to give orders and make decisions that make performance possible. When responsibility exceeds authority, the manager depends on others, gets frustrated, and delays occur while he waits for approvals, yet he still takes the blame.

What the university should do: give him authority matching his responsibility: approve repairs up to a set limit, purchase necessary equipment, and assign tasks to technicians. Larger spending can still need senior approval. Balancing the two removes the delay and makes accountability fair.

Case 3: Staff arrive late, the manager is also late and rarely enforces the rule, then suddenly punishes one employee

Principle applied: Discipline. Discipline means obedience, respect for authority and observing the rules of the enterprise, and it applies to management as well as employees.

How both weakened discipline: employees repeatedly broke the 9:00 a.m. rule, and the manager failed in his own role by arriving late himself and by not enforcing the rule consistently. Because the leader did not follow or enforce the rule, it lost credibility.

Was the punishment fair? No. Suddenly punishing one person for being ten minutes late, when lateness was tolerated and the manager does the same, is inconsistent and arbitrary. It damages trust and may feel like victimization.

How to enforce it: announce the rule clearly, apply it equally to everyone including managers, give a warning before penalties, and use a progressive system of consequences. The manager should set the example by arriving on time.

Case 4: Rafi receives instructions from both Nadia and Karim with the same deadline

Principle violated: Unity of command. A subordinate should receive orders from, and be accountable to, one and only one boss.

Problem Rafi faces: dual subordination. Nadia wants a social-media campaign and Karim wants a market-research report, and each calls their task the top priority by the same deadline, so he has conflicting instructions and cannot satisfy both.

Why performance may suffer: he faces confusion, stress and delays, may complete one task poorly or neither well, and nobody is clearly accountable for the outcome. The conflict can also spread between the two managers.

How to clarify the relationship: assign Rafi a single direct supervisor. If either manager needs his work, the request should go through that supervisor, or Nadia and Karim should agree priorities and deadlines between themselves before assigning tasks. Unity of command gives a disciplined, stable and orderly working environment.

Case 5: Hospital makes an accountant manage medical equipment and a biomedical engineer do administrative work

Principle applied: Order. Order means the right person, at the right time, in the right place, with the right materials.

Problems in human and material order: human order is broken because an experienced accountant is managing technical equipment while a trained biomedical engineer does routine administration, so neither person is in the most suitable job. Material order is broken because equipment is stored in rooms without labels, so there is no safe, specific place for each item.

Effect on hospital operations: staff will waste time searching for equipment, equipment may be misused, damaged or poorly maintained, patient treatment can be delayed, and safety risks increase. Skills are wasted and efficiency falls.

Changes needed: place the biomedical engineer in charge of the medical equipment and the accountant in finance or administration; label and organize all equipment in assigned, safe places; and keep a system to track its location and condition.

Case 6: Two sales employees achieve similar results and hold the same position, but the manager gives one a higher bonus because they are friends

Principle violated: Equity (fairness, kindness and justice). Employees in similar positions should receive similar treatment, and the manager must be impartial.

Has the manager violated it? Yes. The bonus was based on friendship and not on any performance difference, so it is favoritism.

Does equity always mean the same reward? No. Equity does not mean equal pay for everyone; it means fair treatment in proportion to contribution. For example, an employee who beats the sales target by 20% may receive a 10% bonus while one who only meets it receives 5%. Different rewards are fair when they reflect genuine differences in performance, and the same reward is fair when performance is the same.

How to decide bonuses: use clear, objective and transparent criteria such as targets achieved, quality, and effort, apply them to everyone, and explain the decision so employees can see it is fair.

Case 7: A bank transfers new employees to a different department every two months; many become frustrated and leave within a year

Principle neglected: Stability of tenure. Employees should not be moved frequently from one position to another, and their period of service in a job should be reasonably fixed.

Effect of frequent transfers: new staff need time to learn a job and become productive, but they are moved before they become competent, so they never perform at their best. They feel insecure and frustrated, team spirit and belonging fall, and many resign. The time, effort and money spent on training are wasted, and quality and quantity of work suffer.

When a transfer is appropriate: when the organization genuinely needs it, such as filling a vital vacancy, matching an employee's skills to a better role, a promotion or development plan, or the employee's own request. Even then, the employee should get enough time and support to settle in.

Case 8: A customer-service employee suggests a faster response method and the manager refuses, saying staff should follow instructions

Principle involved: Initiative. Initiative means eagerness to start action without being asked, and management should encourage employees to suggest ideas and new methods.

How the manager discouraged initiative: by rejecting the idea without evaluation and insisting that staff only follow instructions, which told the employee that suggestions are not welcome. She eventually stopped sharing ideas.

How the company could benefit: employee ideas can cut response time, improve customer service, reduce costs and foster creativity and innovation. Encouraging initiative builds an atmosphere of trust and understanding and develops future leaders, and rewards (financial or non-financial) can motivate more suggestions.

Does encouraging initiative mean accepting every idea? No. Management should listen, test and evaluate suggestions, adopt the feasible ones, and give reasons when an idea is not used, so that employees keep contributing.

Case 9: Software team members compete for an individual award, hide information and blame colleagues

Principle weakened: Esprit de corps (team spirit and unity). It is about mutual trust, common interest, open communication and cooperation among people working together.

Effect of the reward system: rewarding only individual output made colleagues rivals instead of partners. People hid information, blamed others for errors, and communication broke down. Individual output rose briefly, but cooperation and project quality declined.

How to encourage cooperation and still recognize performance: add team goals and shared rewards alongside individual recognition, reward information sharing and helping others, encourage open communication and joint problem solving, and avoid forced ranking that sets members against each other. Management should also maintain unity of command so the team shares one direction.

Case 10: Two marketing managers run separate campaigns for the same beverage launch, one for students and one for executives

Principle violated: Unity of direction. Activities with the same objective should have one head and one plan.

Why separate plans can damage the launch: the two campaigns send different messages (an affordable student drink versus a premium executive product). This confuses customers about the brand's positioning and price, splits the budget and effort, and duplicates work, so the launch becomes inconsistent.

Why management should coordinate: a single plan under one responsible person, such as a product manager, aligns all teams toward the common goal of a successful launch. Segments can still be targeted, but with one consistent positioning, message and schedule.

Case 11: A purchasing manager awards the contract to his brother's firm, which charges more and gives lower-quality materials

Principle violated: Subordination of individual interest to the general interest.

Interest placed first: the manager put his personal and family interest (helping his brother) ahead of the company's interest in getting good materials at a fair price.

How this could harm the company: higher costs reduce profit, lower-quality materials harm product quality and may lead to customer complaints and brand damage, and favoritism can demoralize other suppliers and employees and create legal or ethical problems.

What he should have done: declare the conflict of interest, withdraw from the decision, and choose the supplier by objective criteria such as price, quality and reliability. The organization is bigger than any one individual, so its interests must prevail.

Case 12: Lina handles difficult complaints and works extra hours; Sara does routine tasks in normal hours; both get the same pay

Principle involved: Fair remuneration. Pay should be fair, reasonable, satisfactory and rewarding of effort.

Why Lina may find it unfair: she carries harder work and longer hours but receives the same reward as Sara, and management has no clear system to explain how rewards are set. This can lower her motivation and lead her to reduce effort or leave.

Factors that should determine pay: the work assigned (difficulty, responsibility), hours and effort, performance, cost of living, the wage rate in the market, and the financial position of the business. The method of evaluating performance should be transparent.

Should equal job titles always mean equal pay? No. Titles can be the same while workload, performance and responsibility differ, so total pay can reasonably differ (for example, overtime pay or performance bonuses) as long as the system is clear, consistent and applied fairly.

Case 13: A restaurant chain requires head-office approval for replacing cheap supplies or handling minor complaints, and customers wait days

Principle involved: Centralization and decentralization. The company is highly centralized, because decision-making authority is concentrated at the head office.

Effect on customer service: branch managers cannot solve small problems quickly. Customers wait days for simple issues, which lowers satisfaction, hurts the branch's reputation and wastes head-office time on trivial matters. Branch managers feel distrusted and less motivated.

Decisions that could be delegated: replacing low-cost supplies and equipment, resolving minor customer complaints, small refunds or discounts, and local scheduling. Major decisions such as menu changes, large purchases and pricing policy can remain with the head office. Fayol said absolute centralization or decentralization is not feasible, so the company should strike a balance based on branch size, manager experience and reliability.

Case 14: A junior technician finds a serious safety problem but must report to his supervisor, then the department manager, then two more levels before reaching the safety director

Principle explaining the reporting line: Scalar chain. It is the clear, direct line of authority from the highest to the lowest level, through which orders and messages pass, so that everyone knows their role.

Risk of following it rigidly in an emergency: a serious safety problem needs an immediate response, but a message passing through many levels causes delay, can be distorted or lost, and may never reach the safety director in time. The delay can lead to injuries, equipment damage or legal liability.

When direct communication should be allowed: in emergencies and urgent safety situations, or when the normal chain is too slow. This is the idea of a "gang plank" (direct contact across the chain). The technician should contact the safety director directly and inform his supervisor immediately afterward so the chain is kept informed.
