Lecture 1

Chapter 1 - Foundations of Economics

What is Economics?

Economics is the study of how people and societies make choices when resources are limited but wants are unlimited. Because we cannot have everything we want, we must choose.

Source example: with Tk 500, you can buy a book or go out to eat.

Micro vs Macro

Microeconomics: households, firms, specific markets, supply, demand, prices and resource distribution.

Macroeconomics: economy-wide inflation, unemployment and economic growth.

Trade-offs

To get one thing we like, we usually have to give up another thing we like. The lecture uses Guns vs Butter: more defense spending leaves fewer consumer goods.

Efficiency vs Equality

Efficiency is the size of the economic pie; equality is how the pie is divided among people.

Opportunity Cost

The opportunity cost of an item is what you give up to get that item - specifically, the next best alternative, not all alternatives.

School example: time spent attending school cannot be spent earning wages at a job. The forgone earnings are an opportunity cost.

Scarcity

Scarcity = limited resources vs unlimited wants.

Resources named in the lecture: time, money, land, labor and capital.

Scarcity forces choice; choice creates opportunity cost.

Marginal Analysis

Rational people think at the margin. A marginal change is a small incremental adjustment to a plan.

Take an action when \(MB > MC\).

Rational people systematically and purposefully do the best they can to achieve their objectives.

Three Basic Economic Decisions

  1. What to produce? - PPF
  2. How to produce? - Isoquant
  3. For whom to produce? - distribution of output

Positive vs Normative

Positive: descriptive; a claim about what the world is; can be examined with evidence.

Normative: prescriptive; a claim about what the world ought to be; involves value judgments.

Graphs + Short Run / Long Run

Graphs show relationships between variables. Convention in the lecture: X-axis = independent variable; Y-axis = dependent variable. Common economic graphs include demand, supply, cost and production possibility curves.

Short run: at least one production factor is fixed. Long run: all production factors are variable.

Lecture 2

Chapter 2 - Economic Systems & the 10 Principles

Economic System

An economic system is the way a society organizes production, distribution and consumption. It answers: What to produce? How to produce? For whom to produce?

Traditional Economy

Decisions are based on customs, traditions and cultural beliefs. Production is mainly for self-consumption; technology is simple and barter may be used.

Advantages: strong social relationships, stability, less competition/stress.

Disadvantages: low productivity, slow growth, lower living standards, limited occupation/income choices.

Command / Planned Economy

Government makes major production and distribution decisions. It owns most land, factories and resources; prices are fixed by government.

Advantages: can reduce inequality, mobilize resources quickly, provide basic goods.

Disadvantages: weak incentives, shortages/surpluses, inefficiency and bureaucracy.

Market Economy

Individuals and private firms make major decisions. Prices, markets, profits/losses and incentives guide activity. A completely hands-off version is called laissez-faire.

Advantages: efficiency, innovation, consumer choice, growth.

Disadvantages: inequality, possible monopoly power, instability.

Mixed Economy

Private individuals and government both play important roles. Most modern countries are described by the lecture as mixed economies.

Advantages: balances efficiency and welfare, limits extreme inequality, protects consumers/workers.

Disadvantages: intervention can create inefficiency, bureaucracy and policy conflicts.

The 10 Principles of Economics

  1. People face trade-offs.
  2. The cost of something is what you give up to get it.
  3. Rational people think at the margin.
  4. People respond to incentives.
  5. Trade can make everyone better off.
  6. Markets are usually a good way to organize economic activity.
  7. Governments can sometimes improve market outcomes.
  8. A country’s standard of living depends on its ability to produce goods and services (productivity).
  9. Prices rise when the government prints too much money.
  10. Society faces a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment.
Lecture 3

Chapter 3 - Inputs, Outputs, PPF & Isoquants

Inputs / Factors of Production

Land: natural resources. Labor: human time spent in production. Capital: durable goods produced to produce other goods.

Output: useful goods or services produced.

What to Produce? - PPF

A Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF) shows the maximum possible combinations of two goods an economy/business can produce with limited resources and current technology.

PPF assumptions in the lecture

  • Only two goods.
  • Same resources are used for both.
  • Quantity and quality of resources stay constant.
  • Technology stays constant.
  • Resources are fully employed.
  • Efficient production methods are used.

How to read a point

On PPF: efficient and attainable.

Below PPF: attainable but inefficient; resources are underutilized.

Above PPF: unattainable/infeasible given current scarcity.

Recreated PPF - Guns & Butter

Recreated from the source lecture’s guns-and-butter PPF discussion; source gives 15,000 guns at zero butter and 5 million pounds of butter at zero guns.

PPF rotation

Rotation occurs when productive capacity/technology changes for only one of the two goods.

How to Produce?

Choose the most efficient technology and combination of labor, land, capital and entrepreneurship. The lecture contrasts:

Labor-intensive

High use of labor; generally chosen when labor is cheap or abundant.

Capital-intensive

Heavy reliance on machinery/technology; preferred when capital is cheaper than labor.

Recreated Isoquant

Source states: point A = 1 capital + 10 labor; point D = 4 capital + 1 labor. Isoquant is downward-sloping and convex to the origin.

Isoquant map

Different isoquants represent different output levels. Source example: initially q = 275, then q = 375, then q = 450; higher output shifts the isoquant outward/right.

For whom to produce? concerns how limited goods/services are distributed based on income, purchasing power or need.

Chapter 4 - Markets & Demand

Market & Perfect Competition

A market is a group of buyers and sellers of a good/service. A perfectly competitive market has identical goods and many buyers and sellers, so no single participant can influence market price; buyers and sellers are price takers.

Demand

Quantity demanded: the amount buyers are willing and able to purchase.

Law of demand: other things equal, when price rises, quantity demanded falls; when price falls, quantity demanded rises.

The demand curve slopes downward.

Recreated demand curve

Source graph convention: price on vertical axis, quantity demanded on horizontal axis.

Demand schedule + market demand

The lecture’s Catherine example: free → 12 cones; Tk/$1 → 10 cones; as price rises, quantity demanded falls; at $6, Catherine buys zero. Market demand is the sum of individual demands.

Demand shifters

  • Income: normal good → income ↑, demand ↑; inferior good → income ↑, demand ↓.
  • Prices of related goods: substitutes and complements.
  • Tastes.
  • Expectations.
  • Number of buyers.

Lecture 5

Chapter 5 - Supply & Market Equilibrium

Supply

Quantity supplied: amount sellers are willing and able to sell.

Law of supply: other things equal, price ↑ → quantity supplied ↑; price ↓ → quantity supplied ↓.

Recreated supply curve

Supply curve slopes upward in the source because higher price means greater quantity supplied.

Supply shifters

  • Input prices: higher input prices reduce supply.
  • Technology: cost-reducing technology raises supply.
  • Expectations: expected future price rises can reduce current supply.
  • Number of sellers: fewer sellers reduce market supply.

Market supply

Market supply is the sum of individual supplies. The source describes horizontal addition of individual supply curves: at any price, add quantities supplied by each seller.

Equilibrium

Equilibrium occurs where quantity supplied = quantity demanded. The price is the equilibrium price / market-clearing price; the quantity is the equilibrium quantity.

Surplus vs shortage

Surplus: price above equilibrium → quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.

Shortage: price below equilibrium → quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.

Recreated market equilibrium

Recreated from the source's Market Equilibrium figure: supply and demand intersect at the equilibrium price of $2.00 and equilibrium quantity of 7 ice-cream cones.
Lecture 6

Chapter 6 - Elasticity

What is elasticity?

Elasticity measures the responsiveness of one economic variable to changes in another. The lecture emphasizes percentage changes rather than absolute changes.

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

Measures how much quantity demanded responds to a change in the price of the same good.

\( PED = \left|\frac{\%\,\Delta Q_d}{\%\,\Delta P}\right| \)

|PED| > 1: elastic   |PED| = 1: unit elastic   |PED| < 1: inelastic.

PED determinants

  • Availability of close substitutes.
  • Necessities vs luxuries.
  • Time horizon: demand is more elastic in the long run.

Midpoint method - source worked example

Source example: P₁ = 10, P₂ = 12; Q₁ = 100, Q₂ = 80.

\(PED = \frac{\Delta Q / ((Q_1+Q_2)/2)} {\Delta P / ((P_1+P_2)/2)} = 1\)

The source ignores the negative sign when interpreting PED.

Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

\( YED = \frac{\%\,\Delta Q_d}{\%\,\Delta Income} \)

YED > 1: luxury/normal; 0 < YED < 1: necessity/normal; YED = 0: neutral; YED < 0: inferior.

Cross-Price Elasticity (XED)

\( XED = \frac{\%\,\Delta Q_x}{\%\,\Delta P_y} \)

XED > 0: substitutes; XED < 0: complements; XED = 0: unrelated goods.

Source worked examples

YED: income 1000 → 1100, quantity 50 → 55 gives YED = 1.0, unit income elastic.

XED: tea price 10 → 12, coffee quantity 40 → 50 gives XED = +1.25; tea and coffee are substitutes.

Chapter 7 - Consumer Theory & Utility

Consumer theory

Consumer theory explores how individuals make consumption decisions to maximize satisfaction within limits of income and market prices. The objective is the highest possible utility.

Utility

Utility is satisfaction or pleasure from consuming goods/services. It is subjective and may be represented analytically in hypothetical units called utils.

Cardinal utility: utility is assigned numerical values; associated in the lecture with Marshall.

Ordinal utility: utility cannot be measured numerically; preferences can be ranked. The lecture associates this approach with Hicks and Allen.

Total Utility (TU)

TU = total satisfaction from consuming a given quantity.

Marginal Utility (MU)

Marginal utility is the additional satisfaction from one more unit.

\( MU = \frac{\Delta TU}{\Delta Q} \)

Source values for Q = 1…5: MU = 10, 8, 6, 4, 2.

Law of diminishing marginal utility: as consumption rises, the marginal utility from each additional unit decreases, holding other factors constant.

Lecture 7 & 8

Chapter 8 - Indifference Curves, Budget Line & Consumer Equilibrium

Indifference Curve (IC)

An IC contains combinations of two goods giving the consumer the same satisfaction.

  • Downward sloping.
  • Convex to the origin due to diminishing MRS.
  • Higher IC = higher satisfaction.
  • ICs never intersect.

Source examples

Apple/Orange example: (2,4) and (3,2) both give utility 10.

Another IC schedule: X = 1,2,3,4,5 with Y = 10,7,5,4,3.5.

Recreated IC map

Budget line

The budget line shows all combinations of two goods the consumer can afford given income and prices. It is a straight line with a negative slope.

Source example: M = 100, Pₓ = 2, Pᵧ = 1 → X-intercept = 50 and Y-intercept = 100.

\( P_xX + P_yY = M \)

Consumer equilibrium

Consumer equilibrium is at the point where the highest attainable indifference curve is tangent to the budget line. There, satisfaction is maximized given the budget constraint.

Chapter 9 - Producer Theory & Production Functions

What is production?

Production is the transformation of inputs such as labor, capital, land and technology into outputs (goods/services). The lecture states that the main objective is to maximize output or profit using limited resources efficiently.

Four factors of production

  • Land: natural resources such as soil, water, minerals.
  • Labor: human effort, physical and mental.
  • Capital: machinery, tools, infrastructure.
  • Entrepreneurship: decision-making, risk-taking, innovation.

Production function

A mathematical relationship showing how inputs are combined to produce output.

\( Q = f(L,K) \)

Q = output, L = labor, K = capital. The lecture assumes technology is constant when explaining this relationship.

Fixed input vs variable input

Fixed input: cannot be changed in the short run. Examples: machinery, factory building, land.
Variable input: can be changed in the short run. Examples: labor, raw materials, electricity usage.

Short run vs long run production

Short run: at least one factor is fixed (usually capital), so firms adjust variable inputs such as labor.

\(Q=f(L,\bar K)\)

Long run: all factors are variable, so firms can change scale. The lecture links this to returns to scale: increasing, constant, decreasing.

Chapter 10 - TP, MP, AP & Production Stages

Total Product (TP)

Total quantity of output produced using given inputs.

Marginal Product (MP)

Additional output from one more unit of the variable input, usually labor, holding other inputs constant.

\( MP = \frac{\Delta TP}{\Delta L} \)

Average Product (AP)

Output per unit of variable input.

\( AP = \frac{TP}{L} \)

TP and MP - source-faithful recreation

How TP and MP move together

  • When MP > 0, TP rises.
  • When MP = 0, TP is at its maximum/flat point.
  • When MP < 0, TP falls.

This relationship is central to the source’s three stages of production.

Three stages of production

Stage Source description Key relationship Conclusion
1 - Increasing returns / Growth TP rises at an increasing rate; MP rises; AP rises MP > AP; MP pulls AP upward Not rational; resources underutilized
2 - Diminishing returns / Efficiency TP rises at a decreasing rate; MP falls but remains positive; AP falls MP < AP; MP pulls AP downward Rational stage
3 - Negative returns / Decline TP starts decreasing; MP negative; AP falls MP is negative, so TP falls Irrational stage
Lecture 9–11

Chapter 11 - Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns

Definition

The law of diminishing returns states that as more units of a variable input such as labor are added to fixed inputs such as capital, the marginal product eventually decreases, holding technology constant.

Conditions emphasized in the source

  • At least one input is fixed (short run).
  • Technology remains unchanged.
  • Units of the variable input are homogeneous.

Remember the stage logic

Stage 1: MP ↑, AP ↑, TP ↑↑.

Stage 2: MP ↓ but positive, TP ↑, AP ↓.

Stage 3: MP negative, TP ↓, AP ↓.

Source conclusion: Stage 2 is the efficient/rational stage.

Q2. Price rises Tk 40 → Tk 60; quantity demanded falls 90 → 70

a) Which good is likely to have more elastic demand?

The source identifies three important determinants of PED: close substitutes, necessity vs luxury, and time horizon . A good with many close substitutes is more likely to have elastic demand because consumers can switch away easily. Luxuries tend to be more elastic than necessities, and demand is generally more elastic in the long run.

For this sample, the supplied numbers tell us the demand response is inelastic; they do not identify a second good to compare directly. So the safest exam statement is: the more elastic good would be the one with more close substitutes (or a luxury / longer adjustment period).

Chapter 6 - Determinants of PED.

b) Calculate PED

Step 1: ΔQ = 70 − 90 = −20; ΔP = 60 − 40 = 20.
Step 2: Use the midpoint percentage changes: average Q = (90+70)/2 = 80; average P = (40+60)/2 = 50.
Step 3: \(PED=\left|(-20/80)/(20/50)\right| =|(-0.25)/(0.40)| =0.625\).
Interpretation: 0.625 < 1, so demand is inelastic.

Q5. Owner’s building + unpaid labor

a) Explicit vs implicit

Implicit costs: the building’s forgone rental income of Tk 15,000 per month, plus the opportunity cost of the owner’s unpaid full-time labor.

Explicit costs: none are described in the question, so none can be identified from the information given.

b) Why include implicit costs?

Economists include implicit costs because economic cost is based on opportunity cost, not only cash payments. The owner gives up rent that the building could earn and gives up the wage she could earn from another job. Those forgone alternatives are real economic costs even though no money changes hands.

Chapter 1 - Opportunity cost gives the underlying logic; the explicit/implicit cost labels themselves are prerequisite material for this problem set.
