Lecture 1 · What is Economics?

Economics is the study of how society uses scarce resources to produce goods and services and distribute them among people. Scarcity means resources are limited but wants are unlimited.

1776Adam Smith — The Wealth of Nations: free markets, competition, and self-interest can drive prosperity with limited government.
1936J.M. Keynes — The General Theory: after the Great Depression, argued government spending and policy are needed to manage the economy.

Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

BasisMicroeconomicsMacroeconomics
Scope of StudyIndividual consumers, firms, and specific marketsThe economy as a whole
Main FocusPrice determination, consumer behavior, firm decisionsNational income, inflation, unemployment, growth
Key VariablesDemand, supply, elasticity, costs, profitGDP, inflation rate, unemployment rate, interest rates
Level of AnalysisIndividual or industry levelAggregate or national level
Policy RelevancePricing, taxation, market regulationFiscal and monetary policy
ExamplePrice of a single product like riceInflation rate or GDP growth of a country
Micro ↔ Macro Linkage: Macro outcomes result from millions of micro-level decisions by households and firms. In turn, macro policies (taxation, interest rates) shape micro-level behavior — the two are closely linked.

Example: Increased government spending during a recession (macro) can boost overall economic activity and reduce unemployment.

Macroeconomic Variables

  • GDP — total value of goods & services produced
  • National Income — total income earned by factors of production
  • Interest Rate — cost of borrowing / return on saving
  • Money Supply — total money circulating in the economy
  • Exchange Rate — value of domestic vs foreign currency
  • Balance of Payments — record of international transactions
  • Inflation Rate — rate at which the general price level rises
  • Fiscal Deficit — gap between govt. expenditure & revenue
  • Growth Rate — annual increase in real GDP
  • Unemployment Rate — % of labor force without jobs
  • Public Debt — total government borrowings
  • Aggregate Demand — total spending by households & firms

Grouped: Output & Growth (GDP, GNP, NNP, National Income) · Prices (Inflation) · Labor Market (Unemployment) · Money & Spending (Consumption, Investment, Money Demand & Supply, Interest Rate)

Relationships Among Macroeconomic Variables

Growth Channel: Consumption + Investment → Aggregate Demand → GDP ↑ → Employment ↑
Inflation Channel: Money Supply / Aggregate Demand ↑ → Inflation ↑ → Central Bank raises Interest Rates → Borrowing/Growth ↓, Inflation eases
Unemployment (Okun’s Law): Output ↑ → Unemployment ↓ — fiscal & monetary policy influence output.

Macroeconomic variables are closely interrelated — a change in one often ripples through many others.

Macroeconomic Objectives

  • Economic growth — increase in Real GDP
  • Full employment / reducing unemployment
  • Reducing inflation / stable price level
  • Reducing inequality of income
  • Foreign exchange management
  • Debt management
  • Sustainable development

Real-Life Examples

  • Government wants to reduce unemployment → uses macroeconomic policies (e.g. public works programs).
  • Government wants to control price rises → studies inflation and adjusts monetary policy.
  • Government wants long-term growth without harming the environment → promotes sustainable development.

Why Study Macroeconomics?

  • Understanding National Income — measures how rich or poor a country is, using indicators like GDP, GNP, and NNP.
  • Solving Unemployment — explains why unemployment exists and how government policy can reduce it.
  • Controlling Inflation — inflation means a continuous rise in general prices; macroeconomics suggests monetary and fiscal policy responses.

Short Run and Long Run

In Microeconomics — Short Run

A period in which at least one factor of production is fixed — the firm cannot change it immediately.

Example (shoe factory): Capital (machines) = fixed FOP; Labor = variable FOP.

In Microeconomics — Long Run

A period in which all factors of production are variable — the firm can change factory size, buy more machines, enter or exit the market. In the long run, there are no fixed factors.

In Macroeconomics — Short Run

A period in which prices are sticky — they do not adjust immediately. Sticky prices are prices that do not change immediately or adjust slowly, due to contracts, wages, or menu costs.

Example: Workers sign a 1-year salary contract. If the economy slows, wages cannot immediately fall.

Key features of short-run macro: output fluctuates; unemployment changes; demand affects production; business cycles occur.

In Macroeconomics — Long Run

  • Prices are fully flexible — wages and prices adjust, and the economy returns to its natural level of output.
  • Output depends on real factors — technology, capital, labor, institutions — instead of the money supply.

Lecture 2 · Measuring GDP

GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period of time.

  • “Market Value” — market prices measure the amount people are willing to pay, reflecting the value of goods. If an apple costs twice as much as an orange, it contributes twice as much to GDP.
  • “of All” — all items produced in the economy and sold legally in markets. GDP excludes illegal goods (like illicit drugs) and items produced and consumed at home, which never enter the marketplace.
  • “Final” — only final goods & services (intermediate goods are already counted in the final price).
  • “Produced” — currently produced; used goods sold today do not count.
  • “Within a Country” — production within a country’s borders.
  • “In a Given Time Period” — measured per year or quarter.

Why “Domestic” and Why “Gross”?

  • Domestic — production within a country. Contrasts with national product: value of goods and services produced anywhere in the world by residents of a country.
  • Gross — before subtracting the depreciation of capital. The opposite, net, means after subtracting depreciation.

What GDP Excludes

  • Intermediate goods (already counted in final price)
  • Sale of used goods (counted when first produced)
  • Financial asset purchases (not current production)
  • Transfer payments (no current production)
  • Home-produced goods (no market transaction)
  • Illegal / unreported activities (unmeasured)
  • Also excluded: leisure time, happiness, environmental quality, and income distribution

The Components of GDP

Y = C + I + G + NX

This is an identity — it must be true because of how the variables are defined.

ComponentDefinition
Consumption (C)Spending by households on goods and services, except purchases of new housing.
Investment (I)Purchase of capital goods that will be used in the future to produce more goods and services.
Government Purchases (G)Spending on goods and services by local, state, and federal governments, excluding transfer payments.
Net Exports (NX)Spending on domestically produced goods by foreigners (exports) minus spending on foreign goods by domestic residents (imports).

Real vs Nominal GDP

  • Nominal GDP — production of goods and services valued at current prices.
  • Real GDP — production of goods and services valued at constant (base-year) prices.

Calculating Nominal GDP

Nominal GDP = Σ (Current Year Price × Current Year Quantity)
= (P₁ × Q₁) + (P₂ × Q₂) + ... + (Pₙ × Qₙ)

Worked solution — click to expand

Nominal GDP (Year 1) = (50 × 1,000) + (20,000 × 100) = 50,000 + 2,000,000 = Tk. 2,050,000

Nominal GDP (Year 2) = (60 × 1,100) + (22,000 × 120) = 66,000 + 2,640,000 = Tk. 2,706,000

Nominal GDP rose because both prices and quantities rose. Nominal GDP does not distinguish whether the increase is due to higher production, higher prices, or both — this is why economists use Real GDP.

Calculating Real GDP

To see real growth, use a base-year price for every year. This “freezes” prices so we can see changes in production volume.

Real GDP = Σ (Base Year Price × Current Year Quantity)
Worked solution — click to expand (Year 1 = base year)

Real GDP (Year 2) = (50 × 1,100) + (20,000 × 120) = 55,000 + 2,400,000 = Tk. 2,455,000

  • In the base year (Year 1), Nominal GDP equals Real GDP.
  • In Year 2, Nominal GDP (Tk. 2,706,000) is higher than Real GDP (Tk. 2,455,000) because prices increased.
  • Real GDP measures changes in production (output) only; Nominal GDP reflects changes in both prices and quantities.

The GDP Deflator

A price index measuring the change in prices of all final goods and services produced within a country — used to gauge overall inflation.

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100
YearNominal GDP (Tk.)Real GDP (Tk.)
Year 12,050,0002,050,000
Year 22,706,0002,455,000

GDP Deflator (Year 2) = (2,706,000 / 2,455,000) × 100 = 1.1022 × 100 = 110.22

  • Year 1 is the base year, so the deflator is 100.
  • A deflator of 110.22 means the overall price level is 10.22% higher than in the base year.
  • Unlike the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks a fixed basket of consumer goods, the GDP Deflator reflects price changes for all domestically produced final goods and services.

Calculating the Inflation Rate

Inflation Rate = [(Deflatorₗ − Deflatorₗ−₁) / Deflatorₗ−₁] × 100

Inflation Rate (Year 2) = [(110.22 − 100) / 100] × 100 = 10.22%

The overall price level of domestically produced final goods and services increased by 10.22% from Year 1 to Year 2, as measured by the GDP deflator.

National Income: The Expenditure Approach

Measures GDP as the sum of consumption, investment, government expenditure on goods and services, and net exports:

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
  • C — consumption expenditure by households
  • I — investment expenditure by firms
  • G — government expenditure
  • X − M — exports minus imports = net exports
Worked example — click to expand

Given: C = 1,500,000; I = 600,000; G = 400,000; NX = 200,000

Step 1: Net exports: X − M = 350,000 − 150,000 = 200,000

Step 2: GDP = 1,500,000 + 600,000 + 400,000 + 200,000 = Tk. 2,700,000

Households spent Tk. 1.5 million; firms invested Tk. 0.6 million; government spent Tk. 0.4 million; exports exceeded imports by Tk. 0.2 million. The economy’s GDP is Tk. 2.7 million.

The Income Approach

When firms produce goods and services, they pay factors of production:

  • Wages → to workers
  • Rent → to landowners
  • Interest → to capital owners
  • Profit → to entrepreneurs
GDP = Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit
GDP = Factor Income + Indirect Taxes + Depreciation
Worked example — click to expand

Given: Wages = 500; Rent = 100; Interest = 80; Profit = 120; Indirect Tax = 50; Depreciation = 30

GDP = 500 + 100 + 80 + 120 + 50 + 30 = 880

In real-world GDP calculation, we add indirect taxes (VAT, sales tax) and depreciation (consumption of fixed capital) to factor income.
