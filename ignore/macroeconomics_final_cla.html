Lecture 1: The Economy as a Whole

Growth, prices and employment

What is Economics?

Economics is the study of how society uses scarce resources to produce goods and services and distribute them among people.

Scarcity means resources are limited but wants are unlimited.

A brief history

1776, Adam Smith
The Wealth of Nations: free markets, competition and self-interest can drive prosperity with limited government intervention.
1936, J. M. Keynes
The General Theory: after the Great Depression, argued government spending and policy are needed to manage downturns.
Smith = markets left alone (1776). Keynes = government steps in (1936).

Branches: Micro vs Macro

Microeconomics
How firms and individuals make decisions and how they interact: prices set by supply and demand, and how incentives affect behavior.
Coffee bean prices rise, so coffee shops buy less and customers pay more per cup.
Macroeconomics
The economy as a whole: aggregate outcomes such as inflation, unemployment and growth, and how government and central banks influence national income and business cycles.
More government spending in a recession boosts activity and cuts unemployment nationwide.

Definition of Macroeconomics

The study of the economy as a whole, including growth in income, changes in prices and the rate of unemployment. Macroeconomists try both to explain economic events and to devise policies to improve performance.

Simple example
One student's income becomes total income of the whole country. One shop's price becomes overall inflation.
Micro and macro linkage
Macro outcomes come from millions of micro decisions by households and firms. Macro policies (taxation, interest rates) in turn shape micro behavior.

Macroeconomic Variables

GDP: total value of goods and services produced
National Income: total income earned by factors of production
Inflation Rate: rate at which the general price level rises
Unemployment Rate: % of labor force without jobs
Interest Rate: cost of borrowing / return on saving
Money Supply: total money circulating
Fiscal Deficit: gap between government expenditure and revenue
Public Debt: total government borrowings
Exchange Rate: value of domestic vs foreign currency
Balance of Payments: record of international transactions
Growth Rate: annual increase in real GDP
Aggregate Demand: total spending by households and firms

Recap by category

  • Output and growth: GDP, GNP, NNP, National Income
  • Prices: Inflation
  • Labor market: Unemployment
  • Money and spending: Consumption, Investment, Money demand and supply, Interest rate

How variables relate

Growth channel
Consumption + Investment ➜ Aggregate Demand ➜ GDP up ➜ Employment up
Inflation channel
Money supply / AD up ➜ Inflation up ➜ Central bank raises interest rates ➜ Borrowing and growth fall, inflation eases
Okun's Law
Output up ➜ Unemployment down

Fiscal and monetary policy also shape exchange rates, capital flows and the balance of payments. The system works together, not in isolation.

Objectives and Why We Study It

Macroeconomic objectives

  • Economic growth (rise in real GDP)
  • Full employment / lower unemployment
  • Lower inflation / stable prices
  • Reduced income inequality
  • Foreign exchange management
  • Debt management
  • Sustainable development
Cut unemployment: public works programs. Control price rises: study inflation and adjust monetary policy. Long-term growth without harming the environment: sustainable development.

Why study macroeconomics?

Understanding national income
Shows how rich or poor a country is using GDP, GNP and NNP.
Solving unemployment
Explains why it exists and how policy can reduce it.
Controlling inflation
A continuous rise in general prices; monetary and fiscal policy respond.

Stock vs Flow Variables

Stock: measured at a point in time (no time period). Like the water level in a bathtub at one moment.
  • Wealth on 31 March
  • Population on 1 January
  • Money supply today
Flow: measured over a period (per year, per month). Like water flowing into the bathtub.
  • National income per year
  • Salary per month
  • GDP per year
A photo is a stock (one instant). A video is a flow (a duration). Income is a flow; wealth is a stock.

Short Run and Long Run

In microeconomics

Short run: at least one factor of production (FOP) is fixed and cannot be changed immediately. Shoe factory: capital (machines) is fixed, labor is variable.
Long run: all FOPs are variable. The shoe factory can build a new plant, buy new technology, expand production. Nothing is fixed.

In macroeconomics

Short run: prices are sticky
They do not adjust quickly because of contracts, wages or regulations. Example: workers on a 1-year contract, so wages cannot fall immediately in a slowdown.
Features: output fluctuates, unemployment changes, demand affects production, business cycles occur.
Long run: prices fully flexible
Wages and prices adjust and the economy returns to its natural level of output. Output depends on real factors: technology, capital, labor, institutions (not money supply).

Lecture 2: Measuring GDP

Nominal, real, deflator and national income

The Measurement of GDP

GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period of time.
  • Market value: prices show what people will pay. An apple twice the price of an orange counts twice as much.
  • Of all: everything produced and sold legally in markets. Excludes illegal goods (illicit drugs) and items produced and consumed at home.
WordMeaning
FinalOnly final goods; intermediate goods are already in the final price. Exception: a firm's inventory for later sale counts as final.
Goods and servicesTangible goods (food, cars) and intangible services (haircuts, doctor visits).
ProducedOnly current production; resale of past-produced items is excluded.
Within a countryValue of production inside the geographic borders.
In a given periodA specific interval of time.
Why "Domestic"?
Production within a country. Contrast: national product is value produced anywhere by the residents of a nation.
Why "Gross"?
Before subtracting depreciation of capital. "Net" means after subtracting it.

Components of GDP

Y = C + I + G + NX

This is an identity: it must be true because of how the variables are defined.

Consumption (C)
Household spending on goods and services, except purchases of new housing.
Investment (I)
Purchase of capital goods used in future to produce more goods and services.
Govt purchases (G)
Spending by local, state and federal governments, excluding transfer payments.

NX = net exports = exports (X) minus imports (M).

Y = C I G NX: "Can I Get eXtra?"

Calculating Nominal GDP

Nominal GDP uses current-year prices times current-year quantities.

Year 1: (50 x 1,000) + (20,000 x 100) = 50,000 + 2,000,000 = Tk. 2,050,000
Year 2: (60 x 1,100) + (22,000 x 120) = 66,000 + 2,640,000 = Tk. 2,706,000

Nominal GDP rose because both prices and quantities rose. It cannot tell which caused the rise, so economists use Real GDP (fixed base-year prices) to measure changes in actual output.

Extra (the slides on Real GDP and the deflator are not in your PDF): Real GDP = quantities x base-year prices. GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) x 100.

The Expenditure Approach

Given: C = 1,500,000; I = 600,000; G = 400,000. NX = X - M = 350,000 - 150,000 = 200,000.
GDP = 1,500,000 + 600,000 + 400,000 + 200,000 = Tk. 2,700,000

The Income Approach

GDP = Total income earned by factors

Firms pay: Wages (to workers), Rent (landowners), Interest (capital owners), Profit (entrepreneurs).

GDP = Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit

In real-world calculation we also add indirect taxes (VAT, sales tax) and depreciation: GDP = Factor income + Indirect taxes + Depreciation.

Example: Wages 500, Rent 100, Interest 80, Profit 120, Indirect tax 50, Depreciation 30.
GDP = 500 + 100 + 80 + 120 + 50 + 30 = 880

Value added is the value of a firm's output minus the value of intermediate goods it used. It measures each firm's net contribution and prevents double-counting.

Other Measures of Income

GNP: total income earned by nationals (residents).
GNP = GDP + Factor payments from abroad - Factor payments to abroad
NNP: GNP minus depreciation (about 10% of GNP). Depreciation is a cost of production, so removing it shows net result.
NNP = GNP - Depreciation
National Income: how much everyone has earned.
NI = NNP - Indirect business taxes (NIT)
Net indirect taxes (NIT) = Indirect taxes - Subsidies.
Personal Income: income households and noncorporate businesses receive.
PI = NI - Corporate profits - Social security contributions + Government transfers
(Slide also lists net interest, dividends and personal interest income.)
Disposable Personal Income: amount available to spend after taxes.
DPI = PI - Personal tax and nontax payments
Shorthand: Yd = Y - T. In intro macro, Disposable Income = PDI.
Chain: GDP ➜ GNP ➜ NNP ➜ NI ➜ PI ➜ DPI. Each step adds or subtracts one thing.

Import Quotas and Tariff Rate Quotas

Import quota
Direct quantity restriction, often run through license-based control.
Tariff rate quota (TRQ)
A dual tariff system with variable tariff rates: low inside the quota, high above it.

A TRQ is a hybrid of a quota and a tariff. Common in agriculture, where the goal is to limit imports.

South Korea rice: 10% ad valorem tariff on the first 1 million tons, then 80% out-of-quota. If it imports 2 million tons: 1 million at 10% and 1 million at 80%.

VER (Voluntary Export Restraints) and quota rent

Your slide only has this title; the explanation below is my expansion.

A VER is a quota the exporting country agrees to impose on itself, usually under pressure from the importer. Limiting supply raises the domestic price above the world price. That price gap times the quantity is the quota rent: extra profit captured by whoever holds the right to sell or import (license holders, or exporters under a VER), instead of by the government as tariff revenue.

Tariff = government gets the money. Quota or VER = license holders or exporters get it (quota rent).

Lecture 5: Inflation and Unemployment

CPI, inflation rate, labor market measures and unemployment types

Consumer Price Index (CPI)

CPI measures the average change in prices of a fixed basket of goods and services over time. It is the most common measure of price level and is widely used to measure inflation. It turns many prices into one index and is used for wage adjustments, pensions and policy.

Steps

  1. Select a base year (CPI in base year is always 100).
  2. Select a basket of commonly consumed goods (rice, fish, transport, rent).
  3. Find the basket cost in each year (step 3 is not on your slides).
  4. Apply the formula.
CPI = [ Sum(P1 x Q0) / Sum(P0 x Q0) ] x 100

P0 = base-year price, P1 = current-year price, Q0 = base-year quantity.

GoodQty (base)Price (base)Price (current)
Rice10 kg5060
Fish5 kg200250
Base basket: (10 x 50) + (5 x 200) = 1,500. Current basket: (10 x 60) + (5 x 250) = 1,850.
CPI = (1,850 / 1,500) x 100 = 123.33. Prices rose 23.33% vs base year.

CPI to inflation rate

Inflation = [(CPI1 - CPI0) / CPI0] x 100

CPI0 = base year, CPI1 = current year. Check: (123.33 - 100) / 100 x 100 = 23.33%.

Unemployment: Definitions

Officially: working-age individuals (typically 15-16+) who are without work, actively seeking employment and available to start. Occurs when someone able and willing to work at the prevailing wage cannot find a job. Some people are unemployed at any moment in all free-market economies.
Employed: paid employees, self-employed, and unpaid family business workers. Also those temporarily absent from a job (vacation, illness, bad weather).
Unemployed: not employed, available for work, and tried to find work in the previous four weeks. Includes those waiting to be recalled after a layoff.
Not in the labor force: neither of the above, e.g. full-time student, homemaker, retiree.
Discouraged worker: wants a job but gave up looking, so counted as not in the labor force.

Formulas

Labor Force = Employed + Unemployed
Unemployment Rate = (Unemployed / Labor Force) x 100
LFPR = (Labor Force / Working-age Population) x 100

LFPR (Labour Force Participation Rate) is the percentage of the working-age population (usually 15+ or 16+) that is in the labor force.

Given: adult population 203.1m, employed 129.6m, unemployed 6.7m.
Labor force = 129.6 + 6.7 = 136.3m
Unemployment rate = 6.7 / 136.3 x 100 = 4.9%
LFPR = 136.3 / 203.1 x 100 = 67.1%

Three Types of Unemployment

Frictional: time taken to match workers and jobs. Workers have different preferences and abilities, job information is imperfect, and geographic mobility is not instant.
Structural: skills do not match available jobs due to structural change. Causes: technological advancement, automation, decline or shutdown of industries, globalization, regional imbalance. Long-term, more serious than frictional, needs retraining. Example: automation replaces garment workers who lack technical skills.
Cyclical: recession or downturn in the business cycle, caused by low aggregate demand (AD). Involuntary; rises in recession, falls in expansion. Causes: recession, falling demand, business losses, layoffs. Example: post-COVID global recession.

Lecture 6: Cyclical Unemployment and the Phillips Curve

Measurement, costs, Bangladesh data and the inflation trade-off

Cyclical Unemployment

Recession: demand falls, production is cut, workers lose jobs. Expansion: demand rises, firms hire, cyclical unemployment falls.

Cyclical Unemployment = Actual Rate - Natural Rate

Natural rate = unemployment when the economy runs normally (frictional + structural).

Can it be negative?

Yes, when Actual < Natural: the economy is above potential output (overheating), usually in a strong boom where firms hire nearly everyone, even those who would normally stay unemployed, and may hire less qualified workers.

Natural 5%, actual 3%: 3% - 5% = -2%. Economy is overheating, which usually shows up as rising wage and price pressure.

Why Unemployment is a Problem

1. Loss of income
No wages, so less spending on food, housing, education, healthcare; living standards fall.
2. Poverty and inequality
Long-term unemployment pushes households into poverty; inequality rises as the employed keep earning.
3. Waste of human resources
Willing workers sit idle, so productive capacity declines.
4. Lower GDP
Output falls below potential, giving slower growth and lower national income.
5. Social and psychological effects
Depression, anxiety, stress, loss of self-confidence, crime, drug abuse, domestic violence, instability.
6. Government burden and skill loss
More spending on benefits and welfare, less tax revenue. Skills decay (human capital depreciation).
Costs to people (1, 2, 5), to the economy (3, 4), to government (6).

Challenges in Measuring Unemployment

The headline rate can understate the problem:

Large informal sector: many workers have no formal contracts or recorded employment.
Underemployment: working fewer hours than wanted, or in jobs below skill level.
Discouraged workers: stopped looking, so excluded from statistics, which understates unemployment.
Hidden (disguised) unemployment: more workers than needed, mostly in agriculture. Example: a family farm where fewer people could produce the same output; marginal productivity is near zero.
Seasonal employment: jobs exist only at certain times of year, so one snapshot is misleading.

The Phillips Curve

Shows the inverse short-run relationship between inflation and unemployment. Low unemployment: inflation tends to rise. High unemployment: inflation tends to fall. Policymakers face a trade-off.
Expansionary policy
Raise G, cut taxes, increase money supply (raises AD). Result: unemployment down, inflation up.
Contractionary policy
Cut G, raise taxes, reduce money supply (lowers AD). Result: unemployment up, inflation down.

Point A (unemployment 5%, inflation 2%): slow growth or moderate demand; firms do not raise wages much, prices stay stable.
Point B (unemployment 3%, inflation 6%): firms compete for workers, wages rise, production costs rise, inflation rises.

Moving A to B: unemployment 5% to 3%, inflation 2% to 6%. Stimulating demand cuts unemployment but adds inflation pressure.

Low unemployment = high inflation. Seesaw: one side down, other side up.
